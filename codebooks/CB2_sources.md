# EU Accession Reform Intelligence Tool | Sources

Version 1.2, 29 October 2026. Made country-neutral, with the language rule generalised, when the pilot was reconfigured before gate G0 (decisions A8 and F2). Version 1.1 followed the architecture review (decisions A4, B2, C1, C2, C4 and C5).

This codebook defines where the tool should look for information, what each source family can legitimately tell us, and how much weight should be placed on that information. The purpose is to give future agents clear boundaries when gathering evidence: different sources serve different analytical purposes. There are five source families.

## 1. Formal EU and accession sources: Used to define the accession requirement

These are official documents produced by the European Commission, Council or other EU institutions as part of the accession process. They include annual country reports, enlargement reports, screening reports, Commission recommendations, opening/interim/closing benchmarks, Council conclusions, Reform Agenda and Growth Plan assessments.

**Use them for:** identifying what the EU expects; extracting formal findings, recommendations and benchmarks; identifying where the EU considers progress insufficient; and tracking how the EU's assessment changes over time.

**Do not use them alone for:** determining why an issue persists, proving that a reform caused an outcome, or deciding which policy solution will work.

**Default guidance:** normally the highest-authority source for determining the accession gap, but not automatically the strongest source for explaining implementation or outcomes.

## 2. National government sources: Used to establish the country response

These are official documents produced by the accession country's government and public institutions, including strategies, action plans, legislation, regulations, national EU-integration programmes, ministry reports, budgets, implementation reports and official statistics. Many of these exist only in the national language or languages; the language rule below applies.

**Use them for:** identifying what government has committed to, designed, adopted or implemented; who is responsible; when action occurred; and what government reports as the result.

**Do not use them alone for:** concluding that implementation has succeeded. Adoption, implementation and outcome must remain separate.

**Default guidance:** strong evidence that an official action, commitment or institutional decision exists; lower confidence for claims about its real-world effectiveness unless corroborated.

## 3. International organisations and independent institutional evidence: Used to test implementation and outcomes

These include the World Bank, OECD, SIGMA, EBRD, Eurostat, Council of Europe, OSCE, relevant UN organisations and other recognised institutional sources.

**Use them for:** testing what happened in practice; identifying implementation gaps, institutional performance, measurable outcomes and trends; and corroborating or challenging government reporting.

**Do not use them automatically as:** an EU accession judgement. Their mandate, indicators and definitions may be different from those used by the Commission.

**Default guidance:** generally strong where the source provides transparent data, methodology or direct institutional assessment; relevance must still be tested against the specific reform issue.

## 4. Think tanks, academic research and specialist analysis: Used to explain why

These include credible think tanks, academic research, independent evaluations and specialist sector analysis.

**Use them for:** understanding why reforms worked, failed or stalled; identifying causal mechanisms, implementation barriers, enabling conditions and lessons from comparable cases.

**Limits:** quality varies significantly. An agent should consider who produced the analysis, the evidence used, the methodology, its date and whether important claims are independently supported.

**Default guidance:** variable. These sources can provide high-value explanations but should not automatically override stronger primary or institutional evidence.

## 5. Civil society and independent monitoring: Used to test implementation in practice

These include watchdog organisations, civil society monitoring coalitions and shadow reports that track how reforms are applied in practice, for example how a public service or an administrative procedure actually works.

**Use them for:** independent monitoring data on implementation; documented cases that test official reporting; and barriers that official sources do not report.

**Limits:** these organisations often advocate particular positions. An agent should separate their monitoring data and documented cases from their policy positions, and record the positions as the organisation's view rather than as a finding of fact.

**Default guidance:** variable. Monitoring data with a transparent method can be strong evidence of implementation; advocacy positions carry the weight of an informed opinion only.

## How evidence should be recorded

For every source, the master source register records at least:

- **Source ID**
- **Source family**
- **Institution/author**
- **Document title and type**
- **Publication date and period covered**
- **Country, role, chapters and themes covered**
- **Language**
- **URL, retrieval date and local file**
- **Availability status and any relevant limitation**

For every piece of evidence extracted from a source, the evidence item records the information extracted, its exact reference (the quote and its page or section), and the dates and actors involved. Source quality, evidence confidence and relevance are recorded as separate judgements, not in the register.

Three judgements should remain separate:

- **Source quality:** how credible and authoritative is the underlying source for this particular topic?
- **Evidence confidence:** how strongly does the available evidence support the particular extracted claim?
- **Relevance:** how applicable is that evidence to the specific country or reform issue being analysed?

A high-quality source can still provide weak evidence for a particular claim, and strong evidence from another country can still have low relevance to the target country.

Each of the three is rated High, Medium or Low with a one-line reason, or recorded as "insufficient evidence" where no fair rating is possible. They are never combined into a single score, and outputs always show them side by side. The criteria are set out in Codebook 6.

## Finding sources

Before collecting documents, the agent drafts a source requirements list: one line for each type of document the analysis expects to exist, for example the Commission's annual report on the target country for each year, or the annual reports of the national body responsible for an in-scope area. The analyst approves this list before acquisition begins. Each source collected is linked to the requirement it satisfies, so any requirement with no source against it is visible as a gap rather than silently missing.

## Language

English-language sources are used wherever they exist. Documents in the target country's official languages are included only where they are core to the analysis and no suitable English version exists; the run record names those languages, and each such document is recorded in the source register. For these, the evidence keeps the original-language quote alongside an English machine translation, clearly flagged as such. Unless a reviewer who reads the source language is named in the run record, evidence that relies on machine translation is capped at Medium confidence and listed in the limits report.

## Overall rule

The source family determines how evidence should be interpreted, not what the agent is allowed to find there. An assessment, intervention, outcome or lesson may appear across several source types. Agents should therefore preserve overlapping and conflicting evidence rather than force one source to provide the answer.
