1. European Commission assessment of the preconditions for Union support  
Effective democratic and rule of law mechanisms  
North Macedonia has a multi-party parliamentary system. According to the Office for Democratic 
Institutions and Human Rights  (ODIHR), the latest parliamentary and presidential elections of 
2024 and local elections of 2025 were competitive and fundamental freedoms were respected, and 
election legislation provides a suitable framework for holding democratic elections. Campaign 
rules, access to the media and distribution of State funding need to be adequately regulated, and 
the Electoral Code merits revision throughout an inclusive process and with consensus across the 
political spectrum to address inconsistencies, gaps, and ambiguities to ensure legal certainty. North 
Macedonia should implement outstanding recommendations from the  Organization for Security 
and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) and ODIHR.   
North Macedonia has undergone several waves of judicial reform and in December 2023 adopted 
a new justice strategy aiming to improve the independence, efficiency, and professionalism of the 
judiciary.  External and political interference remain  a serious concern. The independence of the 
judiciary is marred by insufficient allocations from the state budget. Further efforts are needed to 
increase accountability, transparency, and institutional capacities to prevent undue influence or 
intimidation. The rising deficit in human resources  impacts the quality and efficiency of justice 
and needs to be  addressed. In spring 2025,  97 judges and prosecutors  were elected from 
the eighth generation of graduates from the Academy for Judges and Prosecutors.  Meanwhile the 
admission procedure and initial training for the next generation  is stalled, due to insuff icient 
financial resources  in the 2026 state budget  for the training of the biggest ever intake of 130 
students. The new Law on Judicial Council was adopted in December 2025. A comprehensive 
national anti-corruption strategy is in place, but it is not adequately implemented. A new national 
anti-corruption strategy is currently being drafted.   Significant corruption risks persist across 
various sectors. The institutional framework on the fight against corruption is in place and is 
satisfactory in terms of preventive measures, though the track record in the fight against corruption 
and the law enforcement aspect need to be improved. Amendments to the Criminal Code in 
September 2023 have weakened the legal framework and negatively affected the prosecution of 
corruption, especially in high -level corruption cases. These amendments were challenged by  the 
Constitutional Court  in March 2025, giving  the Parliament six months to review the contested 
provisions, after which  they may be annulled or repealed. The Ministry of Justice formed a 
working group to draft a new Cr iminal Code,  in line with the EU  acquis and European 
standards, but the work has not been completed yet. On 28 January 2026, the Parliament adopted 
amendments to the Criminal Code upon the initiative of individual members, to redress the changes 
made in 2023 and as a response to the Constitutional Court’s review deadline, which had expired 
in the meantime. The President signed the amendments on 29 January  2026. These amendments 
were presented as an interim solution before the adoption of a new Criminal Code.

Respect for all human rights obligations 
The legal framework of North Macedonia on the protection of fundamental rights is partially 
aligned with the EU  acquis and European standards. The country continues to meet its general 
obligations on fundamental r ights, though legislation and pertinent strategies need to be 
implemented in a systematic manner. The country ratified most international human rights 
instruments. It needs to develop a comprehensive monitoring and data collection system to assess 
the leve l of implementation of human rights legislation, policies, and strategies and to 
fully benefit from its observer status in the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights 
(FRA). While overall prison conditions  remain a serious concern, prison infrastructu re has 
been renovated, and several prison facilities have been improved. The Parliament needs to amend 
the Law on Ombudsman’s Office in order to upgrade its ‘B’ accreditation status, based on the Paris 
Principles.  
The preconditions are confirmed as met. 
 
2. European Commission assessment of the general conditions for payments 
a) Assessment of the macro financial stability  
The real sector  in North Macedonia demonstrated robust performance throughout 2025, with 
economic activity accelerating in key quarters. The economy expanded by an estimated 3.5% for 
the full year 2025, with momentum maintained into the fourth quarter at 3.8% year-on-year, 
supported by strong gains in construction (14.6%) and professional and scientific activities (8.1%). 
Final consumption grew nominally by 6.4% in the fourth quarter, underpinning revenue collection 
despite the overall shortfall against plan . Industrial production indices showed positive trends, 
rising to 127.24 (2010=100) , 1.9% year -on-year, by December 2025 , indicating expansion in 
manufacturing and related activities. Unemployment declined to 11.5% in the third quarter, 
supported by job creation amid the growth trajectory. Wage growth also contributed to household 
resilience, with average monthly net wages reaching EUR 746 by November 2025. Overall, the 
sector’s outlook remains positive, with projections for continued steady expansion into 202 6 at 
around 3.2-3.5%. 
The monetary sector  in 2025  focused on balancing inflation control with economic support, 
culminating in policy adjustments by year -end. The policy rate was reduced to 4% in December 
from 5.35% in November, signalling a shift to a mor e accommodative stance amid moderating 
price pressures. Inflation trended upward slightly, with the year -over-year rate at 4.1% in 
December 2025 and an annual average around 3.5-4%, influenced by food prices rising 5.3%. Core 
inflation stood at 4.3%, while the CPI reached 162.50 points by December 2025. Credit expansion 
was strong, with total loans growing 12.98% to reach EUR 9.465 billion, reflecting healthy 
banking sector activity and expected deposit growth of around 9.5%. The introduction of a new 
monetary policy framework in December, shortening the tenor of central bank bills to seven days, 
aimed to enhance operational efficiency. Projections indicate inflation easing to 3% in 2026. 
Fiscal developments in 2025 highlighted challenges in maintaining budgetary discipline, driven 
primarily by a shortfall in revenue collection. For the full year 2025, the central government budget 
recorded a deficit of approximately EUR 679 million (MKD 41,839 million), marginally 
exceeding the planned deficit of EUR 671 million by 1.2%. Total revenues reached only 92.0% of

the annual plan, with non-tax revenues performing particularly poorly at 61.8% of target and excise 
duties at 87.9%. VAT collections were as expected, slightly surpassing the annual plan at 100.4%. 
On the expenditure side, total spending was executed at 93.0% of plan, with capital expenditures

for adherence to fiscal rules. Looking ahead, reforms to broaden the revenue base, improve non -
tax revenue collection, and accelerate capital budget execution will be crucial to stabili sing the 
fiscal position and aligning with EU integration goals. 
North Macedonia’s external sector showed signs of improvement in 2025, though vulnerabilities 
persisted. The current account balance turned positive at EUR 62.3 million in the third quarter, 
reversing a deficit from the prior period and indicating better trade dynamics amid net financial 
inflows. Goods imports rose to EUR 2.6 billion in the third quarter, reflecting strong domestic 
demand, but overall external positions strengthened compared to earlier quarters. However, earlier 
deteriorations in 2024 carried over some risks, with inflation surges potentially impacting 
competitiveness. Looking forward, continued EU reform progress could facilitate better funding 
and trade integration, supporting a more resilient external balance. 
The authorities in North Macedonia appear to be pursuing a stability-oriented economic policy, 
as demonstrated by prudent monetary adjustments to curb inflation while supporting growth, 
efforts to manage fiscal deficits within manageable debt levels, and measures to strengthen the 
external sector through international arrangements and exchange rat e stability. The fixed peg of 
the denar to the euro further underscores this commitment to macroeconomic stability, even amid 
challenges like expenditure pressures and global uncertainties, positioning the economy for 
sustainable development aligned with EU aspirations. 
In conclusion, the authorities are pursuing a stability -oriented macroeconomic policy,  and 
the general condition is met. 
b) Assessment of the soundness of the Public Financial Management system(s) 
The 2025-2028 Public Finance Management Refo rm Programme (PFM/RP) is credible and 
relevant, though further system improvements are still needed. Some reform components continue 
to face delays due to the slow adoption of key legislation and challenges in the implementation of 
critical IT reforms. The PFM Sector Working Group met for the last time in July 2025 to endorse 
the Reform Programme, which was formally adopted by the Government in September 2025. The 
PFM/RP 2025 is structured around seven pillars, addressing the main weaknesses identified in 
these assessments. The Ministry of Finance’s institutional capacity needs to be strengthen, facing 
challenges to attract and retain qualified staff.  
Key weaknesses in the PFM systems are regularly identified by international assessments, 
including Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA), Support for Improvement in 
Governance and Management ( SIGMA), and EU country assessment reports. The most recent 
PEFA assessment was conducted in 2021, based on data from fiscal years 2018, 2019, and 2020. 
The latest SIGMA assessment was published in December 2024.

The budget process is well established and organised. Budget credibility has improved, supported 
by the operationalisation of the Fiscal Council, enhancements to the fiscal strategy and progress in 
monitoring fiscal risks. A new Organic Budget Law (OBL) was adopted by Parliament in 
September 2022. There has been some progress in the adoption of key legislation to implement 
the reforms envisaged in the Law. However, the development of an Inte grated Financial 
Management Information System (IFMIS) is well below scheduled.  
Some progress has been made in Public Investment Management (PIM). A new PIM Decree 
regulating the entire investment management cycle was adopted in April 2025 and the Ministry of 
Finance has delivered training to budget users to ensure its operationalization. Further, the Ministry 
of Finance has established an IT database to monitor the financial implementation of projects.  
Budget execution is well structured. The Government operates a zero -balance Single Treasury 
Account daily. Expenditure controls at commitment level and monitoring of State -Owned 
Enterprises and Municipalities should be strengthened to prevent arrears. 
Public Internal Financial Controls (PIFC) remain weak but there has been some progress after the 
adoption of the PIFC Law in December 2024. The new law is expected to facilitate essential 
reforms, including strengthening administrative capacity, reducing the fragmentation of Internal 
Audit and reinforcing Finan cial Management Controls. The Government approved a new 
methodology to develop risk management registers in September 2025 and there have been 
improvements in Internal Audit Units reporting.  
Public procurement legislation is broadly aligned with the EU acquis. Despite North Macedonia’s 
‘A’ rating in the last PEFA assessment, practical implementation challenges persist, including low 
competition, exposure to corruption, and underdeveloped Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs).  The 
use of the Most Economically  Advantageous Tender (MEAT) criteria remains rare, with 
approximately 96% of tenders awarded based solely on the lowest price. 
The State Audit Office (SAO) is considered operationally independent, but its official status as an 
independent institution has y et to be recognised in the Constitution. Parliamentary oversight of 
audit reports remains limited. 
In conclusion, the  soundness of the public finance management  system (including 
the relevant reform strategy) is sufficiently confirmed, and the general condition is met.  
c) Assessment of the transparency and oversight of the budget  
The Executive’s budget proposal and the final approved budget are published in a timely manner 
on the websites of both the Parliament and the Ministry of Finance. The main budget documents, 
including the annual budget, the Fiscal Strategy, the Debt Management Strategy, and the Final 
Accounts, are generally available to the public. However, the publication of recent Fiscal 
Strategies has faced delays in publication.   
The Ministry of Finance has continued its efforts to enhance budget transparency by publishing 
quarterly data on the revenue and expenditure of public institutions, local government authorities, 
and state-owned enterprises. However, the comprehensiveness and accessibility of this data could 
be improved. 
In the 2023 Open Budget Survey (OBS), North Macedonia scored 35, a slight decline from its 
score of 36 in 2021. The country makes five out of the eight key budget documents mon itored by

According to the latest OBS, there is only limited oversight throughout the budget cycle. The 
government should ensure that the draft budget is submitted to Parliament at least two months

authority may use the e -auction in up to 40% of the number of

reports of the Public Procurement Bureau. It is expected that t his

cancellation and will encourage bidders to submit financial 
proposals more aligned with market prices.  
Following the adoption of the amendment, the Public Procurement 
Bureau submitted  a circular for guidance using the Electronic 
System for Public Procurement and provided training to the 
contracting authorities to ensure appropriate implementation of 
the Law.

Electronic Communications aim to align national legislation with

Consequently, the adoption of bylaws, the establishment of 
institutional capacities and any additional implementation

focuses exclusively on the alignment of primary legislation. 
Compliance with secondary legislation will be assessed under the 
conditionality of subsequent steps within the reform area. 
Prior to law adoption, draft amendments addressing the feedback 
from the Commission were shared by the national authorities for 
further assessment in November 2025. The Commission’s review 
of the legislation concerned both the draft amendments and final 
adopted legislation, noting that alignment has been partially 
achieved. 
On the GIA Regulation and the Law on Construction adopted in 
December 2025, the  Commission considers that full alignment 
with the GIA has not been achieved yet, as some harmonisation 
gaps remain regarding key provisions of GIA. The Law on 
Construction demonstrates a clear intention to align with Articles 
7, 8 and 9 of the GIA Regulation. 
In addition, the Law on Electronic Communications continues to 
include provisions on the financing of the Operational and 
Technical Agency (OTA), even though the European Commission 
raised concerns in this regard. North Macedonia considers that the 
financing provisions, as stipulated in the Law, do not contradict 
Article 16 of the EECC by further emphasising its regulatory and 
supervisory functions as an “other competent authority” to which 
the funds are intended exclusively. In principle, the Commission 
accepts that certain activities of OTA may be funded by 
administrative charges imposed on individual undertakings ,

The New Decree on the Methodology for Determining the 
Criteria for the Distribution of the Block Grant for Secondary 
Education to Municipalities and the City of Skopje was 
adopted by the Government on 29 July 2025  (Official Gazette 
No. 159), establishing the legal basis for a new funding formula 
for secondary education and vo cational education and training 
(VET). Two technical documents informed the drafting process: 
(i) Recommendations for adjusting the proposal formula for

the secondary education with focus on the vocational secondary 
education and introducing finance -based performance. Both 
documents were developed with support from Helvetas and the 
adopted Decree reflects these inputs and recommendations.  
The new funding formula includes basic, variable, adjustment and 
development components, each tailored to factors like student 
enrolment, school type, specific needs (e.g. special education and 
language instruction), and school performance. It aims to improve 
resource allocation, ensuring fair, transparent, and efficient funds 
distribution to municipalities, according to the following 
principles: 
• Cost-based allocation, whereby funding is calculated per 
student, with emphasise on the real costs for education. 
• Smaller municipalities and vulnerable groups, including 
children with disabilities, are prioritised to reduce 
educational disparities and promote equity. 
• Performance-based incentives, whereby schools receive 
additional funding based on measurable improvements. 
• Transparency in funding, as the formula clarifies how 
funds are allocated. 
• Municipalities and schools gain greater autonomy in 
managing education budgets, allowing for locally tailored 
decisions and supporting fiscal decentralisation.  
• Sector-specific differentiation, where funding varies by 
vocational sector to address the unique costs of specialised

scope of work. 
Accordingly, on 24 -26 November 2025, the Commission for 
Protection of Competition organized a two-day training for judges 
on the CPC procedures and on the EU acquis. The training took 
place at the Academy for Judges and Public Prosecutors. At least 
6 judges, mainly from the basic and administrative courts, 
participated in the training, meeting the target set in the Reform 
Agenda. Additional court staff and representatives from the 
Ministry of Justice and the CPC also attended. 
The module was designed to provide extensive training on all 
aspects of state aid and its treatment from both the national and 
the EU legislation level. In the introductory part, the trainees were 
given insights from a state a id case handler at the European 
Commission and an EU law university professor, discussing how 
companies benefit from state aid and public resources. In a second 
panel, a World Bank economist addressed whether measures taken 
by public companies qualify as state aid and if all interventions by 
public authorities in the economy confer an advantage.      
The working segment of the training was divided in three parts:  
(1) Introduction and definition of the concept of state aid and 
criteria for determining whether a measure constitutes aid.  
(2) Procedures in state aid cases according to the domestic 
Law on State Aid Control and presentation of typical cases 
(individual aid and state aid scheme).

hypothetical state aid procedure trying to resolve it based on what

State aid training is ex pected to become a regular component of 
the Academy for Judges and Prosecutors curriculum. As shown in 
the Academy’s 2026 training catalogue, the module is scheduled

(Official Gazette No. 262/2025 ) and it includes provisions and

the law aims to strengthen judicial protection by clearly 
guaranteeing the right to appeal and introducing a two-tier judicial

procedural safeguards, including defined deadlines, reasoned 
decisions, and accountability mechanisms, addressing previous 
shortcomings regarding legal protection and appeal deadlines. It 
would allow confidential proceeding and closed Council sessions

hearings take place upon the judge’s request. The Commission 
concludes this sub-requirement is addressed.
