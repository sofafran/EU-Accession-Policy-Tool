A STRONGER EUROPE THROUGH ENLARGEMENT 
 
INTRODUCTION 
In a geopolitical environment marked by uncertainty, strategic competition, economic 
fragmentation and war, the European Union remains an anchor of peace, security, prosperity 
and democratic stability. 
Enlargement has been one of the European Union’s most consequential policies for promoting 
stability, democratic transformation and economic integration across the continent. Today, 
its strategic importance has grown further. A credible enlargement policy is an investment in 
Europe’s collective security, competitiveness and resilience, strengthening the Union’s 
capacity to protect its interests, reduce vulnerabilities and create opportunities for citizens 
and businesses. 
Enlargement is  already contributing to these objectives. Through reforms, investment and 
gradual integration, enlargement countries are strengthening their democratic institutions, 
becoming more closely integrated into the European economy and contributing increasingly 
to Europe’s security and resilience. At the same time, closer integration with the Union brings 
tangible benefits before accession, including through greater economic opportunities, 
improved connectivity, opportunities for work, study and research and increased participation 
in selected areas of the single market. 
Enlargement countries need to prepare for the rights and obligations of membership, while 
the EU needs to prepare for a larger Union capable of acting effectively and delivering on its 
priorities. To prepare for enlargement, the European Commission adopted a Communication1 
on how the Union can anticipate enlargement in its policies, institutions, governance and 
financial framework, adapting where necessary as the accession process advances. This 
includes ensuring that future accessions are accompanied by effective and propo rtionate 
safeguards to protect the functioning of the Union and ensure that commitments made in the 
accession process continue to be respected after accession. 
The reporting period showed that sustained reform efforts lead to concrete progress to EU 
membership as evidenced in particular by the four frontrunners. Montenegro ’s remarkable 
progress in EU accession negotiations demonstrates that enlargement remains a reality 
within reach. With all 33 negotiating chapters opened and 21 provisionally closed, Montenegro 
has made significant strides in advancing its membership preparations and, in the 
Commission’s opinion, has met the closing benchmarks in all chapters. The country has made 
decisive progress on the fundamentals by achieving good results in areas such as the rule of 
law, economic governance, statistics and public administration reform. The work on 
Montenegro’s accession treaty and the adoption of Council Conclusions 2 on the financial 
package for the accession negotiations with Montenegro confirm the country as the 
frontrunner in the EU’s enlargement process.  
Albania also made significant progress in its accession negotiations. It opened all accession 
negotiation clusters in just over a year, met the interim benchmarks on cluster 1 and 
provisionally closed the first four chapters. Albania needs to maintain the pace of  reforms, 
ensuring speed and quality, to fulfil closing benchmarks in all chapters of negotiations. 
The Republic of Moldova ’s (‘Moldova’) consistent implementation of reforms and its strong 
commitment to align with the EU acquis  despite unprecedented external pressures

The credibility of the enlargement process depends on reforms being put into practice and 
maintained over time. Progress towards membership therefore continues to be determined 
by objective criteria and each country’s own merits, with the fundamentals setting the overall 
pace of accession negotiations. 
Gradual integration brings the Union and enlargement countries closer together before 
accession. Access to particular policies or parts of the single market depends on alignment 
with the EU rules, the capacity to implement and enforce them, and respect for  the principles 
of the accession process. Gradual integration complements and reinforces, but does not 
replace, the accession process. The EU single market is the world’s second largest economy. 
The opportunity to expand it through gradual integration (fol lowed by accession) means 
expanding a highly successful economic model. At a time of increasing global competition, 
enlargement strengthens Europe’s economic foundations and supports sustainable 
prosperity for all Europeans. Alignment with the EU Common Fo reign and Security Policy 
(CFSP) and stepped -up cooperation on CFSP and the common security and defence policy 
(CSDP) is part of gradual integration logic and highlights the enlargement countries’ 
contribution to Europe’s security. 
I. PROTECTING EUROPE – DEMOCRACY, SECURITY AND RESILIENCE 
A stronger Europe starts with a safer and more resilient Europe. Enlargement strengthens the 
foundations of European security by extending the area in which democracy, the rule of law, 
fundamental rights and accountable institutions are firmly anchored. Strong democratic 
institutions and effective rule of law systems are not only core requirements for EU 
membership, they are also essential for the capacity of states and societies to withstand 
internal and external pressures. 
At the same time, Europe’s security environment has fundamentally changed. Russia’s war of 
aggression against Ukraine and recent military escalation in the Black Sea, hybrid and

Developments in enlargement countries can have direct consequences for the EU’s security,

negotiations. Strengthening these foundations is indispensable for building democratic and 
societal resilience. 
1. Democracy, rule of law and fundamental rights 
Progress on the fundamentals remains essential  for every enlargement country. Credible 
reforms require not only legislative alignment but also effective implementation, independent 
institutions, a strong public administration and a sustained track record of results. 
During the reporting period, substantial progress was recorded in several enlargement 
countries, particularly where accession -related reforms benefited from sustained political 
commitment and broad institutional cooperation. Montenegro and Albania continue d to 
advance reforms linked to the fundamentals, with  Montenegro notably meeting the closing 
benchmarks for the rule of law chapters (as assessed by the Commission) and Albania 
meeting the interim benchmarks under cluster 1. Moldova and Ukraine pursued major reforms 
despite significant external pressures and, in Ukraine’s case, wartime conditions. At the same 
time, progress remained uneven across the enlargement countries. Serious concerns persist 
in several countries. Democratic standards, fundamental rights and freedoms and the rule of 
law deteriorated further in Georgia and Türkiye. 
The annual rule of law report involves Albania, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Serbia in 
the same preventive cycle as Member States based on objective and merit-based criteria. The 
exercise is complementary to the enlargement reports. It is also an element of gradual 
integration, and it supports reform efforts aiming to ﬁrmly and irreversibly anchor democracy 
and the rule of law in enlargement countries. Building on the participation of four enlargement 
countries, the Commission will progressively include further candidates as and when they 
become ready. 
Democratic institutions and civil society 
Stable and effective democratic institutions are central to the accession process. Democratic 
systems require effective checks and balances, strong parliamentary oversight, transparent 
and inclusive political dialogue and electoral processes in which citizens can exercise their 
choices freely.

Overall, electoral processes were peaceful, competitive and professionally conducted.  It is 
important for all enlargement countries to ensure alignment with relevant EU acquis  in this 
area, taking into account European and international democratic standards. To address 
longstanding electoral integrity concerns, Bosnia and Herzegovina’s October 2026 general 
elections introduced countrywide use of new election information and com munication 
technologies. At the 25 October elections, which took place in an atmosphere of profound 
polarisation, the Serbian voters have given the next government a strong mandate for reforms. 
The October 2025 municipal elections in Georgia were held under deeply compromised 
political and electoral conditions including intimidation  and pressure on independent media 
and civil society. 
Cross-institutional cooperation facilitated the adoption and implementation of EU -related 
reforms in Montenegro, Ukraine and Moldova. In Albania, the Constitutional Court’s active role 
in upholding institutional safeguards contributed to conducive conditio ns for reform. 
However, political polarisation, weaknesses in parliamentary oversight and excessive use of 
accelerated legislative procedures continue to affect the quality of democratic governance in 
some of the enlargement countries. Respect for both constitutional arrangements and 
implementation of decisions of constitutional courts remains essential. In Bosnia and 
Herzegovina and Kosovo, the functioning of the executive and legislature was hindered by 
political fragmentation. The dismantling of institutional safeguards continued in Georgia and 
Türkiye. Despite some progress in some countries, women remain under- represented in 
political leadership positions and continue to face gender -specific barriers to equal 
participation in political and public life  
EU accession is most transformative where it is supported by broad political and societal 
ownership. Governments have primary responsibility for implementing reforms, but 
opposition parties, parliaments, local authorities, civil society, social partners, independent 
media and citizens all have an important role to play. Enlargement therefore requires a whole-
of-society approach and sustained public engagement on the opportunities and 
responsibilities associated with EU membership. Young people have a vital role to play in 
shaping their countries’ European paths. This is also being supported by enhanced 
communication both in the enlargement countries and in the EU Member States.   
An open, enabling and inclusive environment for civil society  is an integral part of a 
functioning democracy. While the required legal and policy frameworks are broadly in place 
in most enlargement countries, their implementation remains uneven. Progress on some 
matters in Montenegro, Moldova, and Ukraine is not matched in other enlargement countries. 
Public consultations take place in most countries, but the mechanisms behind them have 
limited effectiveness. Civil society organisations and independent actors continue to face 
smear campaigns, strategic lawsuits and administrative obstacles in some countries.  
In three enlargement countries, mounting challenges have been affecting civic space, 
effectively limiting civil society’s ability to operate freely and safely. In Serbia, c ivil society 
organisations have been subject to increasing pressure, attacks and intimidation . In Georgia, 
civil society operates in a repressive environment and is subject to restrictive legislation, 
deepening the chilling effect. In Türkiye, ongoing legal and administrative pressure from 
authorities and the disproportionately harsh implementatio n of national security laws stifles 
and shrinks civic space.

Although the strategic frameworks are broadly in place across all enlargement countries,

comprehensive ﬁscal risks monitoring. Public internal ﬁnancial control systems require more 
effective internal audit functions and improved risk management. 
Judiciary and the ﬁght against corruption 
Independent, impartial and effective judicial systems are indispensable for the rule of law, the 
protection of fundamental rights and a functioning market economy. They are essential for 
societal trust as well as for foreign investor confidence. 
Several enlargement countries continued reforms aiming to strengthen judicial independence 
and accountability, including through vetting, legislative reforms and more transparent and 
merit-based appointment procedures. Montenegro, Moldova, and Albania continued reforms 
aiming to strengthen the functioning and accountability of their judicial systems. Montenegro 
adopted constitutional amendments, strengthening the independence of the Judicial and 
Prosecutorial Councils, fully in line with EU and Venice Commission recommendations. 
Albania completed the comprehensive vetting of judges and prosecutors.   Ukraine advanced 
on the vetting and appointment of vetted candidates at high judiciary bodies and 
Constitutional Court levels. Judicial independence remains seriously weakened, especially in 
Türkiye and Georgia. In Serbia, the initial backsliding caused by legal amendments that 
removed some of the existing safeguards to protect prosecutorial autonomy and judicial 
independence was subsequently partly addressed. 
However, political and other undue influence over judicial institutions is a concern in several 
countries. North Macedonia and Kosovo struggle with political or vested-interest interference 
with and influence over courts and prosecution services. Serbia is  similarly affected, where 
undue pressure has significantly increased. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, the judiciary’s 
independence and impartiality need to be improved, and legislative reforms and integrity 
checks are key to rebuilding public trust in the justice system. The most severe situations are 
in Georgia and Türkiye. 
Structural weaknesses, including unfilled vacancies, backlogs, lengthy proceedings and 
insufficient administrative capacity, continue to undermine the effectiveness of justice 
systems. Access to legal aid also remains limited. Legislative reforms therefore  need to be 
accompanied by adequate resources, merit-based selection procedures, training, institutional 
capacity and a sustained track record of independent decision- making. This requires high 
political attention and leadership.

Corruption remains a serious challenge across the enlargement region and must be 
addressed as a matter of priority. A credible fight against corruption requires transparency, 
effective public procurement systems, independent media and an enabling environment for 
investigative journalism and civil society. The mainstreaming of anti- corruption policies and 
measures across the most vulnerable sectors remains an important priority. Several countries 
strengthened their legal and institutional frameworks and continued to develop track records 
on investigations and prosecutions. Montenegro, Ukraine, Moldova and Albania registered 
progress. In Montenegro, prosecutorial and law enforcement institutions’ effectiveness and 
coordination in the area of corruption continued to improve and the process to adopt two sets 
of amendments to the Constitution is ongoing . In Ukraine, anticorruption institutions 
continued to build up the track record on investigations, prosecutions and convictions in high-
level anti -corruption cases, while certain priority legislative reforms to strengthen the fight 
against corruption remai n outstanding. The risk of state capture risks remains salient, 
especially in Georgia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia and Türkiye.  
Fundamental rights and freedom of expression 
Legal frameworks for the protection of  fundamental rights are generally in place across the 
enlargement countries, but implementation and enforcement remain uneven. The situation 
continued to deteriorate in Türkiye and Georgia. Across the region, the enforcement of human 
rights, including of European Court of Human Rights judgments and recommendations of 
international and European monitoring bodies needs to be improved , particularly in Türkiye 
and Georgia. National human rights institutions need more resources and greater 
independence. Improvements in child protection, gender equality and the rights of persons 
with disabilities were noted in Albania, Montenegro, Moldova  and Ukraine. The rights of 
persons belonging to minorities continued to be generally upheld everywhere and were 
strengthened in Ukraine. 
Montenegro and Moldova took steps to tackle ill -treatment of prisoners and made efforts to 
improve detention and prison conditions, but continued efforts are needed. In North 
Macedonia, and Türkiye, reform efforts have yet to lead to tangible results. Most  countries 
need to respond more decisively to cases of discrimination and hate offences, in particular 
those targeting Roma, LGBTIQ+ persons, persons with disabilities, and minorities. This 
requires effective prevention, investigation, prosecution and access to remedies, alongside 
measures addressing the structural conditions that enable discrimination. Implementation of 
measures to improve gender equality is uneven. The continued incidence of gender- based 
violence requires increased resources for the prevention and for accessible, specialised 
support services for victims. The increase in anti -rights and anti -gender narratives is a 
challenge in several countries. In Türkiye, the recent wave of arrests and legal proceedings 
targeting LGBTIQ+ persons, activist s, human rights associations and businesses has led to 
further stigmatisation and curtailed the exercise of their rights. 
Efforts are still ongoing to align legislation and practice with the EU acquis and European 
standards on victims’ rights and procedural safeguards for suspects and accused persons. 
Montenegro is a good example of considerable progress in alignment in this area. 
Freedom of expression , media freedom and media pluralism remain essential indicators of 
democratic resilience. Some positive developments were recorded in certain enlargement 
countries, but intimidation, threats and attacks against journalists, including strategic lawsuits 
against public participation, political influence over media, concentration of media ownership 
and weaknesses in the independence of regulatory authorities remain of concern in others. 
Montenegro has continued to progress in this area, further aligning with EU standards and 
implementing a zero -tolerance policy to intimidation and violence against journalists. 
Moldova adopted a new media law on transparency, including in relation to media ownership,

2. Internal security, borders and migration 
Effective cooperation on internal security protects citizens in both the Union and enlargement 
countries. Organised crime, traﬃcking in human beings, drug traﬃcking, migrant smuggling, 
money laundering, violent extremism, radicalisation, terrorism and cybercrime are inherently 
cross-border cha llenges and require coordinated responses and effective cooperation. In 
today’s security environment, the ability and commitment of enlargement countries to address 
internal security threats ranging from hybrid attacks to organised crime and terrorism, is 
essential. 
Efforts to strengthen the ﬁght against serious and organised crime  delivered results. 
Montenegro and Albania’s specialised prosecution oﬃces achieved results in investigating 
and prosecuting high- risk criminal networks. Ukraine, Moldova and Bosnia and Herzegovina 
each issued a new national Serious and Organised Crime T hreat Assessment. Albania 
adopted new strategic frameworks to ﬁght organised crime. Shortages in human resources 
and equipment remain an issue across enlargement countries, and integrity measures across 
all law enforcement agencies need to be strengthened. 
Cooperation among enlargement countries, EU Member States and EU agencies continued to 
deepen. Several partners strengthened their specialised law enforcement and prosecution 
capacities and intensified their operational cooperation , including through EMPACT (the 
European Multidisciplinary Platform Against Criminal Threats). These efforts have 
contributed to the investigation and prosecution of organised criminal networks. 
Further progress is nevertheless needed to consolidate track records of investigations, 
prosecutions, convictions and conﬁscation of criminal assets. Some enlargement countries 
have regularly used ﬁnancial investigations and asset recovery, but a systema tic follow-the-
money approach should become standard practice. Capacity to investigate cybercrime, 
cryptocurrency-related crime and complex ﬁnancial crime also needs to be strengthened 
further. Montenegro, Albania, Türkiye and Moldova have taken steps to strengthen their anti -
money laundering and countering the ﬁnancing of  terrorism framework. Although money 
laundering is increasingly recognised as a stand-alone offence, prosecutions and convictions 
in such cases remain scarce. Serbia, Türkiye and Bosnia and Herzegovina have been recently 
assessed by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) and Moneyval. These countries should 
prioritise implementing the Key Recommended Actions –  or, in Bosnia and Herzegovina’s 
case, fully commit to its FATF action plan to address the identiﬁed strategic deﬁciencies that 
have led to its inclusion in the FATF list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring. 
Drug traﬃcking remains the most common activity of organised criminal groups operating 
across enlargement countries. Drugs seizures have tended to increase, and it is essential that 
all enlargement countries ensure a timely destruction of any conﬁscated drugs. Albania,  
Montenegro and Moldova made further progress in aligning their legislation with the EU acquis 
on drugs, while additional efforts are needed in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kosovo and North

Security and resilience 
Foreign information manipulation and interference, cyberattacks and other hybrid threats are 
increasingly targeting democratic institutions, elections, public trust and the accession 
process itself. Strengthening resilience against these threats is therefo re both an accession 
priority and a shared European security interest. 
Preparedness remains uneven across the enlargement region. Moldova and Ukraine have 
developed increasingly comprehensive approaches to fighting foreign interference, strategic 
communication, cybersecurity and electoral resilience in response to sustained h ostile 
activity. EU CSDP missions in Ukraine and in Moldova are supporting capacity building and 
providing advisory support to sustain those efforts. Other enlargement countries, such as 
Montenegro, have also strengthened institutional arrangements and cooperation with the EU. 
Election integrity requires permanent preparedness rather than temporary measures limited 
to electoral periods. Independent institutions, pluralistic media, an informed public and an 
active civil society are central to this effort. Enlargement countries should therefore continue 
strengthening their capacity to detect, analyse and respond to foreign interference and 
disinformation while safeguarding freedom of expression and other fundamental rights. 
The European Democracy Shield and the European Centre for Democratic Resilience provide 
additional opportunities to strengthen cooperation among Member States and enlargement 
countries. On 13 October 2026, Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kosovo, Moldova, 
Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia and Ukraine were associated with the European Centre 
for Democratic Resilience. Participation should support exchanges of expertise, operational 
cooperation and capacity -building, while being tied to clear commitments to democratic 
principles and resilience.  
Cybersecurity, the protection of critical infrastructure, civil protection and preparedness are 
equally important. European energy, transport, digital and communications networks are 
increasingly interconnected, meaning vulnerabilities outside the Union can have direct cross -
border consequences. Gradual alignment with EU standards, preparedness capacities and 
stronger crisis management across both natural and human -induced risks as well as closer 
operational cooperation can reduce these vulnerabilities and strengthen the resilience of 
shared networks and reinforce societal resilience. 
3. Foreign, security and defence policy 
Alignment with the EU’s common foreign and security policy  (CFSP) is a strong signal of a 
candidate country’s strategic orientation.  Full CFSP alignment, including implementation of 
EU sanctions is a key requirement for membership. This includes taking measures to prevent 
circumvention of EU sanctions. A number of enlargement countries benefit from capacity -
building under the European Peace Facility. 
Most enlargement countries maintained a full or high alignment rate with the CFSP during the 
reporting period. Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro and North Macedonia 
continued to be fully aligned. Kosovo’s full alignment with the CFSP is reflected  in the 
dedicated Chapter 31 of the annual report for the first time. Ukraine and Moldova maintained 
very high levels of alignment. By contrast, Serbia’s alignment remained low, while Georgia’s 
alignment declined further. While Türkiye increased the number of consultations and its

cooperation with the EU on a number of regional and international security issues, it continued 
to maintain a very low alignment rate. 
Beyond CFSP alignment, enlargement countries are increasingly contributing to the EU’s 
common security and defence policy  (CSDP). They participate in EU crisis -management 
missions and operations, contribute to European security and benefit from closer cooperation 
through a range of instruments. Security and Defence Partnerships with several enlargement 
countries provide structural fr ameworks for enhancing security and defence cooperation 
across a wide range of areas. 
Ukraine’s contribution is of particular significance. Cooperation between the EU and Ukraine 
on security and defence has deepened substantially 8 since Russia’s full- scale invasion. The 
gradual and future full integration of Ukraine’s defence industrial capacity into the EU defence 
technological base can contribute to strengthening both Ukraine’s defence and Europe’s long-
term defence readiness and  preparedness, industrial capacity and technological innovation. 
Ukraine is increasingly associated to EU instruments and programmes enabling practical 
cooperation and joint projects, such as Security Action for Europe, the European Defence 
Fund, BraveTech EU and European Defence Industry Programme, and participates in European 
Defence Projects of Common Interest. The EU will continue to support Ukraine military needs 
through the defence window of the Ukraine Support Loan.   
These developments show that enlargement countries are already contributing to European 
security. At the same time, deeper security and defence cooperation must remain anchored 
in and be aligned with the Union’s values, foreign policy objectives and access ion 
requirements. 
Upholding international law, including the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court, is 
central to the Union’s values. 
II. GROWING EUROPE – COMPETITIVENESS, INVESTMENT AND OPPORTUNITY
Europe’s prosperity depends on its ability to compete, innovate and create opportunities in a 
rapidly changing global economy. Enlargement contributes to Europe’s economic strength by 
gradually creating a larger, more integrated and more dynamic economic space, increasing 
EU’s weight on a global stage. Economic reforms, investment and gradual integration are 
bringing enlargement countries closer to the EU single market, European value chains and 
investment flows.  
Evidence from previous rounds of enlargement underlines the scale of the opportunity: an 
International Monetary Fund (IMF)9 analysis revealed that GDP per capita in the countries that 
joined the EU in the 2000s was 34% higher after 10 years, and 47% higher after 15 years than 
on a modelled path without accession. The analysis also points to gains for existing Member 
States, wh ile noting that its model covers them less comprehensively. These estimates 
illustrate the combined effects of reform, improved governance, market integration and 
investment, but they are not a forecast of the outcome for every enlargement country. 
The economic potential is significant, but convergence remains incomplete. The IMF 10 
estimates the Western Balkans and Moldova could achieve GDP -per-capita gains of around 
30-35% over 10 years through reforms, deeper market integration and EU investment. The
overall macroeconomic picture is mixed . In 2025, economic growth in many enlargement
countries was weaker than expected. In the Western Balkans, annual growth slowed to 2.6%,
down from 3.6% in 2024, mainly because of a sharp slowdown in Serbia, the region’s largest
economy. North Macedonia was the only Western Balkan economy where growth accelerated.

5. Investing in the green and digital transitions
Successful enlargement requires economies that can integrate in a climate- neutral and

A common framework for gradual integration 
The ‘Preparing for a wider Union’ Communication 18 expands and deepens the concept of 
gradual integration as an integral part of efforts to boost growth, competitiveness and 
security, and an essential part of the accession process. 
In recent years, the EU’s gradual integration offer has been developed through a bottom -up 
approach. This was based on greater access to: (i) EU programmes; (ii) greater support from 
and access to EU agencies; (iii) implementation of single market integrat ion and ad hoc  
measures; (iv) development of connectivity into EU transport, energy and digital networks; and 
(v) access to expert groups and committees. Signiﬁcant progress has been made in recent 
years in all these strands. However, implementation of some measures remains uneven and 
fragmented across enlargement partners and among the different components of gradual 
integration. Furthermore, the work has not systematically focused on or being clearly linked 
to progress in accession preparations.
Therefore, the Commission is now putting forward a more coherent and structured approach 
to gradual integration with follow-up on the ﬁve strands of work, building on the work done so 
far. The offer will be made to every partner in comparably advanced accession situation. The 
mapping in the annex to this Communication shows how each part of this work will be directly 
anchored in – and related to – the dedicated enlargement chapter. 
Gradual integration should expand in a structured manner where it brings clear beneﬁts for 
enlargement partners and the EU, supports accession preparations and serves shared 
strategic priorities. Further opportunities exist in the areas of digital identity, transport, public 
procurement, industrial goods, energy, and emerging technologies, where appropriate. The 
scope and timing of participation and the associated support from the EU budget should 
reﬂect each country’s progress and capacity. Access must remain conditional and reversible 
based on the country’s alignment with the relevant acquis, effective implementation, 
enforcement, and respect for the principles underpinning the accession process. Enlargement 
countries will only be able to beneﬁt from gradual integration in individual sectors if they meet 
the relevant cross -cutting conditions . Gradual integration does not confer the rights of EU 
membership or change the merit-based accession process. 
At the same time, closer economic integration in a growing number of sectors and policies

III. BUILDING TRUST – GOOD NEIGHBOURL Y RELATIONS AND REGIONAL COOPERATION
Lasting stability depends on trust, cooperation and reconciliation. The enlargement process 
has long been a catalyst for regional cooperation and remains one of the EU’s most effective 
instruments for promoting stability across the continent. By encouraging dialogue, supporting 
reconciliation and fostering practical cooperation, enlargement can help overcome historical 
divisions and build the foundations for a shared European future. 
Good neighbourly relations and regional cooperation are an essential part of the Stabilisation 
and Association process and the accession process. Unresolved bilateral disputes, tensions 
between neighbours, and questions stemming from past conflicts can undermine regional 
stability and impede progress on membership. Enlargement countries are therefore expected 
to address outstanding issues constructively and in good faith, in line with international law 
and established agreements. 
At the same time, bilateral issues  should be addressed in a manner that supports the 
accession process. Sustained political dialogue, implementation of existing agreements and 
a commitment to good neighbourly relations are essential for building the trust required for a 
larger and more united Union. 
Western Balkans: reconciliation and good neighbourly relations 
In the Western Balkans, overcoming the legacy of the conflicts of the 1990s remains a major 
part of European integration. Stronger efforts are needed to address outstanding bilateral 
issues, including border questions, missing persons, accountability for war crimes and 
succession issues arising from the dissolution of the former Yugoslavia. Reconciliation must 
be based on facts and respect for victims, recognition of their rights and needs, and their 
meaningful participation in transitional justice processes. 
Political leaders have a particular responsibility to contribute to reconciliation and avoid 
rhetoric or action that deepen divisions. Historical revisionism, denial or glorification of war 
crimes and inflammatory rhetoric are incompatible with the values underpinning the 
accession process and undermine trust between societies.

part of a country’s ability to assume the responsibilities of EU membership. 
IV. DELIVERING ENLARGEMENT – PROGRESS, REFORM AND RESULTS 
The enlargement process is delivering. Since October 2025, 16 intergovernmental 
conferences took place. Montenegro closed 14 chapters reaching a total of 21 overall. Albania 
saw all its chapters opened and the first four closed after meeting the interim benchmarks for 
rule of law requirements in June. Ukraine and Moldova opened four clusters each, building up 
on thorough preparatory work ahead of the formal launch of negotiations in June. 
The reporting period demonstrated that countries that sustain reforms and meet the required 
conditions can advance towards membership, but progress remained uneven in others. 
Several enlargement countries reached significant milestones, while others progressed more 
slowly or stalled. In some cases, countries moved further away from the requirements of EU 
membership. 
These differences reflect the merit -based nature of the accession process. Each country 
advances according to its own progress in meeting the set criteria. The fundamentals remain 
central and determine the overall pace of accession negotiations. There are no shortcuts to 
membership, but sustained reforms produce tangible results. 
The renewed momentum behind enlargement creates an opportunity for countries that are 
ready to advance. Seizing this opportunity depends primarily on their capacity and political 
determination to implement reforms, meet outstanding requirements and build t he broad 
societal support required for EU membership. 
Preparing the Union for enlargement 
Successful enlargement requires preparedness on both sides. Readiness must be built 
throughout the process. Candidate countries must be able to apply the EU acquis, participate 
effectively in the Union’s institutions and assume the rights and obligations of membership 
from the moment of accession. At the same time, the Union must remain capable of taking 
decisions, financing its policies and delivering on its priori ties with a larger membership. 
Progress on one side should inform practical preparations on the other. 
Work on pre -enlargement policy reviews continued during the reporting period. The reviews 
show that most Union policies can accommodate a larger membership, with targeted 
adaptations where needed. Enlargement preparedness should become an integral part of 
Union policymaking throughout the accession process. This means: (i) anticipating 
enlargement across policies, legislation and financial instruments; (ii) making more 
systematic use of gradual integration to support reform and convergence; and (iii) ensuri ng 
that the Union’s governance and financial framework evolve as negotiations advance. This 
approach will support a merit-based accession process.

The Union’s capacity to act is equally important. Work with Member States and the other 
institutions on decision -making and institutional arrangements needs to advance while 
negotiations are under way. As outlined in the Communication ‘Preparing for a Wider Union20’, 
future accession treaties should also contain effective and proportionate safeguards to 
ensure that key commitments made during negotiations continue to be respected after 
membership. These preparations give practical assurance that enlargement will str engthen 
the Union’s ability to act and protect its interests. 
The countries most advanced in the process have declared target dates for closing accession 
negotiations. This demonstrates their ambition, and the Commission is committed to 
supporting them in meeting EU membership requirements, as part of an accession process 
based on the own-merits principle. Roadmaps for the most advanced negotiating countries – 
Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine – are presented in annex to this Communication. 
The roadmaps will give both the candidate countries and the Council a clearer framework for 
the next steps, thus helping to organise the next stages of preparation in a more structured 
and transparent manner. This reflects the ambition of the revised Enlargement methodology 
to make the process more predictable and manageable by setting out clearer priorities, 
sequencing ref orms and negotiating steps in a more orderly way, while also improving 
monitoring of progress. The Commission will update the roadmaps as progress and 
challenges evolve, and d epending on the candidates’ progress, the Commission can in time 
draw them up for additional negotiating countries. 
Until the closure of negotiations with Montenegro, which could be achieved in December 
2026, the priority will be to agree on the ﬁnancial package, on the institutional arrangements, 
including the necessary safeguards, and on closing all remaining chapters, for which the 
closing benchmarks have been met. Following the closure of negotiations , and until EU 
membership, the Commission will put in place a monitoring mechanism to ensure that all 
outstanding reforms are completed by the time of accession and that the commitments made 
during negotiations are maintained and consolidated further, as r elevant. The 2026 
enlargement report on Montenegro could be the last enlargement report prepared for the 
country and be succeeded from next year by monitoring reports. An information and 
consultation procedure will be set up to ensure Montenegro is informed about changes to the 
acquis between signature and accession. The Commission proposes that appropriate 
information arrangements be put in place in the Council and in the Commission already after 
the ﬁnal intergovernmental conference, ahead of signature of the accession treaty. Such 
arrangements would aim to ensure that Montenegro is kept adequately informed of proposals, 
communications, recommendations or initiatives.  
The rigorous accession process aims to ensure that countries joining the EU meet all their 
membership obligations on accession. The strengthened approach to safeguards  will be 
applied for the ﬁrst time in Montenegro’s Accession Treaty. Safeguard clauses are temporary 
provisions designed to tackle, without delay, any shortcomings that may arise from the 
accession of the new Member State and to ensure that negotiation co mmitments are upheld 
after accession. Including robust, effective and proportionate safeguard clauses into the 
accession framework mitigates potential risks and sets a precedent for future candidates, 
balancing incentives with accountability. The core cross -cutting parts  of the Montenegro 
Accession Treaty will serve as a model for  institutional aspects  and safeguards in  future 
accession treaties. 
Preparing the Union also means strengthening public understanding and support for 
enlargement. Member States, EU institutions and enlargement countries share the 
responsibility for communicating transparently about the implications, opportunities and 
challenges of future accessions. A credible enlargement policy requires public confidence

Maintaining a credible and merit-based process 
The renewed momentum behind enlargement must be matched by delivery. The EU will 
continue to support enlargement countries through political engagement, technical 
assistance, investment and gradual integration. In return, progress on membership requires 
sustained implementation of reforms and respect for the values, principles and obligations on 
which the Union is founded. 
Indicative reform ambitions or target dates can help organise work and maintain political 
momentum, but they cannot replace fulfilling accession conditions. The pace of each 
country’s progress will continue to depend on its own merits and its capacity to m eet all 
memberships requirements. 
The enlargement process is both demanding and achievable. The progress made by the most 
advanced candidates shows that reforms lead to transformative results. The responsibility 
now lies with each enlargement country to make full use of the opportunities a vailable, while 
the Union advances its own preparations for a larger and stronger Europe. 
V. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
I. 
For the first time since 2011, when the EU closed accession negotiations with Croatia, the EU 
is in a position to soon close accession negotiations with another candidate country, 
Montenegro. This is testament to Montenegro’s sustained efforts to advance on the European 
path and implement the reforms needed to turn EU membership into reality. It also shows that 
the enlargement process delivers results when the necessary reforms are undertaken.  
EU enlargement is delivering. It is advancing more than at any point in time in the past fifteen 
years. This is reflected in the record high number of intergovernmental conferences held in 
the accession negotiations with Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine. However, 
progress in the other enlargement partners has often been slow or non-existent.

=== PAGE 24 ===
23 
 
The fundamentals of the accession process remain decisive . Democracy, the rule of law, 
fundamental rights, functioning democratic institutions, effective public administrations and 
the economic criteria are indispensable for membership. They continue to determine the 
overall pace of accession negotiations. Once adopted, reforms need to produce sustainable 
results and credible track records. 
Security and resilience has become an increasingly important dimension of accession 
process. Alignment with the EU’s common foreign and security policy , including with EU 
restrictive measures is a key requirement and a signal of strategic orientation in the current 
geopolitical context. In addition, enlargement countries should progressively strengthen their 
alignment and cooperation with the Union on hybrid threats, economic security, cybersecurity, 
critical infrastructure, border management and preparedness. This will help the enlargement 
countries consolidate their role as essential partners in countering cross -border threats and 
in improving the stability and security of the European continent. 
Economic convergence needs to accelerate. Growth Plans, the Ukraine Facility, investment 
and gradual integration offer important opportunities for boosting the economies of 
enlargement countries before accession. The potential gains depend on reforms, regulatory 
alignment and enforcement, and a sound business and investment environment.  
Significant progress has been achieved in recent years on gradual integration . The 
Commission is now putting forward a more structured approach, to bring clear benefits for 
the candidates and the EU alike, support accession preparations and serve shared strategic 
priorities. Participation would remain merit -based, conditional and reversible, and depend on 
policy readiness and strategic alignment. In the first quarter of next year, the Commission will 
launch gradual integration dialogues with the enlargement countries to take stock of existing 
measures and, where possible, to provide a more tailor-made offer.  
The Commission looks forward to discussing further with the Council the conditions that 
should be attached to gradual integration. This could include for instance requirements 
related to CFSP alignment, defence industry integration, rule of law, cooperation on economic 
security or progress in the enlargement process itself. 
Good neighbourly relations and  regional cooperation remain essential. Bilateral issues 
unrelated to accession criteria should not be included in the enlargement negotiations as 
such. Outstanding bilateral issues should be addressed constructively and in good faith, 
through appropriate bilateral or internati onal mechanisms, and existing agreements should 
be implemented. Reconciliation should be advanced: the European path should provide an 
incentive to overcome divisions. 
Strategic communication  and outreach on enlargement across both the Union and the 
enlargement countries will continue to play a key role in making the next EU enlargement a 
success. In parallel, strengthening resilience to foreign information manipulation and 
interference, including disinformation aimed at the enlargement process, remains essential to 
safeguarding the integrity of the process and ensuring informed public debate. The 
Commission has stepped up communication on what accession means for EU citizens and 
businesses, and what benefits and challenges there are for existing Member States. There are 
significant socio-economic, political and security advantages of a bigger and stronger Union. 
An enlarged EU is a better guarantor of political stability, security and peace on the continent.  
The Commission has also raised awareness of the candidate countries most advanced in the 
accession process. Member States and enlargement partners need to lead the national 
debate about enlargement, especially where public support and awareness are comparatively 
weaker. The Commission stands ready to support them in this effort. Two new elements 
complement the 2026 Enlargement package.

First, building on experience from the 2004 and 2013 enlargements, the Commission is 
proposing a set of indicative roadmaps covering milestones and an outlook for the year ahead 
for Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine. These roadmaps will serve both as a pathway 
and an anchor for the accession negotiations for these negotiating countries and for the 
Member States. They will allow for better prioritisation, sequencing and monitoring of the 
process, providing greater predictability – a core principle of the 2020 revised methodology.   
The roadmaps prejudge neither the outcome nor the pace of negotiations or any decision

Second, as some candidates advance towards membership, the EU must be ready to 
welcome them. A larger Union must be stronger, more resilient and remain effective. In its

preparations needed to achieve this ambition - strengthening the Union’s governance, 
safeguarding its values and the rule of law, adapting its policies, and preparing its budget for 
enlargement. 
II. 
Montenegro’s progress is tangible evidence of the transformative power of the enlargement 
process when the acquis alignment is accompanied by ﬁrm focus on implementation. 
Montenegro has largely completed the reforms that are needed to close the negotiations, and 
the Commission has sent the relevant documentation to the Council.  The establishment and 
ongoing work of the Ad Hoc  Working Party on Montenegro’s accession treaty, together with 
the Council Conclusions on the ﬁnancial package based on the Commission’s proposal, 
demonstrates that the EU delivers when a country progresses decisively on its EU path.  
Since the last report, six inter- governmental conferences have been held with Montenegro, 
resulting in the provisional closure of 14 additional chapters, meaning a total of 21 closed 
chapters. Despite recurrent tensions between the ruling majority and the opposition, 
culminating with the failed motion to dismiss the Speaker of Parliament, Montenegro has 
managed to maintain cross -party agreement and broad support for EU -related reforms. 
Through political dialogue and as part of broader efforts to advance the EU accession process, 
this has enabled the Parliament to launch the process for constitutional amendments and 
adopt several of them, along with electoral reforms. It has also enabled the ﬁnalisation of most 
outstanding high-level appointments, including two vacant judges of the Constitutional Court. 
Montenegro has also made great strides in implementing its Reform Agenda, thereby 
supporting the reforms required for accession.  The country remained fully aligned with the 
EU's common foreign and security policy. 
The Commission assessment is that in line with the negotiating framework, an overall balance 
has been ensured between progress under the rule of law chapters and progress in the 
accession negotiations across chapters, and that Montenegro has met all outstanding closing 
benchmarks. As such, it considers that accession negotiations could realistically be closed 
by the end of 2026. The Commission is therefore proposing a roadmap for Montenegro for 
the next phases of the accession process leading up to the signature of the accession treaty. 
In the weeks and months ahead, it will be essential that Montenegro maintains a steady focus 
on completing the preparations for EU membership, sustaining the adopted reforms and 
ensuring continuous cross-party support to its EU path.

ANNEX I  
A roadmap for the accession negotiations with Montenegro 
An outlook for 2027 
 
In the light of the overall progress to date in the accession negotiations, the Commission 
presents the below roadmap for finalising the accession negotiations with Montenegro. The 
roadmap presented below offers clear sequencing for the provisional closure  of the remaining 
individual negotiating chapters as well as the main steps following the conclusion of accession 
negotiations. By the nature of the accession negotiations, the pace of which depends on 
Montenegro’s progress in meeting the requirements for membership, this indicative timetable 
prejudges neither the outcome, nor the pace of negotiations or any decision taken by the 
Council, the Commission or the European Parliament. 
The Commission will continue to support Montenegro in its preparations for accession, and 
will continue to help it with expertise, including by making full use of instruments such as 
TAIEX and twinning, and through well targeted financial assistance, including assistance 
available under the Reform and Growth Facility, based on the Reform Agenda. 
*** 
PHASE I: FROM NOW UNTIL THE CONCLUSION OF NEGOTIATIONS  
Milestone: final intergovernmental conference - indicative date: December 2026 
Following the IGC on 12 October 2026, 14 chapters remain to be closed in the accession 
negotiations, which requires Council agreement that Montenegro has met the closing 
benchmarks as well as agreement on transitional arrangements in certain chapters, on t he 
financial package for Montenegro and the respective institutional and other arrangements 
related to the country’s accession.  
The Commission considers that Montenegro has met all the closing benchmarks in the 
accession negotiations and by mid-November the Commission intends to provide all remaining 
draft EU common positions to the Council. The Commission therefore considers that accession 
negotiations could be completed as soon as December 2026 according to the below priority 
schedule for the closure of the chapters: 
IGC in November 
- Energy  
- Transport 
- Food safety, veterinary and phytosanitary policy 
- Taxation  
- Free movement of goods  
  
IGC in December 
- Financial services  
- Regional policy and coordination of structural instruments 
- Environment and climate change

PHASE II. BETWEEN THE CONCLUSION OF NEGOTIATIONS AND SIGNATURE OF THE 
ACCESSION TREATY  
Without prejudice to the outcome of any decision of the Council, the European Commission 
and the European Parliament in the process leading to Montenegro’s accession to the European 
Union, the Commission would foresee the following steps following the conc lusion of the 
accession negotiations at the last intergovernmental conference in December 2026: 
 
1. Progressive association with the Union’s institutions and working structures – phase 1  
Milestone:  final intergovernmental conference – indicative date December 2026  
The Commission proposes that appropriate information arrangements be put in place in the 
Council and in the Commission already after the final intergovernmental conference, ahead of 
signature of the accession treaty. Such arrangements would aim to ensure t hat Montenegro is 
kept adequately informed of proposals, communications, recommendations or initiatives.  
 
2. Completing the work on the draft accession treaty  
Indicative date: spring of 2027 
Requirements 
- the Commission to continue to submit to the Council the remaining draft texts at the 
beginning of 2027; 
- the draft accession treaty is agreed by the Member States and Montenegro;  
- the draft accession treaty is translated into the current EU treaty languages and 
Montenegrin.  
 
3. Signature of the accession treaty with Montenegro  
Indicative date: June 2027 
Requirements  
- EU primary law is translated into Montenegrin and revised by the Council;  
- the Commission issues a positive opinion on Montenegro’s accession to the EU;  
- the European Parliament gives its consent to Montenegro’s accession; 
- the Council adopts a Council decision on the admission of Montenegro to the European 
Union.

PHASE III: INTERIM PERIOD BETWEEN SIGNATURE AND MONTENEGRO’S ACCESSION TO THE 
EUROPEAN UNION 
 
Progressive association with the Union’s institutions and working structures – phase 2  
Milestone: signature of the accession treaty - indicative date: June 2027 
The European Parliament may invite the parliament of the acceding country to appoint 
observers. The Commission will examine structured political participation in relevant 
Commission activities, in full respect of the Treaties, the Commission’s institutional autonomy 
and the principle of collegiality. 
Following signature of the accession treaty, all Member States and Montenegro will need to

Pre-accession monitoring  
The Commission will continue to asses all the way to accession whether commitments, 
including those related to the fundamentals, are being implemented by Montenegro and 
maintained and will report regularly to the European Parliament and the Council. The 
Commission will continue to make use of regular peer assessment missions, the bi -annual 
monitoring tables as well as of all other available tools and measures. As in the past, the 
Commission stands ready to report to the Council and the European Parliament r egularly on 
Montenegro’s progress in meeting its commitments from the accession negotiations. The 
monitoring should identify shortcomings early and specify the corrective action required. 
Where serious issues of concern arise, the Accession Treaty should enable the Council, acting 
on a Commission proposal, to adopt appropriate measures focused on those commitments. 
Where monitoring identifies backsliding before the first day of membership and targeted 
measures would be insufficient, a postponement clause in the Accession Treaty could provide 
a last resort instrument.

ANNEX II 
A roadmap for the accession negotiations with Albania 
An outlook for 2027 
 
I. Introduction 
In line with the negotiating framework for the accession negotiations, which foresees that the 
intergovernmental conferences take stock of the overall accession process and set out an 
indication for the year ahead of which clusters / chapters could potentially be opened or closed, 
as well as in light of the overall progress achieved to date in the negotiations, the Commission 
presents the below roadmap for accession negotiations with Albania for 2027. 
The roadmap offers a sequencing for the provisional closure of negotiating chapters and cluster 
1, as a whole. It therefore provides both a pathway and an anchor for the accession negotiations 
for both Albania and the Member States.  
Given the nature of accession negotiations, the pace of which depends on Albania’s progress 
in meeting the requirements for membership, this indicative timeline  neither prejudges the 
outcome, nor the pace of negotiations . This  roadmap is a living document, which the  
Commission will update regularly, based on the progress made by Albania.  
In line with the negotiating framework, progress in the negotiations remains measured in 
particular against the requirements of the Copenhagen criteria, including the meeting of the 
closing benchmarks set for the individual chapters and for cluster 1, the fulfilment of Albania’s 
obligations under the Stabilisation and Association Agreement, as well as Albania's progress in 
addressing the key areas identified in the Commission's reports and relevant Council 
conclusions. Progress under the fundamentals’ cluster determines the overall pace of 
negotiations, Albania needs to devote substantial further efforts to complete the work on the 
closing benchmarks set out in the negotiating clusters to be able to provisionally close the 
chapters. Progressing along the timelines set out in this roadmap will therefore de pend upon 
the pace of reforms in Albania. 
II. Outlook for the year ahead  
1. Priority schedule for provisional closure of chapters  
Following the IGC on 13 October 2026, 31 chapters of the accession negotiations remain to be 
closed. In order to reach the ambition of the Albanian authorities to close accession negotiations 
by the end of 2027, it would be necessary for Albania to complete its work on the closing 
benchmarks sufficiently in time as to allow for the closure of the remaining chapters, after 
verification of the fulfilment of the closing benchmarks by the EU, and for the EU and Albania 
to reach agreement on the transitional arrangements. Taking into consideration the relative 
complexity and level of preparation in each chapter, as well as the relevant reform plans of the 
authorities, the following initial priority schedule could be envisaged for the remainder of 2026 
and 2027 for provisional closure of chapters: 
Priority schedule for the remainder of 2026: intellectual property law; economic and monetary 
policy; company law; fisheries and aquaculture; Trans -European networks; foreign and 
security policy

Priority schedule for the first semester of 2027: public procurement; right of establishment and 
freedom to provide services; free movement of capital; digital transformation and media; 
Customs Union; financial control; transport policy; social policy and employment; financial 
services; consumer and health protection. 
Priority schedule for the second semester of 2027: freedom of movement of workers; 
competition policy; agriculture and rural development; taxation; free movement of goods; food 
safety, veterinary and phytosanitary policy; statistics; regional policy and coordination of 
structural instruments; energy; enviro nment and climate change; judiciary and fundamental 
rights; justice, freedom and security; financial and budgetary provisions; institutions; other 
issues; cluster 1 (the fundamentals), closed last. 
Where the pace of reforms allows, the Commission will revise the priority schedule to reflect 
the possibility of earlier provisional closure of chapters, subject to the relevant conditions being 
met. 
2. Other milestones of the accession negotiations 
Subject to Albania making the necessary progress in its preparations, the Commission will 
present a Communication in due course on a financial package for the accession of Albania. 
The Commission further recommends that the Council starts the arrangements for drafting the 
Accession Treaty with Albania in the course of 2027. In the meantime, Albania needs to ensure 
the timely availability of the translation of the acquis in Albanian. 
III. Gradual integration 
Accession preparation must go hand in hand with progressively deeper integration of Albania 
into Union policies, programmes, systems, agencies and working structures before 
membership. This includes advancing participation in strategic sectors, expanding access to 
parts of the Single Market where conditions are met, strengthening energy, transport and digital 
connectivity, deepening security and defence cooperation, and delivering concrete benefits for 
citizens and businesses. This will bring Albania closer to the EU and the Single Market already 
ahead of membership. Bearing in mind Albania’s advanced stage of negotiations, which brings 
membership closer, further areas of cooperation for gradual integration prior to accession will 
be identified through the gradual integration dialogue to be launched in the first quarter of  
2027. Deeper integration requires sustained alignment with the Union’s values, acquis  and 
strategic interests. 
∗∗∗ 
The Commission will continue to support Albania in its preparations for accession and will 
continue to help it with expertise, including by making full use of instruments such as TAIEX 
and twinning, and through well targeted financial assistance, including assistance available 
under the Reform and Growth Facility, based on the Reform Agenda. 
The Commission will closely monitor that the commitments undertaken by Albania in the 
negotiations in particular in the area of the fundamentals, are fulfilled and will report on its 
findings regularly to the Council. It will make use of monitoring tables, regular peer assessment 
missions, including as regards the rule of law, as well as of all other available tools.

A roadmap for the accession negotiations with Moldova 
An outlook for 2027 
 
I. Introduction 
In line with the negotiating framework for the accession negotiations, which foresees that the 
intergovernmental conferences take stock of the overall accession process and set out an 
indication for the year ahead of which clusters / chapters could potentially be opened or closed, 
as well as in light of the overall progress achieved to date in the negotiations, the Commission 
presents the below roadmap for the accession negotiations with Moldova for 2027.  
The roadmap outlines the main deliverables of the accession negotiations by the end of 2027, 
starting with opening the remaining two negotiating clusters, the meeting the interim 
benchmarks on the rule of law chapters, followed by the provisional closure of a number of 
individual negotiating chapters. It therefore provides both a pathway and an anchor for the 
accession negotiations for both Moldova and the Member States.  
Given the nature of accession negotiations, the pace of which depends on Moldova’s progress 
in meeting the requirements for membership, this indicative timeline  neither prejudges the

complete the work on the closing benchmarks set out in the negotiating clusters . Progressing 
along the timelines set out in this roadmap will therefore depend upon the pace of reforms in 
Moldova. 
II. Outlook for the year ahead  
1. Opening of the remaining clusters 
The Commission considers that Moldova has met the necessary conditions and urges the 
accession conference to make every effort to open the outstanding clusters -  cluster 4 (The 
green agenda and sustainable connectivity) and cluster 5 (Resources, agriculture, and cohesion) 
by the end of 2026.  
2. Meeting the interim benchmarks on the rule of law 
In order to reach the next phase of accession negotiations where negotiating chapters can be 
provisionally closed, it would be necessary for Moldova to complete its work on the interim 
benchmarks on the rule of law chapters. The Commission considers that M oldova is well 
advanced in its preparations to meet the interim benchmarks and will present the interim 
benchmark assessment report together with the draft interim common position to the Council, 
allowing Member States to process this assessment.

benchmarks on the rule of law chapters, followed by the provisional closur e of a number of

Given the nature of accession negotiations, the pace of which depends on Ukraine’s progress

benchmarks on the rule of law chapters. The Commission welcomes the int ention of Ukraine
