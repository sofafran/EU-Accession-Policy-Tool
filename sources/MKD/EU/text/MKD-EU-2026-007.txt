1. INTRODUCTION1 
1.1. CONTEXT 
All screening reports on the six clusters were presented to the Council. North Macedonia 
has continued work toward completing the necessary documents to fulfil the opening 
benchmarks for cluster 1 (‘Fundamentals of the accession process’). No progress was made 
towards adopting relevant constitutional changes as required under the 2022 Council 
Conclusions. North Macedonia continued to implement the Stabilisation and Association 
Agreement and several joint bodies under the agreement met. This included the Stabilisation 
and Association Committee, which met in April 2026, the first time since 2021. 
Essential EU- related reforms need to be implemented. North Macedonia has actively 
engaged on the new Growth Plan for the Western Balkans and has stepped up efforts to 
deliver on implementing the plan’s four pillars of gradual integration with the EU single 
market, regional economic integration, fundamental reforms and increased financial 
support. The Commission’s 2026 rule of law report covered North Macedonia for the third 
time. 
North Macedonia  maintained good relations with other enlargement countries and 
continued its engagement in regional initiatives. Existing bilateral agreements with 
neighbouring countries need to be implemented in good faith by all parties, including the 
Prespa Agreement with Greece and the Treaty of Friendship, Good Neighbourliness and 
Cooperation with Bulgaria. North Macedonia  maintained its full alignment with the EU’s 
common foreign and security policy, including on Russia’s war of aggression against 
Ukraine, sending a strong signal of its strategic choice of EU  accession and showing itself to 
be a reliable partner. 
 
1.2. MAIN FINDINGS OF THE REPORT 
CLUSTER 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS OF THE ACCESSION PROCESS 
Democracy 
The legal and institutional framework provides a basis for the democratic conduct of 
elections. However, no progress was made in aligning with EU standards and implementing 
outstanding recommendations of the Organisation for Security and Cooperation in Europe 
(OSCE)/Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR) and the Venice 
Commission with regard to the electoral code. Electoral disinformation and foreign 
information manipulation remain insufficiently addressed. North Macedonia needs to ensure 
alignment with all relevant EU acquis, including Regulation (EU) 2024/900 on the 
transparency and targeting of political advertising, taking into account European and 
international democratic standards. 
Parliament can exercise its powers in a partially effective way. Parliament adopted a new 
Code of Ethics for Members of Parliament and a new strategic plan. Despite having clear

Rules of Procedure, Parliament again failed to adopt its annual agenda. Consensus on

The institutions in charge of the EU integration process  are in place. No monitoring reports

The government coalition remained stable. Some progress was made on local government

Civil society, including human rights defenders, operated in an overall enabling environment. 
The Council for Cooperation between the Government and Civil Society was reactivated. Civil

Public administration reform 
North Macedonia remains moderately prepared in the area of public administration reform. 
Some progress was made by further expanding the offer of electronic services and 
upgrading the system for electronic management of documents. Efforts are on- going for 
amending the Law on administrative servants and the Law on public service employees to 
address weaknesses identified after their adoption. The country also enacted a Law on top 
management, with serious shortcomings regarding the composition of the selection boards, 
the methodologies for the selection and recruitment of top managers and objective criteria 
for dismissal. The reorganisation of several state institutions in line with the amended Law 
on state organisation continued. In November 2025, North Macedonia adopted its roadmap 
on public administration reform. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ adopt and implement the revised Law on administrative servants, the revised Law on 
public service employees and the rules governing top management; 
→ finalise the revision of, and continue implementing, the Law on state organisation and 
other related laws in line with the recommendations on the new organisation of state 
administrative bodies, and ensure full implementation of the Law on general 
administrative procedure; 
→ ensure full implementation of the Integrated Financial Management Information System 
(IFMIS) in line with the revised organic budget law (OBL) requirements, particularly by 
awarding and rolling out the IFMIS and by being fully on track with putting the core 
modules into operation. 
Chapter 23: Judiciary and fundamental rights 
North Macedonia is between having some and a moderate level of preparation in applying 
the EU acquis and European standards in this area. Overall, there was  limited progress . 
North Macedonia made progress in developing its rule of law roadmap, which sets out 
commitments for the coming years on further alignment with the EU acquis  and 
strengthening institutional capacity.

Functioning of the judiciary 
The judicial system of North Macedonia is  between having some and a moderate level of 
preparation and made some progress. The country continued implementing the Strategy for 
Judicial Reform (2024 -2028). It adopted a new Law on the Judicial Council, largely 
addressing the Venice Commission recommendations. Furthermore, it adopted a new Law 
on the Council for Public Prosecutors, a Law on salaries of judges, a Law on salaries of 
prosecutors as well as a new Law on civil procedure. The judiciary’s overall independence 
and financial autonomy is undermined by persisting interference from other state branches 
and the drastic lack of financial and human resources. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented. In the 
coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ accelerate implementation of the Strategy for judicial reform; 
→ implement the newly adopted laws on the Judicial Council, on the Council for Public 
Prosecutors, and timely revise the Law on the Public Prosecutor’s Office to strengthen 
those institutions, addressing the outstanding recommendations of the 2023 peer 
review mission and the opinions of the Venice Commission and adopting necessary 
implementing legislation; 
→ consistently filling judicial vacancies including by stepping up the implementation of the 
human resources strategies for the judiciary and the public prosecution service as well 
as securing sufficient funding and ensure that the Automated Court Case Management 
Information System is fully functional. 
Fight against corruption 
North Macedonia is between having some and a moderate level of preparation and made no 
progress on the prevention of and fight against corruption. Corruption remains prevalent and 
is an issue of serious concern.  The January 2026 amendments to the Criminal Code 
concerning the offences of abuse of official position and criminal association do not 
adequately address the adverse effects of the 2023 amendments and have contributed to 
legal uncertainty. The State Commission for the Prevention of Corruption needs to step up 
the proactive identification of potential cases of misconduct. High-level corruption cases 
continue to face delays and often reach the statute of limitations, contributing to the 
perception of impunity. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were not addressed and therefore 
remain valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ adopt a new Criminal Code and a new Law on criminal procedure in line with the EU 
acquis and international standards; 
→ improve its track record in high- level corruption cases – with timely, professional and 
transparent proactive investigations, prosecutions, final convictions and recovery, 
including confiscation of criminal assets, value -based confiscation, extended 
confiscation and third-party confiscation; 
→ allocate sufficient human and financial resources, including financial experts, to: (i) the 
Public Prosecutor’s Office and the Basic Public Prosecutor’s Office for Prosecuting 
Organised Crime and Corruption, to ensure effective accountability in high- level 
corruption cases; and (ii) the State Commission for the Prevention of Corruption, to 
improve the verification of asset declarations. 
Fundamental rights

The legal framework on fundamental rights is overall aligned with the EU acquis and 
European standards. The country continues to meet its general obligations on fundamental 
rights. However, the implementation of legislation needs to improve. Parliament elected a 
new Ombudsperson, but eight new Deputy Ombudspersons still need to be chosen. The 
Ombudsperson’s Office and the Commission on Prevention and Protection against 
Discrimination still lack financial autonomy. Gender -based violence remains a serious 
human rights issue, encompassing domestic violence, psychological, economic and cyber 
violence, and femicide. No steps were taken to align with the EU acquis  on non -
discrimination and equality, including gender equality. Despite the renovation of some prison 
facilities, the overall situation in prisons remains appalling. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were addressed to a limited extent and 
therefore remain valid. In the coming year, the country should in particular: 
→ address dire conditions in prisons and continue efforts to promote alternatives to 
incarceration, and implement relevant recommendations on detention conditions made 
by national and international institutions, particularly recommendations of the European 
Committee for the Prevention of Torture and Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or 
Punishment (CPT); 
→ appoint the eight Deputy Ombudspersons and ensure that the Ombudsperson’s Office 
and the Commission on Prevention and Protection against Discrimination receive 
sufficient financial and human resources, enabling them to function independently and 
effectively; 
→ provide adequate support to women survivors of gender- based violence, vulnerable 
categories of children, especially child  victims of violence, Roma 2 children and children 
with disabilities. 
Freedom of expression and media freedom 
North Macedonia is between having some and a moderate level of preparation  on freedom 
of expression and has made no progress in this area. The country took some steps to align 
the legal framework with the European Media Freedom Act. The Agency for Audio and 
Audio-Visual Media Services and the public service broadcaster continue to face challenges, 
primarily due to limited financial and administrative autonomy and irregular funding. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and 
therefore remain valid. In the coming year, the country should in particular: 
→ continue to revise the legal framework governing the media to align it with the EU acquis  
and European standards, particularly by introducing rules on full transparency in media 
ownership and allocation of public funds for state advertising, competition and State aid, 
and intellectual property rights; 
→ implement the strategy to reform the public broadcaster; 
→ continue to promptly address all threats and acts of violence against journalists and 
ensure perpetrators are brought to justice.

Fight against serious and organised crime 
North Macedonia is between  having some and a moderate level of preparation in the fight 
against serious and organised crime and has made limited progress  in this area. North 
Macedonia continued to make progress in the fight against organised crime but should 
increase its capacities to fight all forms of serious and organised crime. Two revised laws 
on weapons and on testing and marking firearms, aligning with the relevant EU acquis,  were 
adopted. Further alignment with the EU acquis  on the fight against serious and organised 
crime is needed.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were only partially met and therefore 
remain largely valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ continue to improve its track record of investigations, prosecutions and convictions in

ECONOMIC CRITERIA 
The existence of a functioning market economy 
North Macedonia has made limited progress and is at a good level of preparation in 
developing a functioning market economy. Real GDP growth accelerated in 2025, driven by 
domestic demand. Inflation rebounded, reflecting both domestic and international price 
pressures. The 2025 fiscal deficit declined in line with plans, although the spending 
composition deteriorated. Capital spending execution continues to suffer from weak public 
investment management. Efforts to formalise the informal economy have slowed down. The 
central bank maintained a prudent monetary policy and further strengthened the 
macroprudential framework. The banking sector remained resilient amidst a strong rise in 
credit growth. The labour market situation improved slightly, but structural problems, 
including low participation rates, skills mismatches, substantial emigration and a large 
gender gap, pose obstacles to growth. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were only partially implemented and

therefore remain largely valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ strengthen fiscal consolidation by reducing tax expenditure and containing the rise in 
public sector wages and pensions, and by making  the Fiscal Council fully 
independent and improving its access to data; 
→ accelerate the implementation of the public investment management action plan and 
adopt the Law on public-private partnerships; 
→ adopt and implement a revised action plan for formalising the informal economy. 
The capacity to cope with competitive pressure and market forces within the EU 
North Macedonia has made  some progress and is  moderately prepared to cope with 
competitive pressure and market forces within the EU. The digitalisation of the economy has 
advanced, and the reform of vocational education and training has seen progress. By 
contrast, higher education remains characterised by outdated curricula. Significant 
investment needs in transport and energy infrastructure persist. Integration with the EU in 
trade and investment, as well as overall trade openness, remained high in 2025. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and 
therefore remain valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ address shortcomings in higher education with a view to reducing the skills mismatch; 
→ ensure substantial progress in the implementation of key transport infrastructure 
projects, such as Road Corridors VIII and X; 
→ adopt the new Climate Law with a view to creating the conditions for carbon pricing. 
Chapter 5: Public procurement 
North Macedonia is moderately prepared in the area of public procurement. No progress  
was made since the last assessment. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were not implemented and remain 
valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ adopt the Law on public- private partnership (PPP) and the Law on concessions, to 
further align the legal framework in those areas with the EU acquis; 
→ increase competition and the use of most economically advantageous tender (MEAT) 
criteria for the award of contracts and improve the monitoring and controls of public 
procurement procedures through reporting on and following up on irregularities, cases of 
conflict of interest and fraudulent practices; 
→ strengthen procurement oversight and improve coordination among key stakeholders to 
fight corruption, and strengthen their capacities to implement the principles of 
transparency, free competition, equal treatment and non-discrimination.

The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular:

North Macedonia is moderately prepared on the free movement of goods . No progress was

Chapter 17: Economic and monetary policy 
The country is between a moderate and a good level of preparation in the area of economic 
and monetary policy. Some progress was made, including on a new budget classification,

therefore remain largely valid. In the coming year, North Macedonia should in particular: 
→ ensure the timely implementation of the reforms envisaged in the organic budget law,

2.1.2. Public administration reform 
Strategic framework for public administration reform 
The strategic framework for public administration is in place. North Macedonia adopted a 
revised Public Administration Reform (PAR) Strategy Action Plan (2025–2026) in December 
2025, completing the 2023- 2030 public administration strategy. In November 2025, the 
country adopted the PAR roadmap. The overall implementation rate of the PAR strategy in 
2025 was 67%, while the public financial management (PFM) reform programme’s 
implementation rate reached 47%. The budgetary allocations for both remained unchanged, 
with almost two thirds of all measures funded by donors. Close involvement by the Ministry

On the institutional framework , the PAR Council continued to provide political support,

strategic planning and reporting mechanisms, thus enabling timely identification of 
priorities. 
Policy development and coordination 
Policy development and coordination  mechanisms are largely in place, but their efficiency 
needs to be further improved. During the reporting period, the new framework for sector and 
cross‑sector strategies was fully implemented in the relevant state institutions, with some 
progress by ministries on applying uniformly the new standards and methodologies. With 
this framework in place, the General Secretariat strengthened its quality oversight role by 
consistently issuing formal opinions on all draft strategies. A single register of strategic 
planning documents became fully operational in 2025. The register includes data on 
strategies and action plans and their links to the National Development Strategy and EU 
negotiation clusters. No reports are being published on the implementation of the annual 
government work plan. 
On evidence-based policymaking , a methodology on regulatory impact assessment (RIA) 
adopted in June 2025 ensures consistent quality analysis. The parliamentary administration 
established a dedicated unit on impact assessment, but it is not yet operational. 
On public consultations, feedback on published draft laws in the Single Electronic National 
Register of Regulations needs to improve. However, the government’s annual work plan and 
reports remain inaccessible to the public. Agendas of government sessions remain 
unavailable to the public prior to meetings and delays continue in the publication of minutes 
and decisions of government sessions. Overall, implementation and quality of regulatory 
policy management tools remain insufficient. 
Public financial management 
The public financial management system is broadly in place but needs to be further 
improved. Implementation rates of the activities laid down in the PFM reform programme 
2025-2028, the core strategic framework , remain low. The Ministry of Finance continues to 
be affected by institutional capacity challenges, particularly with regard to attracting and 
retaining personnel with suitable skills and qualifications. 
On budget management, the entry into force of the reformed organic budget law (OBL) was 
postponed further to January 2028 following the approval of a new addendum in December 
2025. Some progress was made in adopting implementing legislation, but full enforcement 
of the OBL by 2028 remains a challenge due to delays in implementing the Integrated 
Financial Management Information System (IFMIS). However, reforms that do not depend 
on IFMIS implementation could be already moved forward. These include implementation of 
spending rev iews, the strengthening of fiscal risks monitoring and the finalisation of a tax 
expenditure report. While the OBL incorporates gender- based budgeting as a principle, 
further efforts are needed to strengthen policy discussions during budget preparation and 
increase the use of gender-disaggregated indicators. 
Progress on public investment management  included: (i) putting into operation a database 
to monitor the implementation of high- value projects; and (ii) efforts to develop a more 
systematic approach to project appraisal and prioritisation, including better tracking of 
budget allocations and alignment with strategic priorities. 
The Fiscal Council is broadly operational, but its administrative capacity should be 
increased.

Public service and human resources management 
Public service and human resources management need to be further improved.  The civil 
service system remained fragmented, as rules governing personnel policy for civil servants 
under special statutes continued to differ from those for the majority of public servants. 
The revised legislation on administrative servants and on public -sector employees, adopted

preserve uniform and standardised selection practices across the administration as well as 
meritocracy and objectivity in granting permanent and fixed -term contracts in the public

promotion procedures are applied inconsistently. Politicisation, pay scales with low salaries 
and limited promotion prospects undermine the public administration’s attractiveness as an 
employer. Although slight improvements to the collection of performance data, the 
performance appraisal system continues to be applied inconsistently. 
The job classification and remuneration  systems still need to be improved to provide for 
more job security, stability and retention of staff. The salary system is not unified across 
public bodies, and it is common for state institutions to individually sign collective 
agreements introducing salary supplements with their employees. Initiatives are ongoing to 
revise the remuneration system to introduce a unified mechanism. A comprehensive 
retention policy for the public administration is still lacking, while a partial retention policy 
for those wh o work on EU integration and IPA (instrument for pre- accession) matters is 
applied since February 2026. A law on top management was enacted in June 2026, with 
serious shortcomings regarding the composition of the selection boards, in the 
methodologies for selecting and recruiting top managers and objective criteria for 
dismissals. 
The Human Resource Management Information System  (HRMIS) is in the process of being 
upgraded to automatically manage key human resources processes, payroll functions and 
employee data. The main bottleneck is the current lack of interoperability between systems 
and the lack of regular updates. The Ministry of Public Administration increased its 
capacities to coordinate and monitor human resources management across the public 
administration, although central coordination capacities still need to be further 
strengthened. 
Gender balance  is respected. According to the 2025 register of public sector employees, 
over 57.5% of all public services employees were women, excluding the army and several 
law enforcement agencies. Preparations to establish a public administration academy are

The accountability of public administration needs further improvement. 
The reorganisation of central government institutions to improve efficiency and 
accountability continued. Discussion on legislative amendments to the systemic Law on the 
organisation and operation of state administrative bodies is ongoing. Internal governance  
still suffers from overlapping responsibilities, and a large number of state bodies are 
accountable to Parliament, even though their performance is not monitored by it in an

Service delivery to citizens and businesses still requires efforts to meet EU standards,

2.2.1 Chapter 23 - Judiciary and fundamental rights 
The EU’s founding values include the rule of law and respect for human rights. An effective

corruption are of paramount importance, as is the respect for fundamental rights in law and in

delayed timely follow- up and the introduction of necessary measures to address 
shortcomings in the judiciary. The implementation rate of activities in 2025 increased to

Institutional framework 
The institutional framework on the fight against corruption is in place. It is adequate for

Freedom of thought, conscience and religion 
North Macedonia provides adequate legal guarantees on freedom of thought, conscience 
and religion. The rights to freedom of thought, conscience and religion remain guaranteed

Freedom of expression and media freedom 
North Macedonia remains moderately prepared in the area of freedom of expression. 
Limited progress was made in advancing the legislative framework in line with EU standards, 
and structural challenges affecting the media environment and institutional independence 
remain. 
Intimidation of journalists 
Existing legal mechanisms remain insufficient to address all forms of threats, especially 
those originating online or from unknown perpetrators. While institutional contact points 
play a role in building trust, effectiveness depends on tangible outcomes in investigations 
and judicial proceedings. In 2025, five cases of violations of journalists’ rights were reported 
by the Association of Journalists. These included serious incidents such as arson targeting 
a journalist’s property, coordinated online harassm ent of women journalists, and a violent 
newsroom intrusion. Legal threats are another concern. In 2025, there was one case related 
to Specific legislation against strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPP) 
lawsuits against journalists and 40 lawsuits for insult and defamation involving journalists, 
compared to 15 cases in 2024 signalling a negative trend in the protection of journalistic 
freedom. Women journalists faced rising levels of online abuse, sexualised attacks and 
discriminatory rhetoric. Judicial transparency remains limited, with courts providing 
information only upon request and lacking systematic reporting, while existing statistical 
systems do not enable proper tracking of cases involving journalists. Rules on digital 
abuses, including AI-generated content, have not yet been introduced to the criminal code. 
Legislative environment 
The legal framework governing the media is in place and partially in line with the EU acquis . 
There are ongoing legislative revision efforts, with the support of EU -funded projects, to 
further align the legislation with the European Media Freedom Act (EMFA). North Macedonia 
should implement preparatory steps to help national authorities fulfil their regulatory 
function upon accession and enable civil society to support implementation of the European 
Media Freedom Act and the Digital Services Act. Operational readiness will require 
institution-building, awareness -raising and stakeholder engagement.  The issue of 
circumventing the register of online media in the context of paid political advertising 
remains unresolved, creating space for abuse and non- transparent financing of the media 
during elections. The reintroduction of state -funded campaigns in private media continues 
to pose a risk of political influence over editorial policy and endangers the economic

still not harmonised with the EU acquis  or with broader European standards. North 
Macedonia has yet to establish systemic mechanisms to support media pluralism and 
public interest journalism.  Specific legislation against strategic lawsuits against public 
participation (SLAPPs) is yet to be adopted to align with the EU Anti -SLAPP legal framework. 
Limited awareness of this issue persists. As a result, protection against this form of 
persecution for journalists and other public figures continues to be limited. 
Implementation of legislation/institutions 
In 2025 and 2026, the state budget funding allocated to the broadcasting sector was 
temporarily decreased below the legal requirements, before the government subsequently 
disbursed the full amount in instalments. This has had negative consequences for both the 
Agency for Audio and Audio- Visual Media Services and the public service broadcaster, 
undermining the predictability of planning and the implementation of annual programmes, 
including hiring of the necessary personnel. 
The Agency for Audio and Audio -Visual Media Services proactively monitored broadcast 
media and published a range of analyses that contributed to a more informed policy 
environment. It also amended several pieces of implementing legislation to further align the 
national framework with the Audiovisual Media Services Directive, particularly regarding 
programming obligations, protection of minors, and audiovisual commercial 
communications. However, the institutional and technical capacities required to fully 
exercise the Agency’s mandate remain limited, particularly in relation to the oversight and 
registration of video-sharing platform providers.  
Despite delays in managerial appointment, the Agency for Free Access to Public Information 
resolved its backlog, in a context in which public institutions regularly fail to respect 
response deadlines for access requests. 
Public service broadcaster 
In April 2026, the Programme Council of Macedonian Radio Television  (MRT) elected a new 
director. Despite the generally appropriate regulatory framework, the broadcaster remains at 
risk of political interference, including through the withholding of allocated funds. Delays in 
appointing governing bodies and reductions in funding can be perceived as structural forms 
of pressure. MRT has yet to settle its outstanding debts and its strategic plan lacks a 
multiannual financial projection. Additional funding for technological development is at the 
government’s discretion, leavi ng scope for political influence. The broadcaster has limited 
administrative autonomy and internal self -regulation mechanisms, and procedures are not 
sufficiently clarified in the Code of Ethics. 
Economic factors 
The country did not adopt a strategy for the development of media sectors. The media 
industry is highly fragmented and operates with limited advertising revenues in a financially 
constrained market, undermining sustainability and investment in quality content. Local and 
regional media, including non -profit and community media, continue to face significant 
financial challenges. Transparency surrounding paid political advertising remains an issue, 
particularly in online and social media. Print, broadcast and digital media are legally obliged 
to disclose their ownership to the audiovisual media regulatory body. In February 2026, the 
Agency for Audio and Audio- Visual Media Services established a register of influencers, 
vloggers and content creators, and adopted a related guidance document following a public 
consultation. A self-regulatory ownership register for digital national media is also in place.

Professional organisations and working conditions 
The Council of Media Ethics supports the promotion of ethical journalism but faces long-

Freedom of assembly and association 
North Macedonia provides adequate legal guarantees to the freedom of assembly and 
association, and the regulation of assemblies is adequate. The right to freedom of assembly 
and association was effectively upheld, with protests during the reporting period taking 
place peacefully and without incidents. No restrictions on assemblies or cases of excessive 
use of force were reported. Freedom of assembly and association is guaranteed by the 
Constitution, and the legal framework is broadly aligned with international standards. Further 
efforts are needed to implement relevant European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) rulings, 
particularly regarding the registration of associations. Future changes to the legislation 
regulating the freedom of assembly and association should fully comply with international 
standards and take into consideration recommendations from the Venice Commission and 
the European Commission against Racism and Intolerance (ECRI). 
Labour and trade union rights are covered in Chapter 19 – Social policy and employment. 
Property rights 
The legal and institutional framework for enforcing property rights is broadly in place. 
However, procedures are lengthy and costly for the public. There has been no progress with 
the national strategy on property rights. People face most difficulties at municipal level due 
to outdated, missing or inaccurate bills, as property registers are outdated and kept in 
analogue format. 
The Cadastre Office continued the process of digitalisation and further reduced the bulk of 
pending cases by additional 40%. Administrative authorities handling property restitutions 
are facing an excessive caseload due to understaffing and funding constraints. Although the 
process has been formally closed, there has been no progress on pending denationalisation 
cases, many of which are pending before the ECtHR. 
Non-discrimination 
The legal and institutional framework on non -discrimination is incomplete. The capacity of 
equality bodies needs to be improved. 
The anti-discrimination legal framework is not aligned with the Racial Equality Directive, the 
Directives on standards for equality bodies, EU gender- equality legislation concerning 
violence against women, work -life balance, pay transparency and women on company 
boards, and need to be more effectively enforced. 
Despite existing legal obligations, the harmonisation of non -discrimination principles across 
relevant legislation continues to be delayed. The Constitutional Court has received two

The Parliamentary selection process for appointments to the Commission on Prevention

In 2025, the Commission on Prevention and Protection against Discrimination addressed

Anti-racism and hate offences 
The legal and institutional framework on anti- racism and hate offences is incomplete. 
Implementation and enforcement need to be improved. 
The legal framework and institutional capacity to address hate speech and hate crimes need 
to be further strengthened to ensure alignment with the EU acquis and European standards, 
in particular the 2008 Council Framework Decision on combating racism and xenophobia. In 
order to further strenghten its national framework, North Macedonia should implement the 
Council of Europe’s ECRI General Policy Recommendation No  15 on hate speech and fully 
follow up on the findings of its September 2023 report, without delay. Systematic data 
collection by authorities continues to be lacking, while civil society organisations continue to 
monitor and document cases independently. 
In 2025, according to civil society organisations, alleged hate speech incidents registered 
were most commonly based on ethnicity, sexual orientation and gender identity. The internet 
and social media have become the primary platform and source of hate speech. North 
Macedonia still needs to ratify Protocol No  2 to the Convention on Cybercrime, which would 
enable authorities to more effectively identify individuals behind social media profiles and 
groups.

The legal and institutional framework on the rights of persons with disabilities is incomplete.

LGBTIQ+ persons 
The legal protection of the rights of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, intersex and queer

relevant ECtHR case- law. No progress was made in implementing the January 2019 
European Court of Human Rights judgment on legal gender recognition  and a quick, 
transparent and accessible procedure for legal gender recognition remains lacking. 
Amendments to the rulebook on reporting violence in schools removed sexual orientation as 
a possible motive for violence, risking excluding cases of hate -motivated violence against 
LGBTIQ+ persons from official data and weakening the evidence base for effective 
prevention.

speech against LGBTIQ+ persons persists, particularly on social media. Increased 
investigations by the Public Prosecutor’s Office into physical attacks against LGBTIQ+ 
persons are a positive development, but hate speech cases are often not pursued and no 
progress was made in long -standing investigations into attacks against LGBTIQ+ persons.

Procedural rights of suspects and accused persons and rights of victims of crime 
The legal framework on procedural rights for suspects and accused persons as well as the 
rights of victims of crime is partially aligned with the EU acquis and needs to be improved. 
The right to a fair trial, and presumption of innocence are formally protected in law. 
However, full alignment with the EU acquis is not yet ensured. The Law on free legal aid 
serves as basis for the network providing primary and secondary free legal aid, including for 
victims of crime. Nonetheless, the system of legal aid in criminal proceedings needs to be 
reformed to improve access to justice. Eligibility criteria in criminal proceedings for 
mandatory defence, defence of people unable to afford legal representation and 
representation of the interests of victims of crime need revision. The country is still not 
aligned with the Europ ean Protection Order Directive. The adoption of an amended Law on 
criminal procedure to ensure alignment with the Victims’ Rights Directive is still pending. 
Public awareness about compensation mechanisms and the role of the Commission for the 
Implementation of the Law on the payment of monetary compensation to victims of violent 
crimes remains insufficient. 
Protection of minorities 
The legal and institutional framework for the protection of persons belonging to minorities is 
in place and persons belonging to minorities are integrated into society. 
The government made progress toward finalising an Action Plan on Advancing the Rights of 
Persons belonging to Communities. The action plan accompanies the rule of law roadmap, 
required as part of North Macedonia’s opening benchmarks for accession negotiations. The 
action plan was developed with the assistance of Council of Europe experts in an inclusive 
process, with two sessions held to consult stakeholders, including representatives of 
minorities and communities, and relevant institutions. The National St rategy for Inter-
culturalism and Social Cohesion 2024 -2026 developed by the Agency for the Rights of the 
Non-Majority Communities is being implemented. The strategy serves as the basis for the 
new strategy for 2026 -2032, which is being developed by the Ministry of Inter -Community 
Relations. The Agency, which focuses its activities on the rights of smaller communities, has 
limited financial and human resources, hampering its activities. Closer cooperation between 
the Agency and the Ministry of Inter- Community Relations is needed to ensure a unified 
approach to upholding the rights of persons belonging to minorities. 
North Macedonia has not yet ratified the European Charter for Regional or Minority 
Languages. 
Roma 
The legal and institutional framework on Roma is incomplete; Roma people continue to face 
discrimination. 
North Macedonia has made limited progress on Roma inclusion, implementing the strategy 
for 2022- 2030. Action plans for 2026 are not yet in place. Interinstitutional cooperation 
remains at a low level. Outstanding challenges include irregular school attendance, street 
children and segregation in schools. Implementation of the priorities of the Poznan 
Declaration is lagging, and no dedicated action plans have been adopted. 
Roma women face systemic discrimination in employment, healthcare and education. Most 
work informally without social protection, and literacy and school dropout rates remain high, 
with only 2% of Roma children attending early education.

Economic governance 
Progress on key reforms remained mixed. The government adopted several bylaws to 
implement the 2022 Organic Budget Law (OBL) but postponed implementation of the new 
deficit rule (3% of GDP limit) from 2027 to 2028, and the full implementation of the OBL from

blocking related fiscal governance reforms. The Fiscal Council remains understaffed and 
underfinanced. The draft law on public -private partnerships is still to be adopted.  The 2025 
budget was revised,  shifting funds from capital to current spending. While the wage bill 
remained stable as a share of GDP, pension costs rose, with  ad hoc increases in September 
2025 and March 2026 instead of adjustments through indexation . Monetary policy remained 
focused on price stability. The Deposit Insurance Fund and Bank Resolution Fund are not yet 
operational. However, the central bank widened its toolkit for providing liquidity to the 
financial market and took steps to strengthen the supervisory framework. Under the  October 
2024 Reform Agenda, the Commission has released EUR  142.1 million as of June 2026, 
including the pre -financing. The country completed  22 of 136 planned steps  to boost the  
digital transformation, improve financial control, achieve better governance of state -owned 
enterprises, and improve the quality of education. Implementation of the policy guidance set 
out in the conclusions of the May 2025 Economic and Financial Dialogue has  been limited, 
with the level of implementation down on the previous year.

Functioning of product markets 
Business environment 
Long-standing obstacles to doing business persist. Competition is distorted by the large 
informal economy, which, according to some estimates, accounts for some 38% of GDP 8. 
Informal employment accounted for 14.5% of total employment in the second quarter of 
2026, according to the State Statistical Office, up by 0.3  pps year-on-year and by 1 pp. on the 
same period five years earlier. Implementation of the  Strategy and Action Plan for the 
Formalisation of the Informal Economy is heavily delayed, partly because of a reorganisation 
of responsibilities in the government. January 2026 saw the entry into force of the new Law 
on Employment , aimed at bringing informal workers into the formal economy through 
flexible contracts. Obstacles to lending to small and medium- sized businesses are the focus 
of several measures in the Reform Agenda, but their implementation is lagging9.

Product markets are still distorted by sizeable State aid. State aid schemes are numerous 
and opaque. The Commission for Protection of Competition still lacks the authority and 
resources to properly regulate state subsidies. The planned update of the State Aid Registry, 
which requires procurement of a management information system connecting different 
institutions, has now been pushed back to 2027.  Public procurement competition is weak, 
with two thirds of tenders awarded to single bidders, raising concerns over favouritism and 
inefficiency. The government has a track record of intervening in market competition 
through caps on  food prices  to contain inflation.  These measures revealed persistent

2.4. PUBLIC PROCUREMENT, STATISTICS; FINANCIAL CONTROL 
Chapter 5 - Public procurement 
EU rules ensure that the public procurement of goods, services and works in any Member State 
is transparent and open to all EU companies on the basis of non -discrimination and equal 
treatment. 
Institutional set-up and legal alignment 
North Macedonia’s legal framework  is largely aligned with the EU acquis. The current 
exclusion framework in public procurement, based on negative references for companies 
due to professional misconduct, remains in force: there are concerns about its 
proportionality and compliance with EU public procurement directives. In 2025, 74 negative 
references were issued; 56 expired, and 6 were removed. By the end of 2025, 12 negative 
references remained active. Controls in public procurement should be further strengthened. 
The current legal framework for public -private partnerships (PPP) is not aligned with the EU 
acquis, while related control systems and fiscal risks management remain very weak. The 
adoption of the law on PPP continues to face delays. In 2025, only one new PPP agreement 
was recorded in the register of awarded contracts. A comprehensive register needs to be 
established for PPP and for concessions. 
Implementation and enforcement capacity 
In 2025, the public procurement market  amounted to 9.3% of the country’s GDP, compared 
with 8.8% in 2024. Competition in public procurement has been limited, and reliance on the 
lowest-price award criterion has continued to dominate. The percentage of single bid 
tenders was 33.9% in 2025. The use of negotiated procedures dropped further to only 0.5% 
of contracts in 2025, while contract cancellations increased to 28.7%. In 2025, 647 contracts 
were concluded using the e- market for low value procurement, an increase from 325 
contracts in 2024. The use of e -auctions decreased to 22% of the published contract 
notices, well below the 40% target agreed in the Reform and Growth Facility. 
The use of the most economically advantageous tender (MEAT) criterion remained limited 
and was only applied for 6% of procurement contracts, up from 5.4% in 2024. In terms of 
value, only 4.8% of contracts utilised MEAT criteria. 
The electronic for public procurement system operates efficiently, covering all transactions. 
Capacity building for managing public procurement  processes requires further 
strengthening, particularly at the local level. 
The public procurement strategy includes measures to combat corruption  and conflict of 
interest, risk management systems and reporting on potential corrupt practices or 
irregularities. However, the framework in this area needs to be strengthened. The Public 
Procurement Bureau (PPB) regularly publishes red flag reports, although follow -up actions 
remain insufficient. 
Efficient remedy system 
The legislation on the remedy system is mostly harmonised with the EU acquis.

Chapter 18 - Statistics 
European statistics are developed, produced and disseminated by EU Member States, guided 
by the principles and quality criteria elaborated in the Regulation on European statistics, such 
as professional independence, impartiality, reliability, accessibility and statistical 
confidentiality. Common rules are provided for the methodology, production and dissemination 
of statistical information, which are further specified in sectoral legislation that constitute the 
EU acquis on statistics. 
The statistical infrastructure remains largely aligned with the EU acquis, and the statistical 
law is aligned with the principles of the European statistics Code of Practice. Despite some 
progress in addressing the long-standing challenging human resources situation at the State 
Statistical Office (SSO), the SSO continues to suffer from insufficient human and financial 
resources. Data transmission to Eurostat has continued to improve, notably in national 
accounts and social statistics. 
On classifications and registers, the national classification standard is aligned with NACE 
Rev. 2.1. 
As regards macroeconomic statistics , annual and quarterly national accounts series have 
been updated. Gross national income (GNI) is compiled based on ESA 2010 standards, with 
regular transmission of GNI and GDP data to Eurostat under the ESA 2010 transmission 
programme. Some progress has been achieved in compiling tax and social contribution data 
on an accrual basis, and in the experimental development and transmission of table 11 on 
government expenditure by function (COFOG). However, further improvements are required, 
particularly regarding the updating of the GNI inventory. The SSO should also resolve the 
sectorisation of units for the government sector, transmit quarterly GFS data for 2025, as 
well as data for the ESA tables 27, 28 and 28A, and remove flags that prevent the publication 
of GFS data, posing broader fiscal transparency challenges. Since December 2025, the 
National Bank transmits financial accounts data on transactions and balance sheets to 
Eurostat, but improvements are needed on the completeness of sector accounts and the 
reconciliation of financial flows with non-financial accounts. 
Business and trade statistics continue to be collected regularly, and further work is required 
to improve data collection and direct reporting on international trade in services and foreign 
direct investments.  
As regards social statistics, accuracy increased following a revision of intercensal 
population estimates based on the 2021 census results, and important improvements were 
made in labour market, European Union statistics on income and living conditions (EU -SILC) 
and European System of integrated Social Protection Statistics (ESSPRO) data transmission.  
Regarding agricultural statistics, preparatory work for the agricultural census is advancing 
but a slow legislative process risks leading to a postponement of the census from 2026 to 
2027. The 2026 state budget includes a dedicated allocation for the census, providing a 
sound financial basis for its execution. 
North Macedonia’s energy statistics  are highly compliant. Regarding environmental 
statistics, data are being transmitted to Eurostat for forestry and monetary and physical 
environmental accounts. Transport statistics are highly compliant, but North Macedonia has 
not yet transmitted passenger mobility data to Eurostat.

Chapter 32 - Financial control 
The EU promotes the reform of national governance systems to improve managerial 
accountability, sound financial management of income and expenditure, and external audit of 
public funds. The financial control rules further protect the EU’s financial interests against 
fraud in the management of EU funds and the euro against counterfeiting. 
Public internal financial control (PIFC) 
PIFC reforms are embedded in the 2025 -2028 Public Finance Management Reform 
Programme. The 2025 Annual Report on the functioning of PIFC 2025 was produced and 
delivered to the Government for approval. As for the legal framework , the implementation of 
the PIFC law progressed, including through the adoption of two pieces of implementing 
legislation by the end of 2025, while the remaining ones are still pending. Delays in the 
operationalisation of the revised budget classifications are slowing down the restructuring 
of internal audit units and the strengthening of managerial accountability systems. The 
central harmonisation unit continues to provide methodological guidance and coordinates 
the PIFC system across the public sector. Despite its limited capacities, systems are robust.   
The legal framework on internal control is broadly harmonised with international standards, 
but implementation is inconsistent, and risk management needs to be further strengthened. 
A new methodology to identify, report and monitor operational and strategic risks was 
introduced in September 2025 and 76% of central budget users developed risk management 
systems in line with it by the end of 2025. Development of implementing legislation for the 
financial inspection law progressed, while new working procedures and methodological 
tools are to be established in line with the Law on inspection supervision. 
Institutional and managerial accountability  remains fragile because of the very high number 
of first-level budget users and shortcomings in delegation of authority. Amendments to the 
organisational classification approved the reduction of the number of primary budget users 
to 35 but it has not been enforced yet. 
Fragmentation, inadequate staffing levels and the lack of a national certification system for 
internal auditors continue to undermine the capacity and quality of internal audit units 
(IAUs). Indeed, IAUs often operate with just one auditor or none, in a system with only 126 
internal auditors across the 116 IAUs established. 
External audit 
The lack of adoption by the government of the new state audit law prevents the legal 
framework from strengthening the State Audit Office’s (SAO) independence and increasing 
Parliament’s oversight role. The 2025 Annual Audit Report was submitted to Parliament and 
published in June 2026. The SAO fully implemented 2025 work plan, which included 57 
regularity audits, 9 performance audits, 6 compliance audits and 4 IT audits. Although the 
quality of the SAI’s audit work complies with INTOSAI standards and is widely recognised, 
administrative capacity  remains insufficient with a workforce of only 145 out of 245 
systematised posts and no additional recruitments are envisaged for 2026. Parliamentary 
follow-up of individual audit reports should be further strengthened. 
Protection of the EU’s financial interests 
The Anti-Fraud Coordination Service  (AFCOS) Network is formally established, and the 
government adopted in July 2026 the Anti -Fraud Strategy 2026- 2030 for the Protection of 
the Financial Interests of the European Union. The National legislation is largely aligned with 
the PIF Directive . Cooperation with the European Anti -Fraud Office (OLAF) is robust. 
However, sufficient staffing for AFCOS needs to be ensured as the AFCOS unit currently has 
only 3 employees.

National legislation is partially harmonised with the EU acquis . The system for tackling euro

North Macedonia  maintained its active engagement in a number of  regional cooperation 
initiatives such as the  Central European Free Trade Agreement (CEFTA), the Energy

(SEECP) and the Regional Cooperation Council (RCC). 
North Macedonia participated both in the sixth annual EU -Western Balkans Summit  in 
Brussels in December 2025 and the EU -Western Balkans Summit in Tivat in Montenegro in

measures to further advance gradual integration. 
North Macedonia has actively engaged in the regional dimension of the Growth Plan for the 
Western Balkans, participating in both ministerial and leaders’ meetings. 
On the Common Regional Market (CRM) , the Berlin process summit took place in London in 
October 2025. At the summit, the leaders of the region: (i) endorsed a revised and 
streamlined action plan to implement the Green Agenda for the Western Balkans; (ii) 
adopted a Western Balkans Climate Adaptation Roadmap; and (iii) committed to making 
progress on the promotion of regional mobility and implementation of relevant regional 
agreements and CEFTA decisions. 
North Macedonia adopted the  CRM action plan 2025- 2028 and is contributing to its 
implementation. 
North Macedonia  signed and ratified  the fifth mobility agreement  on access to higher 
education and admission to studies in the Western Balkans; it has therefore ratified five 
regional mobility agreements.  
The work of CEFTA gained impetus from the appointment of a new director and the 
commitment shown by the parties.  Substantial advancements have been made in specific 
areas, including Green Lanes, recognition of qualified trust services, recognition of advanced 
electronic signatures and advanced electronic seals, the establishment of the Advisory Body 
for Customs Cooperation and the adoption of the Risk Management Strategy 2025-2028. 
North Macedonia ratified Protocols 5 and 6  establishing a trade facilitation framework for 
customs cooperation and  the liberalisation  of trade in services. It also ratified

4. RESILIENCE, SECURITY, DEFENCE 
North Macedonia has a clear strategic orientation anchored in the EU accession process as 
a candidate country. It continues to align with the EU’s common foreign and security policy, 
including restrictive measures, and contributes significantly to EU crisis management 
operations and missions. 
Democratic resilience 
Hybrid threats remain a challenge to North Macedonia’s democratic institutions, particularly 
foreign information manipulation and interference (FIMI), including disinformation. North 
Macedonia has initiated, and to a degree put into operation, strategies for countering cyber 
and hybrid threats. However, there is no clear legal framework on FIMI. 
North Macedonia led global efforts against disinformation, being one of WB6 (and the 
world’s) first countries to include media literacy in primary and secondary school curricula in 
2018/19. Skopje hosted the second (2021) and latest (2025) edition of the EEAS -funded EU-
WB Media Literacy conference. 
Economic security and resilience 
North Macedonia provides opportunities for investment, supporting supply chain resilience. 
Key vulnerabilities include considerable dependence on energy imports and products such 
as digital and electronic products, exposure to regional economic conditions and the need 
for continued reforms and infrastructure development. Energy import dependency exceeds 
60%, reflecting limited domestic resources and exposure to external supply risks. The 
country relies on coal for much of its energy needs but is increasingl y diversifying its energy 
mix and increasing renewable energy capacities. The country has made remarkable strides 
in renewable energy, with green power now accounting for 58.22% of installed capacity. This 
shift is largely driven by solar and wind investments. 
North Macedonia’s critical mineral resources, including iron, nickel, copper, and zinc, are vital 
for the region’s industrial and manufacturing sectors. The country also produces lead and 
other essential minerals that support its growing mining industry. 
There is marked progress on the digital transformation. In 2025, several key legislative and 
infrastructure advancements brought the country closer to EU standards. Main challenges

=== PAGE 69 ===
68 
 
include shortages of specialised skills and data infrastructure needs. The country also 
strengthened its cybersecurity framework with the adoption of the Security of Network and 
Information Systems (NIS2)-aligned Act, effective from January 2026. 
The protection of critical infrastructure needs to be improved. Institutions with 
responsibilities in the protection of critical infrastructure need to increase capacity. North 
Macedonia has not aligned with the EU Directive on the resilience of critical entities. 
North Macedonia still needs to align with the Critical Raw Materials Regulation (CRM) and 
draft and adopt the necessary legislation to integrate with the EU’s CRM value chain. 
Defence capacity building and defence industrial dimension 
North Macedonia’s military expenditure reached 2% of GDP in 2025. The defence industry is 
relatively small and focused on supporting national security needs, maintenance, and limited 
defence production. 
The defence industry in North Macedonia is undergoing a significant transformation under 
its Army Modernisation Plan 2023 -2032. The country is focused on boosting its military 
capabilities, aligning with NATO standards, and improving operational readiness. Key 
priorities include the procurement of armoured vehicles, tactical communications, air 
defence systems, and training infrastructure. 
In terms of defence capacity building, the Army of North Macedonia has benefitted from EU 
support via three bilateral assistance measures under the European Peace Facility, an off -
budget instrument. The EU committed a total of EUR 38 million to date, with a view to 
strengthening operational effectiveness and interoperability, and modernising their military 
equipment. As one of the framework nations of the Balkans Medical Task Force, North 
Macedonia also benefits from the 2022 regional EPF assistance measure (EUR 6 million) 
which has strengthened the medical military capabilities of the Task Force.  
The country’s defence sector comprises one main dedicated defence manufacturer (ATS 
Group) and 44 companies registered as economic operators eligible for NATO -related 
procurement processes. 
Civil protection and disaster resilience 
North Macedonia is increasingly integrated into European civil protection and disaster 
preparedness structures, notably through its participation in the Union Civil Protection 
Mechanism (UCPM). It nevertheless needs to step up efforts to improve its civil protection 
and disaster preparedness capacities to strengthen its resilience and ensure it can deal 
effectively with natural and human-induced crises.

Chapter 1 - Free movement of goods 
The free movement of goods ensures that many products can be traded freely across the EU 
based on common rules and procedures. Where products are governed by national rules, the 
principle of the free movement of goods prevents these rules from creating unjustified barriers 
to trade. 
General principles 
The country has a functioning institutional framework for the marketing of industrial 
products. A new Strategy for free movement of goods and a new 2024 -2028 Action plan to 
eliminate non -tariff barriers to trade and ensure compliance with Articles 34 -36 TFEU still 
needs to be adopted. The capacity of the Ministry responsible for the legal alignment with 
the EU acquis in this area remains insufficient. The country is not yet aligned with the latest 
EU acquis on general product safety 
Harmonised area: quality infrastructure 
The necessary institutional and legal structure for technical regulations, standards, 
conformity assessment, accreditation, metrology and market surveillance are in place. The 
National Standardisation Institute has now adopted 25 230 European standards, 
representing over 99% of CEN/CENELEC standards. 
The Institute for  Accreditation ensures the accreditation procedures for testing, calibration 
and medical laboratories, conformity assessment bodies, and inspection bodies, and 
successfully completed its fifth peer evaluation by the European co -operation for 
Accreditation (EA) to maintain its status as an EA MLA signatory. There are 229 active 
conformity assessment bodies in the country. A new law on market surveillance  designed 
to align with the latest EU acquis  is yet to be adopted. The State Market Inspectorate (SMI) 
performed 1  933 market surveillance inspections in 2025 to mid -2026, resulting in 12 
products being withdrawn from the market. The human resource capacities of the SMI have 
strengthened, with 20 new employees. However, the SMI’s restructuring and digitalisation 
remains, as well as its operational independence, remains challenging.  
Harmonised area: sectoral legislation 
The level of alignment with the EU ‘new and global approach’ product acquis remains low, 
and no progress was made in this area. 
No progress was made on aligning with the latest EU ‘old approach’ product acquis,  
including on vehicles, on chemicals (including on registration, evaluation, authorisation and 
restriction of chemicals (REACH); chemical classification, labelling and packaging (CLP), 
and good laboratory practice (GLP)), on detergents or on firearms. There was no progress in 
aligning with EU procedural measures. The national legislation on returning cultural goods 
remains partly aligned with the relevant EU acquis. 
The recommendations from the national Strategy for the Prevention of Corruption related to 
market surveillance and product safety have not yet been addressed. 
Chapter 2 - Freedom of movement for workers 
Nationals of one Member State have the right to work in another Member State and must be 
given the same working and social conditions as other worker nationals of that Member State. 
On access to the labour market , there were no legislative developments in the reporting 
period. The Law on employment of foreigners does not distinguish between EU citizens and 
other foreigners. The quota of work permits is set annually (10  000 permits for 2026), and 
family reunification is allowed.

Chapter 3 - Right of establishment and freedom to provide services 
EU natural and legal persons have the right to establish themselves in any Member State and to 
provide cross -border services. For certain regulated professions, there are rules on mutual 
recognition of qualifications. EU rules on postal services focus on improving the quality of 
universal service, gradually opening the market to competition, and the establishment of an 
independent regulator. 
On the right of establishment and freedom to provide cross-border services, there has been 
no progress to align legislation with the EU Services Directive. The number of electronic 
services for citizens and businesses available on the e-services portal reached 336 and 1316 
institutions. To serve as ‘Single point of contact’ for services, the portal needs to be further 
improved to increase the number of e-services and the information available. 
In the area of postal services , the national legislation is partially aligned with EU postal 
acquis. The full postal market opening to competition was postponed until the country’s 
accession to the EU. There is one universal state- owned service provider and 28 active 
private postal operators. A plan for restructuring of the state -owned company ‘Post Office’ 
was drafted but not yet adopted. The Postal Agency is the independent regulatory authority 
ensuring compliance with the Law on postal services and has sufficient institutional and 
financial capacity to monitor and enforce the new postal market rules. 
On the mutual recognition of professional qualifications , the Ministry of Education and

Kosovo, Montenegro and Bosnia and Herzegov ina. However, the country needs to intensify 
efforts to fully align with the Directive on the recognition of professional qualifications, 
especially with the minimum training requirements for professions benefiting from 
automatic recognition, and to set up a transparent electronic register of all regulated

Chapter 4 - Free movement of capital 
In the EU, capital and investments must be able to move without restriction, with common rules 
for cross-border payments. Banks and other economic operators apply certain rules to support 
the fight against money laundering and terrorist financing. 
North Macedonia has achieved partial progress regarding capital movements. Nevertheless, 
as the National Land Consolidation Strategy 2025 -2035 fails to provide for the liberalisation 
of foreign acquisition rights, the broader capital account liberalisation agenda remains 
stagnant, leaving the European Commission’s concerns on residents’ accounts abroad and 
the restriction of foreign acquisition of agricultural land unaddressed. 
Concerning payments, North Macedonia became operationally ready for participation in the 
geographical scope of the SEPA schemes in October 2025, following the European 
Payments Council’s (EPC) confirmation that the domestic legal and operational framework 
met the requirements for participation in the SEPA schemes. Further progress was made in 
the field of instant payments in July 2026, as the National Bank signed the agreement with 
the EPC on the use of the SEPA Instant Credit Transfer Scheme, establishing the 
prerequisites f or aligning the national instant payment solution with European standards. 
Nevertheless, North Macedonia is yet to align with more recent EU payment services acquis  
developments, including the revised Payment Services Regulation/Payment Services 
Directive 3 (PSR/PSD3) package and full technical alignment of all PSD2 provisions beyond 
those required for adherence to the EPC scheme. 
Limited progress has been achieved regarding alignment with the EU acquis  on the fight 
against money laundering and terrorist financing (AML/CFT). In July 2026, a 
multidisciplinary working group was established to align national legislation with the new EU 
AML/CTF package, in particular Directive (EU) 2024/1640 and Regulation (EU) 2024/1624. 
Prepared under coordination by the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU), the National Strategy for 
Combating Money Laundering and Financing of Terrorism was adopted in March 2026.  
On anti-corruption measures, the Law on the prevention of money laundering and financing 
of terrorism is aligned with the acquis  and FATF recommendations are being effectively 
implemented, although further alignment with the new EU Anti-Money Laundering package is 
needed. The new National Strategy for Combating Money Laundering and Financing of 
Terrorism 2026-2029 was adopted in March 2026. 
Chapter 6 - Company law 
The EU has common rules on the formation, registration and disclosure requirements of a 
company, with complementary rules for accounting and financial reporting, and statutory audit. 
On company law and corporate governance , alignment with the Directive on company law is 
well advanced on the formation, registration and domestic mergers and divisions of 
companies. The Central Registry provides a solid and available one -stop shop system where 
company registration and filing of documents, – including financial documents, – can be 
carried out fully online. The Agency for Real Estate Cadastre provides a fully digital database 
easily accessible for users. 
A new Law on Public Enterprises and Trade Companies in State Ownership or with the 
Participation of the Republic of North Macedonia, which regulates the legal framework for 
more efficient, transparent and accountable management of state- owned enterprises was 
adopted. This law sets professional criteria are set for members of the supervisory and 
management bodies, including higher education, and also provides controls of operations 
and management for internal audits.

Chapter 7 - Intellectual property law 
The EU has, to a large extent, harmonised rules for the legal protection and enforcement of 
copyright and related rights, as well as of industrial property rights, including trademarks, 
designs, patents and related supplementary protection certificates (SPCs), and geographical 
indications (GIs), including craft and industrial GIs. 
On copyright and related rights, the new law on copyright aimed at aligning with the EU 
acquis on collective rights management, orphan works and the implementation of the 
Marrakesh Treaty still needs to be adopted. As regards the Directive on copyright and 
related rights applicable to certain online transmissions of broadcasting organisations and 
retransmissions of television and radio programmes (SatCab II Directive) and the Copyright 
in the Digital Single Market Directive (DSM), national legislation is not aligned with the EU 
acquis. However, partial alignment was achieved through amendments to the law by 
incorporating the provisions on “journalistic work”, thus aligning with Article 15 of the DSM 
Directive. The full alignment with Directives remains to be done. The relevant intellectual 
property rights unit in the Ministry of Culture is left without functional staff and its 
operations are frozen. 
On intellectual property rights, North Macedonia needs to further align its legislation with 
the EU acquis , in particular in the area of patents and trade secrets. Alignment with the 
Directive on IPR enforcement is still pending. The State Office for Industrial Property (SOIP) 
fully digitalised its database for patents and trademarks. SOIP continued cooperating with 
the EU and international bodies, in particular with the European Union Intellectual Property 
Office. 
In 2025, the Customs Administration in cooperation with the State Market Inspectorate 
identified and seized more than 145  000 goods suspected of infringing intellectual property 
rights across 84 cases, involving 34 different trademarks. Most of the counterfeit goods 
originated from Türkiye, China and the Middle East. 
Chapter 8 - Competition policy 
EU rules protect free competition. They include antitrust rules against restrictive agreements 
between companies and the abuse of a dominant position and also include rules on 
concentrations between companies which would significantly impede effective competition. 
EU rules also set out a system of State aid control. Governments are only allowed to grant 
State aid if restrictive conditions are met, with a view to preventing distortion of competition.

Chapter 9 - Financial services 
EU rules aim at ensuring fair competition among financial institutions, and the stability and

There were no developments on banking and financial conglomerates,  and the country

On securities markets and investment services, implementation of the Laws on Prospectus

regarding undertakings for collective investment in transferable securities and alternative

Chapter 28 - Consumer and health protection 
EU rules protect consumers’ economic interests and also ensure product safety, prevent 
dangerous imitations and establish liability for defective products. The EU also guarantees a 
high level of protection of public health and, in particular, also ensures high common standards 
for tobacco control, blood, tissues, cells and organs, and medicines for human and veterinary 
use. The EU also has rules  for upholding patients’ rights in cross -border healthcare and in 
preparing for and responding to cross-border health threats. 
Consumer protection 
While the country’s Law on consumer protection  was broadly aligned with most of the EU 
consumer acquis and clearly set out the responsibilities of physical and legal commercial 
bodies towards consumers, the EU acquis  in this area has evolved, including by the adoption 
of the EU acquis on the sale of goods, on digital content, and the right to repair, so these also 
require alignment. National legislation is also still not aligned with the EU acquis on package 
travel. The consumer protection strategy (2025- 2028) is still not adopted. A web platform 
providing information on consumer protection has not been developed. The national Law on 
mediation provides mediation process in consumer disputes, however access to the 
process remains difficult and complicated for consumers. 
On general product safety there is a  high level of alignment with the EU acquis , apart from 
some definitions. National legislation remains partly aligned with the legal requirements on 
the EU rapid alert system for dangerous non-food products (Safety Gate). 
The State Market Inspectorate (SMI) faced increased workload as a result of government 
decisions. In the reporting period, the SMI executed a total of 4  340 inspections based on 
the Law on consumer protection and issued a total of 170 decisions based on identified 
violations. A total of 12 products were withdrawn from the market due to safety issues.  
On non-safety related issues, further efforts are needed to develop guidance and 
procedures on consumer appeals and on compensation. 
Public health 
The slight increase in financing of public healthcare  in North Macedonia continued in 2025 
but remains far below the EU average. Universal health coverage is offered free of charge 
through primary healthcare, but patients have to pay for some hospital care, specialist 
outpatient visits and medicines. The scope of health services and their availability, in 
particular of specialist medical services, and their geographical distribution is inadequate. 
The ratio of nurses per 100 000 inhabitants still stands below the EU average. Medical staff 
continue to leave th e public health sector to work in the private sector or abroad. The 
healthcare system is heavily reliant on private funding through out- of-pocket payments (up 
to 42%) and voluntary insurance. This represents a barrier to equal access to healthcare. 
The national e -health system collects data only from the public healthcare, with missing 
data from private health facilities to complete patients’ files. The e -health application is 
functional, though improvements in the appointment system are needed.  
On tobacco control,  North Macedonia should adopt a new law to further align with the EU 
acquis on Tobacco Products. Its implementation and enforcement will remain a challenge, 
as smoking continued to be tolerated and unsanctioned in closed areas, including public 
offices, some medical facilities and restaurants.

Chapter 10 - Digital transformation and media 
The EU supports the smooth functioning of the internal market for electronic communications, 
electronic commerce and audio -visual services. The rules protect consumers and support the 
universal availability of modern services. 
On digital services, North Macedonia is partially aligned with the EU acquis . In line with the 
long-term ICT Strategy and its Action Plan, the Ministry of Digital Transformation advanced

Chapter 16 - Taxation 
EU rules on taxation cover value added tax, excise duties and aspects of corporate taxation. 
They also deal with cooperation between tax administrations, including the exchange of 
information to prevent tax evasion. 
On indirect taxation, the Law on VAT was amended  abolishing the threshold of EUR 22 for 
imports of small parcels free of VAT, effectively introducing a rate of 18% on all imports. 
Government took several decisions temporarily reducing the VAT rate on fuels from 18% to 
10%, as part of the anti -crisis measures to suppress the effects of the fuel crisis. The latest 
measure expires at the end of September 2026. The excise on diesel and unleaded fuels 
was reduced, with an effect in the period April -  July 2026. Delays in the VAT refund process 
remain quite prominent, as only 14.3% of the refund was paid within the legal deadline. The 
Public Revenue Office (PRO) launched an e -invoice pilot phase, enabling all non -cash 
transactions to be registered in real time. 
As regards direct taxation , a new Law on minimum global corporate profit tax is in force 
since 1 January 2026, introducing a profit tax rate of 15% for domestic companies which are 
subsidiaries of multinational companies, thus bringing it into line with OECD global minimum 
tax (GMT) and Directive (EU) 2022/2523. The country still needs to align its legislation with 
the Merger Directive. 
On administrative cooperation and mutual assistance , North Macedonia has signed 49 
double taxation agreements, including with 23 EU Member States except Cyprus, Malta, 
Portugal and Greece. The country has not yet committed to a date to start the automatic 
exchange of financial account information as per the OECD standard, although technical 
cooperation and exchange of information is ongoing. The country continued to implement 
the Inclusive Framework on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) minimum standards. 
On operational capacity and digitalisation , the Public Revenue Office  (PRO) improved its 
strategic planning, but still needs to improve its business processes, develop compliance 
risk management and further expand e- services. As regards administrative capacity, 55 
employees were hired in 2026 on temporary contracts. However, additional recruitments are 
necessary in view of the Office’s expanding tasks. The PRO still operates with a fragmented 
IT system. Progress with procurement of a new integrated e-PRO tax system remains slow.

Chapter 26 - Education and culture 
The EU supports cooperation in education and culture through funding programmes and the 
coordination of Member State policy through the ‘open method of coordination’. The EU and the

The 2026- 2032 Strategy for education , adopted in March 2026 is the main document

Chapter 29 - Customs union 
All Member States are part of the EU customs union and follow the same customs rules and 
procedures. This requires legislative alignment as well as adequate implementing and 
enforcement capacity and access to the common computerised customs systems. 
The customs legislation is highly aligned with the EU acquis. The Customs Tariff for 2026 
was adopted, aligned with the EU combined nomenclature. 
On administrative and operational capacity, the Development Strategy of the Customs 
Administration for the period 2025 –2027 continued to be implemented. A comprehensive 
intelligence system in support of investigation and enforcement is in place. Controls are 
based upon intelligence -led risk analysis, pre -arrival information and selectivity techniques. 
A new risk management system was introduced, as well as a customs data warehouse. The 
percentage of physical controls -red channel increased to 13.5% (5.5% in 2024), and 
documentary controls accounted for 9.5% (17.4% in 2024) of all processed customs 
declarations in 2025. The Strategy on Integrity and Anti-Corruption for the period 2024–2027 
continued to be implemented. The administrative capacities of the controls and 
investigations sector were significantly strengthened with financial support from EU funds. 
The support covered the supply of vehicles for customs mobile units and personal 
protection equipment for customs officers, and a new IT hardware infrastructure for the 
main server room. This improved the daily operations and IT security of customs operations 
and ensured business continuity. 
Total revenues collected by the Customs Administration in 2025 were 5.2% higher compared 
with 2024, exceeding EUR 2.1 billion. Maintenance and further development of the 
Integrated Tariff Environment (ITE) system was secured, and the pilot phase of the 
Economic Operator Registration and Identification (EORI) and Uniform User Management 
and Digital Signature (UUM&DS) systems was launched. The Customs Administration has 
put in place regular data exchange with a number of state institutions through the 
interoperability platform. 
The Customs Administration participated in five international operations in 2025 and 
continued its cooperation and exchange of intelligence with the customs agencies from the 
region and with international and regional organisations.

March 2026, the National Plan for the Sustainable Use of Pesticides was adopted and 
integrated pest management activities have been initiated in two pilot regions. The adoption 
of implementing legislation on the sustainable use of pesticides is still pending. Significant 
efforts are still needed to collect reliable data on national pest icide use and its effects on 
human health. 
On the marketing of seed and other plant reproductive material , the country should focus 
on aligning with the EU acquis on the rules plant and forest reproductive material (PRM and 
FRM), keeping in mind the need to start setting up the necessary institutional frameworks 
and technical capacities to implement and enforce functioning certification systems for 
PRM and FRM. 
The legislation on genetically modified organisms is yet to be fully aligned with the EU 
acquis. 
On the fight against corruption , North Macedonia should strengthen oversight and integrity 
mechanisms within the Food and Veterinary Agency and the Phytosanitary Directorate, and 
ensure that all inspection activities –  including controls at border control posts – are 
conducted on a risk-based basis and subject to independent audit. 
Chapter 13 - Fisheries and aquaculture 
The common fisheries policy lays down rules on fisheries management, protects living 
resources of the sea and limits the environmental impact of fisheries. This includes setting 
catch quotas, managing fleet capacity, rules on control and inspection, rules on markets and 
aquaculture and support for fisheries and coastal communities. Further, it promotes a 
sustainable aquaculture. 
As a landlocked country, North Macedonia does not apply much of the EU legislation on 
marine fisheries; nevertheless, it is required to align with the relevant parts of the EU acquis 
on inland fisheries, aquaculture, control measures, data collection, structural actions, State 
aid and market policy. 
Implementing legislation related to the Law on Fisheries remains delayed. In February 2026, 
the Constitutional Court annulled a provision on the cancellation of procedures for awarding 
fishing rights due to insufficient safeguards against arbitrary decisions. The Department for 
Fisheries and Aquaculture within the Ministry for Agriculture, Forestry and Water Economy 
remains understaffed. 
On resource management , North Macedonia adopted a National Eel Management Plan 
establishing conservation and management measures for the European eel in the Crn Drim 
river basin, including Ohrid and Prespa Lakes, with measures to reduce human -induced

Data collection continued, although further alignment with the EU Data Collection

In the area of  inspection and control, enforcement activities increased during the reporting

Chapter 22 - Regional policy and coordination of structural instruments 
Regional policy is the EU’s main tool for reducing regional disparities and investing in 
sustainable and inclusive socio-economic growth. It is operating through ‘shared management’ 
between the Commission and EU Member States. The implementation of cohesion policy 
programmes requires appropriate administrative capacity on programme and project level, the 
establishment of systems of sound financial management and control and the fulfilment of 
other EU acquis elements such as environmental or public procurement legislation. 
On legislative framework , the amendment to the law on inter- municipal cooperation that 
could facilitate transnational cooperation between the countries and local authorities within 
the EU territorial cooperation (EGTC) initiatives are pending adoption by parliament. 
The institutional framework for the management of IPA III reflects the specific EU 
requirements, aligning with the principles of the EU cohesion policy. Nevertheless, operating 
structures established for the indirect management of IPA programmes continue to function 
under limited administrative capacity and have resulted in a significant loss of EU funding 
under IPA II (decommitments). The institutional setting for implementation, coordination, 
management, audit and control of the Reform Agenda would benefit from reinforcement.  
Weaknesses in the Ministry of Finance’s role in costing and budgeting reforms continue to 
constrain the effective implementation of reforms, underscoring the need for stronger 
institutional capacity and processes. Still, North Macedonia made significant progress in

Weaknesses persist in the administrative capacity of key institutions managing EU funds.

Insufficient administrative capacity has contributed to significant losses of EU funding in the

On monitoring and evaluation, North Macedonia participates in sector monitoring 
committees, although follow -up on their conclusions remains limited. Reform and Growth

delayed institutional inputs, and inadequate reporting continues to undermine the 
implementation of EU assistance, underscoring the need for stronger political commitment 
to improve IPA fund absorption and Reform Agenda delivery. 
Financial management and control systems are in place but continue to operate with limited

bodies’ implementation of audit findings remains very limited. 
On the fight against corruption , the Anti-Fraud Coordination Service Network is in place and 
fully operational, but its capacity must be further strengthened.

Chapter 33 - Financial and budgetary provisions 
This chapter covers the rules governing the funding of the EU budget (‘own resources’). These 
resources mainly consist of: (i) contributions based on the gross national income of each 
Member State; (ii) customs duties; (iii) the non -recycled plastic resource and (iv) a resource 
based on value added tax. Member States must have the appropriate administrative capacity 
to adequately coordinate and ensure the correct calculation, collection, payment and control of 
own resources. 
There was some progress on the underlying policy areas that indirectly affect the own 
resources system (for progress on these areas, see Chapters 16 – Taxation, 17- Economic 
and monetary policy; 18 – Statistics, 29 – Customs union, and 32 – Financial control). 
The administrative infrastructure  to ensure the timely and efficient functioning of the 
process of calculation, collection, payment and control of own resources as well as to 
improve reporting and coordination mechanisms among all bodies involved remains 
underdeveloped. Several tasks are still pending, including the development of guidelines for 
the application of EU Traditional Own Resources, the establishment of an IT system for 
managing A and B accounts and the enabling of access to the OWNRES electronic system 
for reporting fraud and irregularities related to traditional own resources. 
Customs legislation is well aligned with the EU acquis . The customs tariff for 2026 has been 
fully harmonised with the EU combined nomenclature. A comprehensive intelligence system 
has been implemented in support of investigation and enforcement, and a new risk 
management system was introduced, as well as a customs data warehouse. Additionally, 
the percentage of physical controls (red channel) was increased. Total revenues collected 
by the Customs Administration in 2025 increased by 5.2% compared with 2024, exceeding 
EUR 2.1 billion. 
On indirect taxation, the Law on VAT was amended abolishing the threshold of EUR  22 for 
imports of small parcels free of VAT, effectively introducing a rate of 18% on all imports. 
Persistent delays in the VAT refund process remain a concern, with only 14.3% of the 
refunds paid within the legal deadline. The Public Revenue Office has maintained its efforts 
to implement the Integrated Tax Information System (ITIS), aimed at digitalising business 
processes and improving the monitoring of tax collection. (See Chapter 16 – Taxation for 
more information.) 
Some progress has been achieved in strengthening public internal financial control (PIFC ), 
particularly through the adoption of legislation implementing the PIFC law and the 
development of a new methodology to strengthen risk management. Nevertheless, internal 
controls require further improvement to ensure the effective safeguarding of public funds.

Member States must be able to conduct political dialogues in the framework of foreign,

ANNEX I – RELATIONS BETWEEN THE EU AND NORTH MACEDONIA 
North Macedonia has been a candidate country since 2005. On 26 March 2020, the 
European Council endorsed the Council’s decision to open accession negotiations with 
North Macedonia. The first Intergovernmental Conference took place on 19  July 2022, 
following the Council’s approval of the negotiating framework. The Commission launched 
the screening process on the same day. The screening was completed in December 2023 
and all screening reports on the six clusters were presented to the Council. 
North Macedonia has been participating in the Stabilisation and Association process since 
1999. The Stabilisation and Association Agreement (SAA) with the EU, signed in 2001 and in 
force since 2004, sets the framework for relations with the EU, including political, economic 
and technical dialogues. Experts met in seven subcommittees and one special group, 
according to schedule. The Stabilisation and Association Council took place in March 2023 
in Skopje. 
The EU is North Macedonia’s main trading partner. In 2025, total EU -North Macedonia trade 
in goods reached EUR  12.9 billion, with North Macedonia exporting EUR 6.5 billion to the EU 
and importing EUR  6.4 billion, resulting in a slight trade surplus. The EU accounts for 
approximately 77% of North Macedonia’s total exports and 49% of its imports. The country’s 
main export products are catalytic converters, wiring sets for vehicles, car seats, iron and 
steel and tobacco. 
North Macedonia is also a beneficiary of the EU’s autonomous trade measures (ATMs) in 
favour of the Western Balkans. 
Visa liberalisation for citizens of North Macedonia travelling to the Schengen area has been 
in force since December 2009. A readmission agreement has been in force since 2008. The 
Commission’s eighth report under the visa suspension mechanism 21 concluded that North 
Macedonia continues to meet the visa liberalisation benchmarks. 
North Macedonia has maintained its full alignment with EU common foreign and security 
policy (CFSP) over the reporting period. Substantial participation in EU crisis management 
missions and operations continued, notably in the EUFOR ALTHEA operations in Bosnia and 
Herzegovina and the EU Battlegroup HELBROC. 
A Security and Defence Partnership  between North Macedonia and the EU was signed in 
November 2024 and the EU -North Macedonia Security and Defence Dialogue was launched 
in Skopje in May 2025, with a second edition held in Brussels in May 2026. 
The EU has supported the Army of North Macedonia via three bilateral assistance measures 
under the European Peace Facility worth a total of EUR  38 million by April 2025, with a view 
to strengthening their military equipment, operational effectiveness and mobility. The 
Balkans Medical Task Force, in which North Macedonia participates, benefits from a EUR  6 
million 2022 support measure under the European Peace Facility. 
Under the Instrument for Pre -accession Assistance 2021- 2027 (IPA III) , EUR 706.5 million 
were allocated for the period 2021- 2027, supporting key areas such as rule of law, good
