1. INTRODUCTION1 
1.1. CONTEXT 
During the reporting period, Albania opened the final remaining cluster of negotiations, met 
the interim benchmarks under cluster 1 - fundamentals and provisionally closed four 
chapters. Albania continued to implement the Stabilisation and Association Agreement, and 
meetings of the joint bodies under the agreement took place.  
The Albanian authorities maintained their firm commitment to the strategic goal of EU 
integration as the country’s key priority, with the aim of closing all chapters of negotiations 
by the end of 2027. Keeping up the momentum will require swift delivery on high quality EU-
related reforms.  
The largest civic mobilisation since Albania’s democratic transition has been taking place in 
Tirana since May 2026 with daily protests which started in opposition to the planned 
development of a luxury tourism resort in the protected area of Pishë Poro – Narta. Building 
on the civic movement, student- led protests were organised in October 2026, during which 
violent incidents took place. 
Albania has actively engaged and started delivering on the implementation of the new 
Growth Plan for the Western Balkans across the four pillars. Under the Reform and Growth 
Facility (RGF), a performance-based EU instrument supporting the EU accession process, the 
plan is in line with the country’s reform agenda, which was adopted in late 2024. Up to over 
EUR 920 million in grants and loans is available to Albania under the RGF by the end of this 
period.  Thus far the Commission concluded that Albania has completed 33 of the 66 steps 
due until December 2025. Albania also made significant progress on most other steps.  
The Commission’s 2026 rule of law report2 covered Albania for the third time.  
 
1.2. MAIN FINDINGS OF THE REPORT 
CLUSTER 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS OF THE ACCESSION 
Democracy  
The legal and institutional framework regarding elections  is generally adequate. However, 
several longstanding recommendations from the Organization for Security and Co -operation 
in Europe (OSCE) / Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR) and the 
Venice Commission remain unaddressed. A comprehensive electoral reform is urgently 
needed, in particular ahead of the 2027 municipal elections.  
Daily protests have been taking place since May 2026. The protests were triggered by 
environmental concerns around construction works in the protected landscape of Pishë 
Poro – Narta and developed into a broader movement calling for transparency, 
accountability, anti -corruption efforts and good governance. This is the largest civic

Public administration reform 
Albania remains moderately prepared on public administration reform. Some progress was 
made, especially with the adoption of the amendments to the Law on the organisation and 
functioning of the State Administration. However, the government continued to adopt 
budget revisions through normative acts, without parliamentary scrutiny . Public 
consultations were not conducted efficiently on a regular basis or followed by appropriate 
follow-up actions. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
mostly valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ improve the quality of Regulatory Impact Assessments (RIAs) and the efficiency of 
public consultation processes and extend their use to implementing legislation, 
especially where implementing legislation addresses key substantive elements of the 
regulatory framework;  
→ improve fiscal discipline as well as budgetary oversight by limiting the use of normative 
acts by the executive for budget revisions, and reinstituting regular budget revision 
processes;  
→ adopt and implement a new legislative framework for the civil service, which includes 
revised rules on merit-based recruitment, promotion and dismissal in the civil service law 
at all levels, especially senior level, and begin automating the payroll system again;

Chapter 23: Judiciary and fundamental rights  
Albania is between a moderate and a good level of preparation  in the area of judiciary and 
fundamental rights and has made good progress during the reporting period. 
Functioning of the judiciary  
Albania is between a moderate and a good level of preparation  and has made good 
progress on the functioning of the judiciary. Albania continued to implement the 2016 
justice reform. The vetting process for all judges and prosecutors was fully completed 
following the finalisation of the appeal phase , further strengthening independence 
accountability and integrity in the justice sector. Rules on the appointment of non-magistrate 
members to the High Judicial Council (HJC) and High Prosecutorial Council (HPC) were 
aligned with those applicable to magistrate members regarding asset and background 
verifications. The new Chief Special Prosecutor at the Special Prosecution’s Office was 
appointed through a competitive and transparent process. Challenges remain in conducting 
timely and qualitative evaluations of magistrates.  
The lack of human resources, including magistrates as well as legal and administrative 
support staff, continues to affect the quality of justice. Attempts by public officials or 
politicians to exert undue influence on the judicial system, including on SPAK, and personal 
attacks on magistrates continued during the reporting period. While the backlog of cases 
has been partly reduced, pressing issues on judicial efficiency, quality and integrity – 
including court infrastructure, security and the delayed establishment of an electronic case 
management system – remain to be addressed.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and some 
remain valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ further strengthen accountability and reduce political interference on the judiciary and 
prosecution services, including by enhancing whistleblower protection within judicial 
structures, ensuring merit-based and transparent appointments, promotions, evaluations 
and inspections, in line with the high standards set by the vetting process, and filling 
magistrate-inspector vacancies at the High Justice Inspectorate; 
→ improve the quality of justice, in particular by continuing to fill vacancies and ensuring 
enough administrative court staff at all levels of the judiciary, increasing the total 
number of magistrates and legal advisers towards the EU average, and finalising the 
revision of the initial and continuous training curricula and entrance examination for the 
School of Magistrates and improving the school’s capacity to deliver judicial training; 
→ improve judicial efficiency, including by adopting further measures to increase efficiency 
at all levels, and further reduce backlog, in line with the Roadmap for Judicial Efficiency, 
the principles of justice reform and European standards , ensuring the swift 
implementation of the integrated case management system and expanding the online 
notification system to all courts of general jurisdiction. 
Fight against corruption  
Albania is between a moderate and a good level of preparation  and has shown good 
progress overall. The 2024- 2030 Anti-Corruption Strategy and its 2024-2026 action plan are 
being implemented. The Specialised Structure Against Organised Crime and Corruption 
(SPAK) has continued to demonstrate independence and deliver positive results in fighting 
high-level corruption, making tangible progress towards a solid track record in the fight 
against corruption. However, corruption remains a real challenge. Legislation  to prevent 
corruption was further aligned with European standards, including on conflicts of interest

Fundamental rights  
Albania’s legal framework sets out a generally good basis for the protection of fundamental 
rights. Progress was made with the adoption of the new gender equality and alternative care 
laws, amendments to the Law on the prevention and protection from violence against 
women and domestic violence, the remaining pieces of implementing legislation to the Law 
on personal data protection. Amendments to the Law on protection from discrimination , 
were also adopted, although full alignment with the acquis  is still expected. The pace of 
progress in advancing prison reform has not matched the ambition required and the 
conditions within correctional institutions continue to warrant careful attention. The 
enjoyment of the right to property and of the rights of persons belonging to minorities need 
to improve. An overall increase in implementation efforts is needed.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
mostly valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ further ensure effective implementation of the legal framework on the rights of persons 
belonging to minorities and further increase the capacity and resources of the State 
Committee on National Minorities and of local authorities; 
→ further secure the right to property by concluding first- time land registrations and 
digitisation of ownership titles and cadastral maps; improving systematically the quality 
and accuracy of cadastral data; further enhancing integrity, transparency, and 
effectiveness in delivering cadastral services; accelerating judicial cases for determining 
land ownerships, and finalising the process of compensations for properties confiscated 
during the communist regime; 
→ implement the recommendations of international monitoring bodies, including of the 
European Committee for the Prevention of Torture and Inhuman or Degrading Treatment 
or Punishment (CPT); ensure effective implementation of the Law on personal data

Freedom of expression and media freedom 
Albania is between having some and a moderate level of preparation  in the area of freedom 
of expression and made some progress during the reporting period, especially as regards 
aligning its legislative framework with the EU acquis and European standards.  Albania 
provides insufficient legal guarantees on the freedom of expression while the enabling 
environment needs to improve for media to operate freely, independently and professionally. 
Albania exempted registered journalists from criminal provisions on defamation under 
certain conditions, though full decriminalisation of defamation is yet to be achieved. Media 
independence and pluralism continue to be affected by economic penetration of the media 
market by business groups with political links and the overlap of political, business and 
other interests, the lack of transparency of financing sources, a high concentration of media 
ownership, as well as intimidation and smear campaigns, strategic lawsuits against public 
participation (SLAPPs), and the precarious working conditions for journalists. Concerns 
continued to be voiced about political influence impacting appointments of both the media 
regulatory authority and the public broadcaster. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ adopt further reforms of the legal and regulatory framework through an inclusive and 
transparent process to: (i) fully implement measures to ensure transparency of media 
ownership, strengthen media plurality and editorial independence; (ii) strengthen 
transparency of media financing from private and state resources; (iii) strengthen the 
autonomy of the media regulator and the independence of the state broadcaster; (iv) 
fully repeal criminal provisions on insult and defamation; (v) align civil aspects of 
defamation with European standards; and (vi) further ensure fair and transparent 
allocation of state advertising and strengthen access to public information; 
→ further ensure zero tolerance for intimidation, threats and smear campaigns, and provide 
effective protections against strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPP)  and  
enforcement in cases of attacks against journalists, including judicial follow- up; further 
increase capacities for law enforcement bodies to handle cases of violence, including on 
the margins of protests, and other criminal cases involving journalists, notably by 
ensuring high human rights compliance in handling incidents involving journalists, 
through binding instructions, disciplinary, administrative and/or judicial procedures, data 
collection and capacity building measures; 
→ improve Albanian journalists’ working conditions, in particular by strengthening the 
protection of journalists’ employment and social rights and ensuring that the Labour 
Code is applied consistently.

Fight against serious and organised crime 
Albania is between a moderate and a good level of preparation and has made  good 
progress. The capacity and effectiveness of the prosecution system (both specialised and 
general, including at district level) to investigate criminal groups and its use of financial 
investigations continued to develop. The fight against money laundering and the seizure of 
assets linked to organised crime were strengthened. SPAK has also further consolidated 
cooperation with its international counterparts, in particular with EU Member States and EU 
agencies, to investigate high- risk criminal groups with initial tangible results. Albania 
reinforced its capacities and cooperation with Member States on cybercrime. Albania also 
adopted amendments to its Criminal Code , further aligning with the EU acquis  on the 
criminalisation of money laundering, trafficking in human beings, online and offline child 
sexual abuse and exploitation, as well as drug trafficking and combating terrorism.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ establish a solid track record of investigations, prosecutions and final convictions in all 
fields of serious and organised crime, including drug trafficking, money laundering and 
terrorist financing through continued international cooperation, reinforced domestic 
inter-agency cooperation and methodologies to tackle complex criminal cases, and 
improved criminal and financial analysis capacity, while ensuring comprehensive 
alignment with the EU acquis;  
→ improve the efficiency of the asset recovery and management system by strengthening  
operational cooperation of the asset recovery office notably with asset recovery offices 
in EU Member States, by improving the use of legal instruments to confiscate proceeds 
of crime and property derived from criminal activities, and by better managing frozen 
and confiscated property also for social reuse;  
→ prioritise the reinforcement of the district prosecution offices by investing in human and 
technical resources; guaranteeing access to relevant databases and information 
systems and streamlining inter- agency cooperation to enable joint investigative task 
forces.

ECONOMIC CRITERIA 
The existence of a functioning market economy 
Albania has a good level of preparation  in developing a functioning market economy and 
made some progress in this area. The economy maintained strong growth momentum in 
2025 thanks to robust domestic demand and tourism. Sustained growth in tourism helped 
bring down the current account deficit to a historic low in 2025. Inflation remained below the 
central bank's target amid the continued appreciation of the lek. The fiscal deficit widened 
but stayed below target, and the public debt ratio continued to decline. Despite recent 
increases, government revenue as a share of GDP remains low, while spending on public 
investment is repeatedly under-executed. T he labour market situation continued to improve, 
but structural problems, such as emigration, persist. The banking sector remained sound, 
while lending to the private sector increased. The business environment remains affected by 
a weak rule of law, limited access to finance for small- and medium-sized enterprises, and 
shortcomings in the oversight and governance of state-owned enterprises.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were  partly addressed and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ advance the implementation of the Medium -Term Revenue Strategy, finalise the fiscal 
cadastre and accelerate the adoption of the property tax law; 
→ strengthen fiscal risk management and transparency, including regarding risks arising 
from state-owned enterprises, public-private partnerships, population ageing and climate 
change; 
→ ensure the effective functioning of the National Single Project Pipeline, including the 
integration of public- private partnerships, and reinforce project appraisal and 
prioritisation to ensure effective implementation of public investment projects. 
The capacity to cope with competitive pressure and market forces within the EU 
Albania has  between some and a moderate level of preparation  to cope with competitive 
pressure and market forces within the EU and made some progress  in this area. Despite 
efforts to reduce barriers in accessing education for children with disabilities and those from 
disadvantaged backgrounds , the education system still faces numerous challenges. Skills 
gaps persist, and the youth NEET rate (those not in education, employment or training) 
remains a concern. Transport and digital infrastructure improved, but infrastructure gaps 
persist. Albania continues to benefit from strong tourism growth, but industry and 
agriculture remain low- productivity sectors that have been contracting. Manufacturing 
exports fell for the third consecutive year, reflecting weak external demand, lek appreciation 
and businesses' limited pricing power. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and 
therefore remain mostly valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ expand and improve the dual VET system to increase the number of graduates and 
better align skills with labour market needs, while improving the quality of the broader 
education system; 
→ implement the plan to increase public research and development (R&D) expenditure 
towards the 2030 target of 1% of GDP and strengthen links between businesses and 
research institutions to develop a more dynamic innovation ecosystem; 
→ accelerate the implementation of transport and energy infrastructure projects.

Chapter 5: Public procurement 
Albania has between a moderate and a good level of preparation in public procurement. 
Some progress was made during the reporting period, especially through the adoption of the 
law on concessions and public private partnerships (PPPs) aligning the legal framework in 
this area with the EU acquis. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain

→ continue efforts to improve competitive procurement procedures and ensure integrity of 
the procurement function; 
→ complete outstanding alignment gaps in the legislative framework with the EU acquis in 
the areas of public procurement and defence and security procurement; 
→ ensure compliance with the EU acquis  and the Stabilisation and Association Agreement

Chapter 18: Statistics 
Albania is moderately prepared in the area of statistics. Some progress was made in 
improving the compilation of macroeconomic statistics in line with the European System of 
National and Regional Accounts (ESA 2010).  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ continue increasing the production and dissemination of timely and high -quality data in 
accordance with the EU acquis in statistics especially in the areas covered by the closing 
benchmarks; 
→ conduct the agricultural census in line with the relevant EU acquis; 
→ ensure adequate staffing at the Institute for Statistics (INSTAT) as well as progress on 
supporting IT systems and data security. 
Chapter 32: Financial control 
Albania is moderately prepared in the area of financial control. Some progress was made by 
re-establishing the subcommittee on Public Audit as a mechanism for parliamentary 
scrutiny over external audit matters.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
mostly valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ enhance the delegation of financial and management responsibilities, notably by 
entrusting routine administrative approvals to lower levels, clarifying accountability lines 
and ensuring that no subordinate unit is accountable to more than one superior unit, 
upgrade irregularity and fraud management; 
→ ensure parliamentary scrutiny through the subcommittee on Public Audit, which has to 
systematically and effectively conduct hearings, issue recommendations for follow-up 
and monitor their implementation, with the aim of increasing their level of 
implementation; 
→ fully align with the PIF Directive, strengthen controls and consolidate capacities to 
protect the EU’s financial interests, ensure appropriate reporting of irregularities and 
continue implementing both the anti -fraud strategy and action plans agreed with the 
European Commission to follow up investigations.

CLUSTER 2: INTERNAL MARKET 
Chapter 1: Free movement of goods 
Albania has between some and a moderate level of preparation o n free movement of 
goods. No progress on alignment with the EU acquis was achieved in the reporting period. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were not fully implemented and remain 
valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ complete the alignment of its legislation with the relevant EU acquis on quality 
infrastructure, i.e. accreditation, conformity assessment and market surveillance; 
→ significantly strengthen the enforcement capacity of the market surveillance 
inspectorate; 
→ finalise and adopt an Action Plan to comply with Articles 34 -36 of the Treaty on the 
Functioning of the European Union ( TFEU), fully covering the non- harmonised area, with 
a timeframe for screening all legislation and practices, introducing mutual recognition 
clauses, and repealing or amending legislation and practices as necessary.  
Chapter 2: Freedom of movement for workers 
Albania is moderately prepared on freedom of movement for workers. Some progress was 
achieved by negotiating and concluding further bilateral agreements on social security 
coordination with EU Member States and further advancing the preparations to connect 
Albania’s employment services system to the European network of employment services 
(EURES).  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and 
therefore remain mostly valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ continue alignment of national legislation with the EU acquis  on free movement of 
workers, including access for EU citizens to the labour market and equal treatment of 
EU workers; continue the enforcement of the Law on foreigners as per the 2024 -2030 
National Migration Strategy and start the preparation of its new action plan;  
→ continue developing the employment service’s IT system, allowing its connection to 
EURES upon accession; work to ensure technical and organisational preparedness, 
administrative and financial capacities and resources as well as development of IT 
infrastructure, systems and relevant databases for full participation in the Electronic 
Exchange of Social Security Information (EESSI) system and the European Health 
Insurance Card scheme.  
→ conclude additional bilateral agreements on social security coordination with EU 
Member States.  
Chapter 3: Right of establishment and freedom to provide services 
Albania is moderately prepared in the area of the right of establishment and freedom to 
provide services. Some progress was achieved in the reporting period especially by adopting 
the Law on freedom of establishment and the provision of services. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and 
therefore remain mostly valid. In the coming year, Albania should in particular: 
→ continue efforts to align secondary legislation related to the Law on freedom of 
establishment and provision of services, as well as sectoral legislation with the EU acquis  
and ensure appropriate institutional coordination and implementation capacity; 
→ continue efforts to align its legislation in the area of mutual recognition of professional

Chapter 4: Free movement of capital 
Albania has a  good level of preparation on free movement of capital. Good progress was 
made in aligning with the EU acquis  through the adoption of the Law on anti -money 
laundering and laws on payment services. Albania continued its action against money

2. CLUSTER 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS OF THE ACCESSION PROCESS 
2.1. FUNCTIONING OF DEMOCRATIC INSTITUTIONS AND PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION REFORM  
2.1.1 Democracy  
The functioning of democratic institutions in Albania is partially challenged, among others 
due to a deeply polarised political environment, limited parliamentary oversight over the 
executive, the urgent need for comprehensive electoral reform, Parliament’s persistent 
failure to implement some Constitutional Court rulings and the lack of meaningful public 
consultations. Daily protests have been taking place in Tirana since May 2026. This is the 
largest civic mobilisation since Albania's democratic transition, with tens of thousands of 
participants joining the protest at its peak. The protests started around environmental 
concerns linked to construction works in the protected landscape of Pishë Poro Narta and 
called for more transparency in the authorisation of large- scale projects, including 
consultation with the local population. They developed into a broader movement of 
dissatisfaction with the government and the main opposition party, calling for incre ased 
transparency, accountability, anti-corruption and good governance as well as pointing to the

=== PAGE 24 ===
23 
 
cost of living and a lack of investment in areas such as education and healthcare. In 
September 2026, an initiative aiming to improve the process of public consultation and 
access to information was launched by the government.  Building on the civic movement, 
student-led protests took place in October 2026 during which violent incidents occurred 
between protestors and the Albanian State Police, with destruction of public and private 
property by protestors followed by allegations of excessive violence by the police against 
protestors and journalists. No dialogue has taken place so far. All sides should make 
genuine attempts to engage in constructive dialogue. The Constitutional Court continued to 
be effective in upholding institutional checks and balances, delivering rulings on prominent 
cases affecting the functioning of the institutions at central and local level.  
Elections 
During the reporting period, mayoral by- elections took place on 9 November 2025 in the 
municipalities of Berat, Cërrik, Mat, Tepelena, and Vlora. The participation rate ranged from 
12.54% to 39.23%. These elections were held in the second half of the current mayoral 
mandate. Of the five mayors, four men and one woman were elected.  
The OSCE/ODIHR Final Election Observation Report confirmed the preliminary findings, 
indicating that the May 2025 general elections were competitive and professionally 
conducted but took place in a highly polarised environment and contestants did not enjoy a

raises concerns regarding checks and balances and the principle of equality before the law. 
Parliament made appointments to independent institutions, but the politicisation of these 
appointments remains a serious issue. The Ombudsperson was elected by ruling majority 
votes only, breaking an established tradition of cross -party consensus. The same process 
applied to the appointment of a new Commissioner for Protection against Discrimination. 
The legal framework for public consultation processes is in place but does not consistently 
result in timely and transparent follow -up. The recently established tools for public 
consultation are operational but have limited impact on policymaking.  Draft laws tabled 
often at short notice by ruling majority MPs affected the quality and transparency of the 
legislative process, often bypassing adequate public consultations. 
Women remain under-represented in Parliament, holding 48 of the 140 seats.  
EU integration 
Albania’s EU integration coordination structures continued their work during the reporting 
period. Additional financial and human resources are required to face the increased 
workload linked to accession negotiations, especially for the EU integration units in line 
ministries and agencies.  
The National Plan for European Integration  2025-2027 is in place. Work is ongoing for the 
adoption of a new plan.  
In September 2025, the standing Parliamentary Committee on European Affairs was merged 
into a new Committee on European and Foreign Affairs. This Committee is no longer chaired 
by the opposition, which limits cross-party dialogue and its ability to play an active role in the 
EU accession. In the reporting period, the Parliamentary Committee for Europe and Foreign 
Affairs reviewed 20 draft laws and 14 amendments to existing legislation to align legislation 
with EU acquis . The  National Council of European Integration has reduced its activities. 
Three meetings were held on fulfilling EU accession obligations,  but which was only 
attended by one of the co-chairs. 
The government has made efforts to communicate more regularly on EU integration, 
contributing to increased transparency and public involvement in the EU accession process. 
However, these efforts are fragmented, dominated by a silo culture between ministries and a 
lack of communication experts to carry out an effective communication strategy.  
Governance 
A substantial government reshuffle involving seven ministers took place in February 2026, 
following a three -month political and institutional deadlock. This was initiated by the SPAK

=== PAGE 26 ===
25 
 
court’s November 2025 decision to suspend the Deputy Prime Minister, as part of an 
investigation into alleged violations of equality of participation in public tenders.  
During the reporting period, both the ruling majority and the main opposition party employed 
rhetoric discrediting the judiciary or individual members thereof. This raises concerns about 
the separation of powers. 
While predominantly focusing on EU-related reforms in its legislative and policy planning, the 
government occasionally engaged in legislative activities not in line with the EU acquis  and 
European standards, including the adoption of an amendment to the law on touristic ports 
(see Chapter 5). The transparency of government decision-making on EU-related reforms did 
not improve, not did the sufficient allocation of financial and human resources to ensure 
their sustainability.  
On local self-government/decentralisation, the ongoing territorial and administrative reform, 
coupled with a tendency towards recentralisation, created uncertainty for local self -
governments. The lack of political commitment and institutional capacity limited the 
implementation of the Crosscutting Strategy on Decentralisation and Local Governance 
2023–2030. No annual monitoring reports have been published since 2023. The majority of 
local government revenues continues to come from central government transfers. Capacity 
development at the local level is expected to increase through the newly established 
Academy for Local Governance. Multi-level governance has been moderately reinforced  
thanks to a new ad hoc  Advisory Committees within the Consultative Council. Integrity plans 
were adopted in 44 of the 61 municipalities. Consultation with and accountability to the 
public at local level remain limited. 
Civil society  
Civil society, including human rights defenders, operates in a partly enabling environment, 
but has faced continued negative narratives, including online smear campaigns, particularly 
targeting those working on women’s rights, gender and LGBTIQ+ rights.  
The absence of dedicated safeguards against intimidation, including strategic lawsuits 
against public participation (SLAPPs), has contributed to a challenging environment for civil 
society and the protection mechanism must be strengthened. The government’s strategic 
framework for civil society support expired in 2023, with no indications on the development 
of a new plan. Structured consultations with civil society, especially in the context of EU 
accession, must be reinforced to ensure meaningful engagement . While consultation 
exercises were carried out with CSOs on the Law on donations and sponsorship, concerns 
remain on its potential impact on the enabling environment for civil society including 
excessive administrative burdens. Additionally, tax incentives remain limited, and public 
funding levels are insufficient. Progress has been made in the implementation of VAT 
exemption, addressing a long -standing concern raised by civil society. A new volunteering 
law improves the legal framework but is still largely regulatory rather than supportive.  
2.1.2. Public administration reform 
Strategic framework for public administration reform 
The strategic framework for public administration is in place. 
Implementation of t he Strategy on Public Administration Reform 2025 –2030 is progressing 
steadily. However, the 2026 implementation report shows that challenges remain in the 
pillars for green administration and services.  
The implementation of the crosscutting Strategy on Decentralisation and Local Governance 
2023–2030 remains limited due to the lack of reliable data or monitoring reports since 2023. 
The consistent delays in the preparation of the reports undermines the ability of the

challenges. 
Greater consistency between policy planning and budgeting is needed, as the financial 
sustainability of key reforms is heavily dependent on external funding. 
Policy development and coordination 
The legal basis and the institutional set- up for policymaking are in place, yet there is still 
room for improvement.  
Half of the draft laws proposed by the government in 2025 continued to originate outside 
the Government Analytical Programme, thereby reducing the effectiveness of legislative 
planning and undermining the predictability of the legislative process. The Strategic 
Planning Committee continued to meet regularly and steered the policymaking process. No 
progress was made on the use of the Integrated Planning System, mainly due to its 
complexity and the non-functioning supporting IT systems.  
The use of regulatory impact assessments (RIAs) for evidence -based policymaking and 
legislative development continued but needs to further increase. In 2025, 40 out of the 47 
RIAs reported by the government were reviewed satisfactorily by the PMO quality control 
unit, while 43 out of 57 RIAs  were considered of sufficient quality in the period from January 
to August 2026.  
However, the RIAs still lack substantial analysis, proposals for alternatives, analysis if 
impact on affected groups, and cost-benefit assessments. Implementing legislation remains 
largely exempt from the application of RIAs, but in early 2026, a pilot project was launched to 
extend RIA requirements to six sub-legal acts. Implementation remains to be assessed.  
In 2025, 45 of 47 adopted legal acts were subject to public consultation, while implementing 
legislation was rarely subject to public consultation. More action is needed to ensure 
meaningful public consultations, strengthen quality control and guarantee proper follow -up. 
Stakeholder participation in public consultations, particularly from civil society, should 
further increase. The government did not systematically follow -up on public consultations, 
which limited their effectiveness. 
Public financial management 
The public financial management (PFM) and revenue strategies are in place, and 
implementation is regularly reported on, indicating satisfactory implementation rates. The 
legal basis for PFM is mostly in place with a comprehensive organic budget law.  
The provisions of the organic budget law are largely followed. However, the practice of 
conducting budget revisions exclusively through a high number of normative acts by the 
executive has continued, weakening fiscal sustainability, budgetary oversight and 
transparency. More decisive action is needed to return to regular budget review processes 
through parliament, including the effective involvement of the public. The independent fiscal 
oversight body, the Fiscal Council, remains to be set up.  
Budgetary reforms advanced, notably with the introduction of climate change budgeting 
elements and continued implementation of gender responsive budgeting (GRB), where the 
formal introduction of GRB through the newly adopted Gender Equality Law was also an 
important benchmark.  
The link between strategic priorities and budget programmes improved but should be further 
strengthened. The national single project pipeline procedures were amended to improve 
their application and the quality of project feasibility assessments. In December 2025, the 
revised Law on public-private partnership (PPP) and concessions was adopted, envisaging a 
clearer inclusion of these projects in the overall investment pipeline. Fiscal risk monitoring

comprehensive fiscal risk statement covering 2023 and 2024, covering a broader range of 
risks such as climate and natural disasters and deepening analysis of other fiscal risks.  
Nevertheless, continued efforts are needed to improve fiscal risk reporting and analysis for 
state-owned enterprises and PPPs. Budget commitment compliance should be further 
strengthened to ensure a structural stabilisation of payment arrears. A new debt strategy 
was adopted, pursuing the targets of reducing public debt/GDP, extending debt maturities 
and lowering exchange risk exposure. 
Budget transparency is mostly satisfactory with key budget documents regularly published. 
But further improvement is needed on clear arrangements for consistent collection, 
consolidation and publication of financial statements from all government -controlled 
entities, including on extra-budgetary entities, while enhancing coordination between the line 
ministries and the Institute of Statistics (INSTAT) in this regard.   
Public services and human resources management 
Public services and human resources management still require improvement.  
The amendments to the Law on the civil servant are yet to be adopted. A substantial part of 
civil service will be regulated through implementing legislation.  
The current rules on merit -based recruitment, promotion and dismissal are applied 
inconsistently. Most civil servants are still recruited through a group or pool recruitment 
process. Implementation of the 2025 recruitment plan reached 55%. A total of 69% of 
advertised vacancies were filled, down from 81% in 2024 and below the PAR Strategy target 
of 100%.  
Overall, the gender distribution is 57.8% female and 42.2% male. Women account for 55.% of 
middle-level managerial positions and 54.% of senior-level managerial positions Three senior 
positions are still vacant despite the availability of a pool of candidates from the top 
management- corps group. Opening vacancies for managerial positions to external 
candidates should remain an exception.  
There were eight dismissals from the civil service from January to August 2026, compared 
with nine dismissals in 2025 and14 in 2024. Regarding the execution of final court decisions 
on unlawful dismissals, 13 final court decisions were executed in 2025. The enforcement 
rate of final court decisions on dismissals and reinstatements went down slightly to 85.8%, 
from 86.6% in 2024.  
Timely government enforcement of such decisions remains inadequate. The current Human 
Resources Management Information System (HRMIS) is still unable to cover the entire 
public sector and remains unsuitable as an automated payroll system. Efforts to redesign 
the HRMIS accordingly are required. 78 out of 177 institutions have an integrity plan, while at 
the local level, 44 out of 61 municipalities have an integrity plan. The quality and 
implementation of integrity plans vary. 
The Albanian School of Public Administration continued to provide online training 
programmes for professional development.

The accountability of the administration needs to be further improved.  
The revised law on the organisation and functioning of the state administration aiming to 
reduce the high number (29) of authorities/agencies subordinated directly under the Prime

accountability and, improve delegation of decision -making power at director level was

accountability remains to be assessed.  
Further effort is needed to strengthen the right of citizens to good administration. In 2025, 
only 17% of the recommendations issued by the People’s Advocate (PA) were fully 
implemented and 56% were implemented partially. The implementation rate requires 
significant improvement. Cooperation between Parliament and the PA should be enhanced 
and the relevant institutions should be held accountable.  
On transparency and access to information , the Commissioner for the right to information 
reported a steady number of complaints received in 2025 (861), often resolved after his

Administrative justice continues to face significant challenges due to the growing backlog

Service delivery to citizens and businesses 
Service delivery to citizens and businesses is broadly in place, with room for further 
improvement.  
The implementation of the 2022-2026 Digital Agenda continued to progress, and the offer of

accessibility issues for groups in vulnerable situations, including people with disabilities, 
persist and need to be addressed.  
In 2025, the pillar on service delivery remains one of the poorer performers in terms of

2.2. RULE OF LAW AND FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS 
2.2.1. Chapter 23: Judiciary and fundamental rights  
The EU’s founding values include the rule of law and respect for human rights. An effective 
(independent, high -quality and efficient) judicial system and an effective fight against 
corruption are of paramount importance, as is the respect for fundamental rights in law and in 
practice. 
Functioning of the judiciary  
Strategic documents 
The Cross-Sector Justice Strategy for 2024- 2030 (estimated budget: EUR 56.4 million) and 
its action plan remain in place, but their implementation needs to be improved, particularly 
through enhanced monitoring mechanisms, regular stakeholders’ meetings and clear 
communication. A new regulatory framework for the harmonised collection, reporting and 
communication of judicial data has been adopted, but additional resources are needed to 
ensure timely and qualitative reporting.  
Management bodies 
The efficiency of the  High Judicial Council (HJC) and the High Prosecutorial Council (HPC) 
in appointing, promoting, transferring and evaluating magistrates has improved, enhancing 
judicial quality and efficiency at first instance courts. The HJC and the HPC have reviewed 
their internal rules and procedures and adopted new regulatory frameworks, relating to the

improved to ensure that all judges are evaluated in a timely manner to access promotion. 
Coordination between the HJC and the HPC has improved through the organisation of four 
joint meetings and the adoption of a regulatory framework to ensure the regularity of such

The 2016 justice reform and recent legal changes strengthened the independence of the

oversight of their members in practice. Continued training and enforcement of

remain weak.  The rules of procedure to ensure transparency in relation to promotions, 
appointments and transfers, have been strengthened through legislative amendments, The 
HJC and the HPC still need to urgently address the lack of specialised resources, notably

courts and prosecution offices, as provided for in the law.

Freedom of expression and media freedom 
Intimidation of journalists 
While the institutional framework for the protection of journalists is in place, the deterrent 
impact of procedural safeguards remains limited. Cases of intimidation and threats against 
journalists, including by high- ranking state officials and police officers, became more 
prominent through smear campaigns and strategic lawsuits against public participation 
(SLAPPs), politically motivated physical and verbal attacks, and disinformation. Investigative 
and female journalists were targeted to a greater extent.  Media freedom organisations 
denounced 42 cases of violations of journalists’ safety in 2025 (down from 52 in 2024). The 
amendments to the Criminal Code adopted by the Parliament in January 2026 strengthened 
the protection of journalists against violence and serious threats by increasing sanctions 
related to attacks against them. Journalists still have limited access to legal assistance, and 
systematic monitoring of judicial cases involving journalists remains limited. 
Despite some efforts made in judicial follow up on cases involving threats or attacks against 
journalists by police, prosecution and judges, impunity remained prevalent due to the 
continued lack of a robust track record of investigations and judicial action. The State Police 
recorded 21 cases involving journalists, including six complaints related to threats to life or 
bodily harm and 14 involving other forms of threats or harassment. According to the General 
Prosecution Office, nine referrals involving jou rnalists which were carried over from previous 
years were reviewed between in 2025, while the number of newly referred cases for the 
same year was six. For the first quarter of 2026, the General Prosecutor has received seven 
new referrals. Nevertheless, perception s urveys conducted by independent media 
organisations indicate low awareness of these judicial and law enforcement mechanisms 
and limited confidence in institutional responses.  
Digital threats and online harassment continued. Serious concerns persist regarding privacy 
violations, as journalists’ personal and professional data continue to be misused by 
unauthorised entities. Institutional mechanisms for reporting, addressing and mitigating 
digital threats remain insufficient. There is still a lack of systemic monitoring and reporting 
on judicial cases involving journalists. Access to legal assistance and support from media 
outlets remain limited for journalists. 
Legislative environment 
Albania has taken some steps towards aligning the legislative framework with the EU acquis , 
notably with the European Media Freedom Act. In January 2026, Albania adopted 
amendments to the Law on Audiovisual Media to increase media ownership transparency. 
However, media independence and plurality continued to be negatively affected by the high 
market concentration and the lack of transparency in media financing, especially in electoral 
contexts, and of economic interests, including public funding. Provisions on defamation 
were abrogated with the aim of protecting registered journalists under cert ain conditions.   
Albania is expected to fully decriminalise defamation. Albania needs to align with the EU

dialogue between Albanian institutions and media actors on media reform progressed

of the platform are reflected will depend on further developments in 2026 as Albania 
continues efforts to conduct a broader media reform.  The Law on access to information

Public service broadcaster

detailed assessment by the European Broadcasting Union (EBU), a reform support mission 
for RTSH was launched in 2026 to address identified issues including in the br oadcaster’s 
structure with the aim of aligning with European standards in this area. 
Economic factors 
High ownership concentration and opaque funding practices still compromise media 
independence and plurality. The legal framework continues to lack provisions on clear and 
transparent distribution of public sector advertising in line with international standards. 
Standard public procurement rules in the advertising procurement procedure can still be 
circumvented. Some steps have been taken towards establishing an audience measurement 
system. A Joint Industry Committee, including representatives of national and regional 
broadcasters, the RTSH, and marketing agencies, was formally registered as an independent 
legal entity in September 2025. The influence of politically affiliated business groups, 
particularly during the pre-electoral period, continued to raise concerns.

Procedural rights of suspects and accused persons and rights of victims of crime 
The legal framework on procedural rights is incomplete, and the protection of victim’s rights 
needs to improve. The legal framework for legal aid is comprehensive, and primary and

aligned with European standards. Persons belonging to minorities continued to face 
discrimination. The implementation of secondary legislation related to self -identification

The legal framework still needs to be effectively implemented, notably by allocating

State Committee on National Minorities increased in 2026, while the institutional fund for the 
minority related projects was doubled for 2025 and 2026 compared with previous years.  
Roma 
The legal and institutional framework on Roma needs to be further improved. Roma people 
continue to face discrimination.  A new National Action Plan for equality, inclusion and 
participation of Roma and Egyptians in Albania (2026 -2030) was adopted in June 2026. The 
overall employment rate of Roma and Egyptians continues to be very low. Their access to 
additional out-of-pocket health services remains financially constrained. Challenges persist 
in civil registration, as DNA testing is still required. The implementation of the Poznan 
commitments needs to be significantly improved. The institutional capacity of the Ministry 
of Health and Social Protection, where the National Roma Contact Point is placed, needs to 
be strengthened. Additional efforts and resources are required to accelerate the inclusion of 
Roma and Egyptians, tackle anti -gypsyism, discrimination and educational and physical 
segregation, promote Roma participation and reduce the gaps with the rest of the 
population.

Functioning of product markets 
Business environment 
Structural weaknesses continue to weigh on Albania's business environment, with rule of 
law, corruption and informality remaining the most persistent concerns. While businesses 
acknowledge some progress on justice reform, weaknesses in the rule of law are 
compounded by administrative burdens, including cumbersome licensing procedures and 
inconsistencies in case law. Curbing tax evasion and broadening the formal economy 
remain key policy priorities. Informality, estimated at around one third of GDP 9, is high, 
including in tourism, where many short -term rental operators remain outside official 
registers, limiting oversight and leaving formal employment and tax revenue lagging behind 
the sector's growth. The MTRS, inter alia, aims to address this through improved tax 
administration and formalisation. 
The investment climate is further weighed down by weaknesses in legal certainty and 
property administration. Businesses frequently question the transparency of strategic 
investor status, PPPs and concession contracts. Furthermore, recent protests over large 
tourism and coastal development projects have further highlighted weaknesses in legal 
certainty, land ownership and environmental safeguards. Albania completed the 
digitalisation of approximately 4.3 million property cards by June 2026, continued work on 
the initial registration of private and public properties, and on the full digitalisation of 
physical cadas tral maps. However, incomplete and inconsistent property registration 
continues to deter investment by creating ownership uncertainty and disputes. More broadly, 
public service delivery remains heavily centralised in Tirana, leaving rural businesses and 
regions underserved. Continuation of judicial reform measures, enhancing legal certainty 
and simplification of business procedures will be essential for creating a more predictable, 
transparent and investment-friendly business environment. 
State influence on product markets 
The adoption of a unified investment law has been further delayed, and the Law on 
Strategic Investment has been extended until end- 2026. Under the Reform Agenda, Albania 
committed to analyse the existing FDI legal framework and adopt a unified investment law

Privatisation and restructuring 
SOEs pose significant fiscal risks, and their governance remains in need of improvement. 
Fiscal risks stem primarily from government loans and guarantees to SOEs and PPPs, 
particularly in the energy sector, which concentrates over 80% of guaranteed debt, although 
the overall stock of guarantees edged down to 1.1% of GDP in 2025. In June 2026, Albania 
adopted a Decision of the Council of Ministers to strengthen transparency in the selection, 
composition and functioning of SOEs’ supervisory boards. The Ministry of Finance’s Fiscal 
Risk Unit continued monitoring 12 SOEs, and technical assistance i s ongoing to improve 
their financial reporting and performance monitoring. The 2025 Fiscal Risk Statement was 
also published, although broader coverage is envisaged. Moreover, strengthening oversight 
and improving transparency in SOE reporting remain essential for containing fiscal risks and 
ensuring a level playing field. 
Functioning of the financial market 
The banking sector remained well -capitalised and profitable, with non -performing loans 
falling to their lowest level since 2008. Banks maintained a capital adequacy ratio of 20.4% 
in December 2025, well above the 12% minimum requirement, supported by strong liquidity 
positions. Profitability declined but remained positive, with return on assets falling to 1.9% 
from 2.2% and return on equity to 15.8% from 18.3% a year earlier. The non- performing loan 
ratio declined to 3.8% in May 2026. Private sector credit accelerated, driven by higher 
demand and favourable financing conditions, reaching 35% of GDP by end -2025, with.  
growth exceedin g 14% year -on-year in the first half of 2026. Nevertheless, financial 
intermediation remained limited, while capital markets remained underdeveloped. Moreover, 
government securities continued to represent a high share of bank assets, exposing banks 
to sovereign risk. Albania also still lacks a legal framework for private equity and venture 
capital, although crowdfunding legislation, in preparation since 2024, is expected to be 
adopted in 2026. At the same time, bank supervision remained prudent amid continu ed 
strong credit growth. Progress was made in collecting data on banks' exposure to the real 
estate sector and monitoring credit standards, and the Bank of Albania introduced borrower-
based macroprudential measures, including loan -to-value and debt service- to-income limits, 
in response to rapid mortgage growth and rising residential real estate prices, while doubling 
the countercyclical capital buffer to 0.5%, effective from December 2025. Oversight of non -
bank financial institutions should continue to be s trengthened. Two new banks were 
licensed during the reporting period. 
The establishment of the National Development Bank (NDB) raised concerns over its 
design and supervision. Following the final approval of the Law on the NDB by Parliament in 
March 2025, the NDB continued preparations to start operations, including the recruitment

Sectoral and enterprise structure 
While the services sector continues to expand, agriculture retains a structurally significant 
role in the Albanian economy. Services accounted for around 49% of GDP in 2025, driven by 
stable tourism growth. Construction retained a solid contribution at 12.6% of GDP (11.9% in 
2024). Industry and manufacturing faced headwinds from weak external demand and lek 
appreciation, with their shares in GDP falling to 10.1% and 6.5% respectively in 2025. 
Agriculture, whose share of GDP has fallen from over 21% in the early 2020s to 14.6% in 
2025, continued to play a structurally significant role, with a share of employment still well 
above the EU average.

structural challenges. In 2024, SMEs employed 82% of the workforce and generated 78.1%

4. RESILIENCE, SECURITY, DEFENCE 
Albania has a clear strategic orientation anchored in the EU accession process. It continues 
to align with the EU’s common foreign and security policy, including restrictive measures, 
and contributes significantly to EU crisis management operations and missions. 
Democratic resilience  
Albania’s security -related challenges to its democratic institutions are largely related to 
hybrid threats, including foreign information manipulation and interference (FIMI) and 
disinformation, which can exacerbate political polarisation and affect democratic 
processes. The revision of the legislative framework around elections should meet the 
challenges of modern influence campaigns, including transparency of political advertising, 
political party financing and the use of social media in election campaigns. The EU -Albania 
Security and Defence Dialogues could be used to discuss further the countering of hybrid 
threats. Capacity-building should increase, including through targeted training and improved 
inter-institutional coordination, building on TAIEX support. Albania ne eds to leverage its 
membership of the Hybrid Centre of Excellence in Helsinki to develop expertise and set up a 
horizontal coordination mechanism.  
Economic security and resilience 
Albania’s strategic geographic location presents opportunities for alternative trade routes 
and investment, as well as deeper integration into the European market, supporting supply 
chain resilience . However, the economy is exposed to external shocks due to its high 
reliance on tourism, remittances and FDI. Participation in global value chains remains 
concentrated in textiles, footwear, agrifood, and tourism -related services, with FDI inflows

=== PAGE 73 ===
72 
 
reaching EU 1.8 billion in 2025 (around 6% of GDP). Its largely low- carbon electricity system 
relies heavily on hydropower. While Albania produces 98% of its electricity consumption, it 
remains dependent on diminishing water resources. Renewables account for 46.6% of 
Albania's energy consumption, nearly double the EU average of 24.5%. Yet, changing 
weather patterns threaten the country's hydroelectric advantages, as Albania is dependent 
on electricity imports during dry periods. Albania has the great potential for increased and 
long-term EU supply chain integration, particularly in the critical raw materials sector through 
large titanomagnetite deposits for titanium and vanadium, chromite for chromium mining 
capabilities as a strategic asset, copper mining capabilities as well as one nickel and several 
small to medium sized iron -nickel deposits that are currently being exploited. Albania has 
fully embraced the AI transition, with a focus on the automation of public procurement 
procedures to reduce corruption.  Following recent cyber- attacks, Albania has developed 
cyber defence capabilities (see Chapter 10 – Digital transformation and media ). Further 
efforts are needed to strengthen resilience to hybrid threats. Albania should continue to 
align with EU digital standards, including in the field of AI and cyber-security. 
Defence capacity building and defence industrial dimension  
Albania is well positioned to contribute to the EU’s defence readiness given its consistent 
and continuous CFSP alignment, its NATO membership and interoperability, and its strategic 
location for military mobility across the Western Balkans and Europe, in particular via the 
Adriatic region.  
In terms of defence capacity building, Albania benefits from EUR 49 million under the 
European Peace Facility (EPF, an off -budget instrument, through three bilateral assistance 
packages, enabling it to actively contribute to the CSDP. It also benefits from EPF support at 
regional level, as a non-EU participating nation in the Balkan Medical Task Force.  
Albania’s defence production capacity is limited. The country's defence sector comprises 22 
registered companies and eight major defence- related product importers in both the private 
and public sectors. Efforts have increased to revitalise the sector. A state -owned enterprise 
was established in 2024 to mutualise the production capacities of three production plants. 
Moreover, entities established in the EU or in an associated country and subject to control by 
Albania or by an Albanian entity could be eligible for funding under the European Defence 
Industrial Programme (EDIP).  Albania, Kosovo and Croatia signed a Memorandum of 
Understanding in March 2025 on defence cooperation. A MoU was also signed between the 
state-owned enterprise and an Italian defence company to revitalise the Pashaliman 
shipyards.  
Civil protection and disaster resilience 
Albania continues improving its resilience by further strengthening its civil protection and 
disaster preparedness capacities in line with the EU Preparedness Union Strategy and in the 
framework of the Union Civil Protection Mechanism (UCPM).

Chapter 28: Consumer and health protection 
EU rules protect consumers’ economic interests and also ensure product safety, prevent 
dangerous imitations and establish liability for defective products. The EU  also guarantees a 
high level of protection of public health and in particular also ensures high common standards 
for tobacco control, blood, tissues, cells and organs, and human and veterinary medicinal 
products. The EU also has rules for upholding patients’ rights in cross -border healthcare and in 
preparing for and responding to serious cross -border health threats, including communicable

Consumer protection 
On horizontal aspects , Albania has a 2023 –2030 cross-cutting strategy on  consumer 
protection and market surveillance in place. In reporting period, the Consumer Protection 
body (CPC) received complaints, though no information was available on how this was dealt 
with. Albania should finalise and adopt its new package of law on consumer protection, 
which is designed to align with many elements of the EU acquis  in this area, including on 
consumer rights, on unfair business -to-consular commercial practices, on the pricing of 
products offered to consumers, on empowering consumers for the green transition, on 
promoting the repair of goods, on sale of goods and digital content and on distance and off -

municipalities. There have not been any developments on enforcement and redress.

to operate largely unregulated. On product safety, Albania is yet to align with the new EU 
acquis on general product safety. Albania is partly aligned on product liability. ( For 
information on market surveillance, see Chapter 1 – Free movement of goods).

Chapter 10: Digital transformation and media 
The EU supports the smooth functioning of the internal market for electronic communications, 
electronic commerce and audiovisual services. The rules protect consumers and support the

On electronic communications, limited progress was made in implementing the 112 single

broadband penetration rate among households reached 97% at the end of 2025, about 5 
percentage points higher than in the previous year.  
Mobile broadband usage continued to improve during the reporting period. In 2025, an active 
mobile user consumed on average 10.38 GB of mobile broadband data per month, up 16% 
compared with 2024. According to official data, 5G technology covers up to 40% of the 
population.

EU rules on taxation cover value -added tax, excise duties as well as aspects of corporate 
taxation. They also cover cooperation between tax administrations, including the exchange of 
information to prevent tax evasion. 
Albania has been implementing its medium- term revenue strategy (MTRS) and met the 
target for the increase in tax revenue in 2025. This was attributed in large part to the 
targeted formalisation and compliance improvement efforts in the tourism and construction 
sectors.  
As part of the 2026 fiscal package, Albania adopted the law on tax and customs debt 
cancellation, which fully cancels tax and customs liabilities from before 2014 and partially 
cancels tax liabilities from 2015 to 2024 against payment of part of the principal debt. This 
measure is expected to bring additional revenue and reduce part of the sizeable tax debt 
stock but poses future tax compliance risks.  
Albania also adopted a Fiscal Peace Agreement, which raises concerns as it offers eligible 
taxpayers the possibility of a lighter tax audit regime for a limited time, and the possibility to 
re-declare financial statement elements at a highly reduced rate in exchange for payment of 
a pre- determined profit tax set at the previous year’s taxable profit with an 18% growth 
margin.  
On indirect taxation, the standard VAT rates remained unchanged. A substantial number of 
VAT exemptions and reduced rates not set out in the EU acquis  remain and none were 
removed in the reporting period, although the June 2025 tax exemption review provided 
some tax expenditure assessment of them. As part of the fiscal package 2026, the VAT law 
was amended to align Albania’s framework for the flat -rate compensation scheme for 
farmers with Directive 2006/112; introducing a 10% flat-rate compensation and clarifying the 
calculation method.  
The national tax legislation imposes tax on imported plastic as good/raw material, which 
stands in contradiction to the commitment under the SAA. 
Excise taxation is partially aligned with the EU acquis regarding excise duty for alcohol and 
alcoholic beverages, tobacco, energy products and general rules for the production, keeping, 
storage, movement and controls on excisable products. Alignment on EU excise minima on 
tobacco products is ongoing, through a gradual and scheduled increase of cigarette excise 
rates from 2025 to 2033, which continued to be applied. Albania still needs to move to a 
mixed excise duty structure ( ad valorem  and specific) for cigarettes. N ational legislation 
providing for a preferential rate of tax for ‘Albanian rakia’ produced by private individuals is 
not aligned with EU legislation . Excise duty rate on some fuel products are still below the EU 
minimum according to energy tax Directives. 
On direct taxation , the income tax law is partially aligned with the EU acquis . A broader 
overhaul of income tax legislation with EU acquis  is under preparation, including mergers 
and anti-tax-avoidance provisions.  
On administrative cooperation and mutual assistance , Albania is preparing legal 
amendments to fully align with the Directive on administrative cooperation (DAC) . Albania 
has double taxation agreement with 23 Member States and committed to conclude 
agreements with the four outstanding ones – Denmark, Cyprus, Portugal and Lithuania. The 
agreement with Lithuania has been signed but is yet to be ratified.

Member States) in 2025 and has received information from 88 jurisdictions (including all 27

Chapter 17: Economic and monetary policy 
EU rules require the independence of central banks and prohibit them from directly financing 
the public sector. Member States coordinate their economic policies and are subject to fiscal, 
economic and financial surveillance. 
On monetary policy, Albania has a high level of alignment with the EU acquis  on the 
prohibition of privileged access of the public sector to financial institutions. The legislation 
is aligned with the EU acquis  on the central bank independence and on the prohibition of 
monetary financing to the public sector.  The Law on the Bank of Albania (BoA) was adopted 
in October 2026. Due to the end of mandate in December for six out of the nine members of 
the Supervisory Board, the Central Bank lacked the quorum to hold meetings until March, 
when the new members were appointed. Albania needs to ensure that the appointment of all 
members of the Supervisory Council is conducted in a timely manner as delays create 
operational risks for the BoA’s functioning.  
On economic policy, numerical fiscal rules, including those on the primary balance and the 
debt ratio, are in place, promoting fiscal prudence (see also Section 2.3 - Economic criteria). 
Budget revisions continued to be endorsed through normative acts in 2025, and during 2026. 
Albania aligned with Directive 2011/85/EU on requirements for budgetary frameworks. 
Albania also adopted a guideline on the preparation and publication of the report on 
deviations of macroeconomic, fiscal, and budgetary forecasts. Work progressed on 
addressing the remaining alignment requirements, including the adoption of the law on the 
Fiscal Council. An MoU between INSTAT, the Bank of Albania, and the Ministry of Finance 
was signed in May, to strengthen inter- institutional data exchange and coordination 
mechanisms. 
Albania is aligned with the requirements on the coordination and surveillance of economic 
policies laid down by the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. Its active 
participation in the Economic Reform Programme (ERP)  exercise has contributed to 
developing the administrative capacity of the country relevant for this chapter. Albania 
submitted the 2026-2028 ERP on 15 January 2026. Albania’s ERP outlines the country’s key 
macroeconomic and fiscal policies. The policy guidance jointly adopted at the 2025 
Economic and Financial Dialogue has been partially implemented. Albania's ERP would 
benefit from stronger analysis of long -term fiscal sustainability, a more comprehensive 
assessment of fiscal risks and the budgetary impact of key fiscal measures, and a more 
systematic use of productivity indicators.

Chapter 25: Science and research 
The EU provides significant support for research and innovation. All EU Member States can 
participate in the EU’s research and innovation programmes and benefit from them, especially 
where there is scientific excellence and solid investment in research and innovation. 
On research and innovation policy, Albania  has made  steady progress by adopting and 
implementing legislation and policies  aligned with the  EU acquis and by reinforcing its 
administrative and technical structures. The required legislation to ensure effective 
implementation of the Law on Science and Scientific Research and the 2023- 2030 National 
Strategy for Science, Technology and Innovation were adopted. Albania made progress in 
implementing the European Research Infrastructure Consortium (ERIC) acquis  and further 
aligning its national research policies with the European Research Area. Albania took 
steps to meet the target of 1% of GDP by 2030 for investment in research and science. 
Nonetheless, funding for R&D in 2025 amounted to only 0.3% of GDP. Further action remains 
necessary to increase public funding of research and development, to provide more 
incentives to the private sector to increase R&D expenditure, and to improve cooperation 
between the private sector and universities.

National Agency for Scientific Research and Innovation, are adequately resourced. The 
implementation of the Smart Specialisation Strategy should be prioritised along with the 
continued implementation of the Law on Science and Scientific Research as well as the 
2023-2030 National Strategy for Science, Technology and Innovation.

The EU supports cooperation in education and culture through funding programmes and the 
coordination of Member State policies through the ‘open method of coordination’. The EU and

Albania has the necessary institutional and policy framework in place, as demonstrated by

Chapter 27: Environment and climate change 
The EU promotes strong climate action, sustainable development and protection of the 
environment. EU rules contain provisions addressing, horizontal environmental issues , climate 
change, water and air quality, waste management, nature protection, industrial pollution,

Environment 
In terms of horizontal legislation, Albania still needs to substantially improve alignment with 
and implementation of the environmental impact assessment (EIA) and the strategic 
environmental assessment (SEA) Directives. Persistent weaknesses in institutional 
capacities hamper coordination, planning and investments. The quality of EIAs remains 
inadequate and recommendations are occasionally implemented or ineffectively monitored. 
Public participation in decision -making processes remains limited and enforcement of 
horizontal legislation insufficient. A draft law improving public consultations for environment 
related matters was submitted for consultation. The effectiveness of inspection and 
enforcement mechanisms remain a concern.  Albania needs to make significant steps to 
improve environmental monitoring and strengthen administrative capacity to meet EU 
standards. 
A law establishing a National Environmental Fund underwent public consultation. A decision 
to establish the Green Tree Fund was adopted, which requires any construction project to 
plan tree planting. Albania still needs to align with the Environmental Crime Directive and 
tackle environmental crime, especially in protected areas. Inter-ministerial collaboration on 
environmental issues is to be developed.  
On air quality, a dedicated Directorate for Air Quality and Environmental Statistics was set -

organisational step. Albania is amending the Law on the protection of ambient air quality to

align with the relevant EU acquis. Implementing legislation still needs to be adopted. Albania

Climate change 
Over the reporting period Albania made good progress on alignment with EU climate acquis , 
but the country’s ability to integrate climate change into sectoral strategies and plans 
remains limited. The Ministry of Environment has established a Directorate for Climate 
Change composed of eight staff members.  
In December 2025, Albania amended the climate law to increase alignment with the 
provisions of the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) Directive, and the climate 
adaptation provisions of the European Climate Law. However, the climate law should be 
further updated to reflect the EU’s 2050 climate neutrality target. The country should also 
develop a long -term low -carbon development strategy consistent with this goal. In June 
2026, Albania adopted an updated National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and submitted to the  
Energy Community secretariat its National Energy and Climate Plan in August 2026. 
The amended Climate Law and two pieces of implementing legislation were adopted by the 
government in July 2026 which set the legal basis for the monitoring, reporting, verification 
and accreditation (MRVA) system covering the full scope of the EU ETS with Albania aiming 
to fully implement MRVA for power generation, heavy industry and aviation sectors by the 
end of 2026.  Implementation of MRVA in the shipping and ETS 2 sectors (buildings, road 
transport and small industry) is due to be achieved by the end of  2027. Albania shall ensure 
that all operators and regulated entities submit their monitoring plans and receive 
greenhouse gas (GHG) permits on time, so they are ready to start monitoring as 1 of January 
2027 or, for shipping and ETS 2 sectors, 1 January 2028. Albania also needs to take decisive 
steps to introduce carbon pricing. This will ensure easier integration into the EU ETS upon 
accession. Lastly, Albania should prepare and adopt a roadmap for EU ETS implementation,

CLUSTER 5: RESOURCES, AGRICULTURE AND COHESION 
This cluster covers: agriculture and rural development (Chapter 11); food safety, veterinary

Chapter 11: Agriculture and rural development 
The EU’s common agricultural policy supports farmers and contributes to ensuring Europe’s 
food security. It helps tackle climate change and the sustainable management of national 
resources; maintains rural areas and landscapes across the EU; and keeps the rural economy 
alive by promoting jobs in farming, agri -food industries and associated sectors. This requires 
strong management and control systems. There are also common EU rules for marketing 
standards, quality policy and organic farming. 
On horizontal issues, Albania needs to build -up its institutional capacity and align its policy, 
financial management and control systems with the common agriculture policy (CAP). 
During the reporting period, some progress was made in developing the integrated 
administration and control system (IACS). An implementation plan for its establishment is 
being developed. The farm register still needs to be fully aligned with the EU acquis . Setting 
up the land parcel identification system (LPIS) has advanced and needs to continue. In this 
regard, Albania has established the financial framework for LPIS implementation until 2028, 
with an overall envelope of EUR 8 m. Two pilot exercises have been completed, which 
included testing on -the-spot verification, overlapping declarations and quality -control 
procedures. These resulted in the selection of a reference parcel model and standard 
parameters for minimum parcel size, land cover and land use categories and lan dscape 
features. Albania is also developing an implementation plan on the setting up of the future 
paying agency. 
Albania made progress on the setting -up of a farm sustainability data network (FSDN). On 
31 August 2026, Albania adopted a Ministerial Order which establishes the national legal 
and institutional framework for FSDN, transforming the Farm Accountancy Data Network 
into the Farm Sustainability Data Network and extending its scope to environmental and 
social sustainability data. The advisory system reform remains at an early stage and 
capacity remains low.  
On agricultural markets - common market organisation (CMO), legislation in the wine sector 
was adopted in December 2025 and is partially aligned with the EU acquis . Further efforts 
are needed to continue legislative alignment and strengthen the institutional capacity for 
implementation. 
On rural development, Albania submitted its request for entrustment with budgetary tasks 
for the IPARD III Programme on 1 April and 18 June 2026. 
No progress was made to align the legislation on quality policy with the EU acquis during the 
reporting period. 
On organic farming, the implementing legislation to the Law on organic production, labelling 
of organic products and their control, which would further align  national rules  with the EU 
acquis, remains pending. 
On the fight against corruption , the Albanian authorities are obliged to prevent, detect and 
report irregularities.

food and feed safety and a high level of protection of consumers’ interests. Animal health and

On general food safety, the legal framework remains partially aligned with the EU General

and to intensify surveillance as it is the only true indicator to declare a rabies-free zone.

Chapter 13: Fisheries and aquaculture 
The common fisheries policy lays down rules on fisheries management, protects living 
resources of the sea and limits the environmental impact of fisheries. This includes setting 
catch quotas, managing fleet capacity, rules on control and inspection, rules on markets and 
aquaculture and support for fisheries and coastal communities. Further, it promotes a 
sustainable aquaculture. 
Regarding resource and fleet management, Albania’s legislation is broadly in line with the 
recommendations of the General Fisheries Commission for the Mediterranean, which has 
adopted several management plans for the sustainable exploitation of fish stocks applying 
to Albania. Efforts are ongoing to implement the EU common fisheries policy into the 
national legislation. 
On data collection, data on fishery production is regularly maintained and updated. However, 
further efforts are needed to align on data collection requirements on sharks, eels, biological 
data collection, recreational fisheries and discard plans.  Further improvements on scientific 
capacity and data management are also needed.  
Regarding inspection and control,  the capacity to monitor fishing activity 24/7 to combat 
illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing is in place, based on a functional and 
operational vessel monitoring system (VMS). The online electronic inspection system 
provides for a transparent procedure, secure data recording and transmission to the relevant 
authorities. However, full alignment with the EU acquis and additional monitoring capacities 
are still required. 
On market policy , legislation was aligned with the EU acquis  on common market 
organisation.  
Regarding aquaculture, ten contracts were granted in the district of Durres and Saranda for 
mussel and fish production. Further progress was made on preparation of the legal basis for 
the use of alien and absent species.  
Regarding international agreements on fisheries and aquaculture management, Albania 
continued to fully implement the General Fisheries Commission for the Mediterranean

Chapter 22: Regional policy and coordination of structural instruments 
Regional policy is the EU’s main tool for reducing regional disparities and investing in 
sustainable and inclusive socio -economic growth. It is operating through ‘‘ shared 
management’’ between the Commission and EU Member States. The implementation of 
cohesion policy programmes requires appropriate administrative capacity on programme and 
project level, the establishment of systems of sound financial management and control and 
also the fulfilment of other EU acquis elements such as environmental or public procurement 
legislation.  
Regarding the legislative framework , Albania has established a framework aligned with EU 
requirements, including the NUTS classification. However, this framework is currently not 
used for territorial analysis or evidence- based policy design. The Council of Ministers has 
not yet adopted a decision on the establishment of the partnership principle in line with the 
European Code of Conduct on Partnership. Albania progressed in its readiness to meet the 
enabling conditions of the Common Provisions Regulation (CPR) with the update of the 
planning for required investments in water and wastewater sectors. The updated Chapter 22

European Grouping of Territorial Cooperation legislation should advance. 
Regarding the institutional framework, staff turnover and organisational changes, especially 
with SASPAC as the Technical Secretariat to the Strategic Planning Committee, have

On administrative capacity,  progress was made in strengthening staff retention within the

Chapter 33: Financial and budgetary provisions 
This Chapter covers the rules governing the funding of the EU budget (‘own resources’). These 
resources mainly consist of: (i) contributions based on the gross national income of each 
Member State; (ii) customs duties; (iii) the non-recycled plastic resource and (iv) a resource 
based on value-added tax. Member States must have the appropriate administrative capacity 
to adequately coordinate and ensure the correct calculation, collection, payment and control of 
own resources. 
On traditional own resources, Albania has continued to implement the 2024- 2027 Medium-
term revenue strategy (MTRS) and the 2024- 2027 Strategy on public finance management. 
Implementation of the MTRS includes reform measures in both tax policy review and tax and 
customs administration efficiency with the aim of an additional increase of revenues by 
1.8% of GDP at the end of 2027. To sustain revenue mobilisation, increase the government 
revenue-to-GDP ratio, and reduce tax evasion, a continued implementation of the MTRS,

good governance, critical raw materials and the EU integration process.  These programmes 
are complemented by a significant package of multi- country programmes. The rural 
development programme IPARD III (EUR  94 million EU contribution) has not yet started, due 
to the ongoing entrustment process. Albania also participates in five cross- border 
cooperation programmes, transnational cooperation programmes and EU programmes. 
Under the Reform and Growth Facility (RGF) , Albania’s indicative maximum allocation 
amounts to EUR 922.1 million. Disbursement of EU funds under the RGF is conditional on 
compliance with the preconditions, general conditions and on delivery of reforms set out in 
the Reform Agenda. As of May 2026, EUR 212.8 million (including pre -financing) in total 
have been released for Albania. This funding comprises both grants and loans, with a 
majority portion being channelled directly through the WBIF and used for infrastructure 
projects in the country . The release of the funding is based on the achievement of 33 out of 
66 reform steps due in this period.  
Albania has signed cooperation agreements with all EU justice and home affairs agencies of 
the EU. Albania participates in two transnational cooperation programmes. Albania 
participates with IPA support in these EU programmes: Erasmus+, Creative Europe (Culture 
and Media strands), Horizon Europe and the European Cooperation in Science and 
Technology (COST –  Agency funded under Horizon Europe), Customs 2020, Fiscalis 2020, 
Single Market Programme, Justice Programme, Citizens, Equality, Rights and Values 
Programme (CERV), EU Fundamental Rights Agency (FRA)  (as an observer), the Digital 
Europe programme, Union Civil Protection Mechanism, European Solidarity Corps (youth 
component - volunteering). To improve the sustainability in local public finances and to 
enhance the implementation of effective green budgeting practices, Albania is participating 
as an observer in a pilot project under the Technical Support Instrument. 
As of April 2026, the Western Balkans Investment Framework  (WBIF) has supported Albania 
to the amount of approximately EUR 479 million, in the form of IPA III grants for flagship 
projects in the areas of clean energy, the environment and climate, the digital future, and 
sustainable transport. Western Balkans Investment Framework  has also supported 
additional 5 infrastructure projects with a total contribution of EUR 80 million financed under 
the Reform and Growth Facility. 
The EU provides support via IPA and the Foreign Policy Instrument to strengthen Western 
Balkans partners’ resilience in facing hybrid threats, including cyber and foreign information 
manipulation and interference.
