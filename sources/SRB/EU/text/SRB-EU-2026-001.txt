1. INTRODUCTION1 
1.1. CONTEXT 
Since Serbia’s accession negotiations opened in January 2014, 22 out of the 35 negotiating 
chapters have been opened, including all chapters under cluster 1 (fundamentals) and 
cluster 4 (green agenda and sustainable connectivity). Two chapters have provisionally been 
closed. The Commission’s assessment from previous years, i.e. that Serbia has fulfilled the 
opening benchmarks for cluster 3 (competitiveness and inclusive growth) and that this 
cluster is technically ready to be opened, remains valid. The overall pace of the negotiations 
will continue to depend on progress on the rule of law and on the normalisation of Serbia’s 
relations with Kosovo*. 
EU membership remains the strategic goal of the Serbian government, which has set up a 
new operational team led by a Chief Negotiator to speed up progress on the technical 
criteria for membership. Meeting Serbia’s objectives for EU accession negotiations requires 
strong political will, the implementation of necessary reforms, a whole -of-society approach, 
effective policy planning and coordination, along with sufficient human and financial 
resources for EU accession across all institutions involved. The anti- EU narrative evident not 
only in Serbian media outlets but also used by political office holders, including at highest 
levels, remains. As a matter of priority, the Serbian authorities should take much more 
responsibility for proactively and objectively communicating on Serbia’s EU accession 
process and on the EU, and for countering disinformation and information manipulation.  
The funeral of convicted war criminal Ratko Mladić that took place in September 2026 with 
official support and in presence of senior officials and military contributed to glorification of 
a convicted war criminal in a manner contradicting the values underpinning Serbia’s path 
towards the European Union. 
Mass student and public protests continued across the country over allegations of 
government corruption and the perceived lack of accountability and transparency, 
particularly in relation to the 2024 Novi Sad station canopy collapse. The incident and the 
aftermath have further deepened the polarisation of Serbian society. 
Early parliamentary elections were held on 25 October 2026. According to preliminary 
results, the “Student List” which grew out of the mass students and citizens movement, 
obtained an absolute majority of mandates.  
The EU -facilitated Dialogue on normalisation of relations between Kosovo and Serbia 
continued during the reporting period. Domestic political preoccupations on both sides 
hindered decisive steps towards implementing the 2023 Agreement on the Path to 
Normalisation and its implementation annex and thereby progressing on normalising their

1.2. MAIN FINDINGS OF THE REPORT 
CLUSTER 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS OF THE ACCESSION PROCESS 
Democracy 
Tangible improvements are needed in the way elections are conducted. It remains vital that 
outstanding recommendations by the Organisation for Security and Cooperation in Europe 
(OSCE) / Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR) and the Council of 
Europe bodies are fully implemented in a transparent and inclusive manner and well ahead 
of new elections. The adoption of six electoral laws in May, July, and August 2026 marked a 
step forward in this regard, however not all of ODIHR’s key recommendations were 
implemented. The full implementation of those laws as well as further reforms are needed, 
in particular to prevent misuse of office, misuse of state resources, intimidation and 
pressure on voters, and to ensure the safety of media professionals. Serbia also needs to 
ensure alignment with all relevant EU acquis  in this area, including Regulation (EU) 2024/900 
on the transparency and targeting of political advertising, and taking into account European 
and international standards. 
Parliament’s effectiveness and oversight functions remain hampered by the low frequency 
of parliamentary sessions, the lack of genuine political debate, and the absence of an annual 
work plan for Parliament. Public scrutiny and involvement in the legislative process are 
limited while parliamentary debates continue to be marked by tensions between the ruling 
coalition and the opposition. Parliamentary rules of procedure and the Parliament’s code of 
conduct for punishing offences by parliamentarians need to be applied consistently. 
As regards governance, Serbia’s political debate and decision -making continued to be de 
facto steered by the President in a manner not intended by the Constitution. Local 
government reform continues to advance slowly. 
The institutions in charge of the EU integration process are in place. An operational team for 
EU accession was established in January 2026, replacing the previous coordination 
structure. A new chief negotiator was appointed in March 2026. The current deep societal 
polarisation and persistent anti-EU rhetoric run counter to the authorities’ stated objective of 
EU accession. The authorities are not yet managing to communicate more proactively and 
objectively about the EU and Serbia’s accession process.  
Civil society , including human rights defenders, continued to operate in a restricted 
environment and  have faced increasing pressure, smear campaigns and attacks, including

Public administration reform 
Serbia is  moderately prepared in the area of public administration reform. However, there 
was backsliding during the reporting period. While reforms on public investment 
management are ongoing, this was overshadowed by amendments to the government rules 
of procedure which lowered the standards for public consultations, while government 
decrees exempted European integration- related draft regulations and public policy 
documents from mandatory impact assessments. The excessive number of senior civil 
servant positions occupied in an acting capacity further increased. In April 2026, the Director 
of the Public Policy Secretariat was dismissed, without sound justification. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were mostly not implemented and 
remain valid. In the coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ reduce the excessive and increasing number of senior civil servant positions occupied in 
an acting capacity and allocate sufficient resources to ensure effective, transparent and 
merit-based recruitment processes; 
→ strengthen the mechanism for systematically integrating opinions issued by the Public 
Policy Secretariat into draft laws and policy documents, in line with the Law on the 
planning system, and reconsider the exemption from impact assessments for European 
integration-related draft regulations and public policy documents; 
→ continue public investment management reforms and put in place a unified, 
comprehensive and transparent system for capital investment planning and 
management, in line with the Reform Agenda. 
Chapter 23: Judiciary and fundamental rights 
Serbia has reached some level of preparation and has made no progress.  
Functioning of the judiciary 
Serbia is between having some and a moderate level of preparation in the area of judiciary. 
No progress took place during the reporting period. While in August 2026, Serbia adopted 
amendments to the Law on Judicial Academy, implementing the recommendations of the 
Venice Commission, undue political pressure on the judiciary and the prosecution increased 
significantly with little to no follow- up from the relevant institutions. Concerns about 
prosecutorial autonomy persist. Although a case management system has been finalised for 
the prosecutors’ offices, Serbia does not have a comprehensive court case management 
system. Moreover, while new judges have been appointed, overall, a considerable number of 
vacancies of judges and prosecutors remain to be filled, including the remaining pending 
appointments to the Constitutional Court. Serbia has yet to show genuine commitment to 
investigating and adjudicating war crimes cases, including by appointing a Chief Public 
Prosecutor for War Crimes. 
In addition, in January 2026, the Parliament adopted legislative amendments on several laws 
governing the judiciary and prosecution by urgent procedure without a transparent and 
inclusive consultation process. According to an ex-post  assessment in April 2026 by the

Venice Commission this constituted the removal of some of the safeguards designed to 
protect prosecutorial autonomy and judicial independence introduced through constitutional 
amendments in 2023. In May and June 2026, Serbia adopted subsequent legislative 
amendments and took other non -legislative measures, which the Venice Commission 
assessed as addressing a considerable part of its recommendations. Additional prosecutors 
were also seconded to the Prosecutor’s Office for Organised Crime (POOC). Two Venice

valid. In the coming year, Serbia should in particular:

High Judicial Council and the High Prosecutorial Council, the government and 
Parliament defend judicial independence and prosecutorial autonomy effectively and 
proactively in cases of undue influence; 
→ make the uniform and centralised case management systems operational; improve the 
efficiency of the justice system, in particular by reducing the backlogs and disposition 
times for the Administrative and Constitutional Courts and fully implement the human 
resources and ICT strategies. 
Fight against corruption 
Serbia is between having some and a moderate level of preparation  in the fight against 
corruption. Overall, it has made limited progress. In its Second Compliance Report of 18 
August 2026, GRECO reported that Serbia had satisfactorily addressed only three of 24 
recommendations under its fifth evaluation round. Implementing some of the GRECO and/or 
ODIHR recommendations, Serbia amended the laws on lobbying, corruption prevention, and 
political financing. However, the latter two did not address all ODIHR recommendations. 
Implementation of these laws will need to be closely monitored to assess their 
effectiveness. Overall, corruption is prevalent in many areas and remains an issue of 
concern. The widespread public protests prompted by the canopy collapse at Novi Sad 
railway station in 2024 continued, with demonstrators expressing concerns about corruption 
as well as the perceived lack of accountability and transparency in public infrastructure and 
construction projects. The corruption investigations launched following the canopy collapse 
at the Novi Sad railway station have yet to be completed. In June 2026, Serbia adopted a 
new 2026- 2028 action plan for implementing the national anti- corruption strategy. 
Nevertheless, there is a need for stronger political will to tackle corruption effectively, 
establish a robust track record and put in place a strong criminal justice response, including 
action on high- level corruption. The role and mandate of the Prosecutor’s Office for 
Organised Crime (POOC) is insufficiently regulated. Operational obstacles as well as 
attempts to unduly influence the POOC increased further during the reporting period. A 
majority of the activities of the previous anti-corruption action plan 2024-2026 have not been 
achieved, with most of them carried over to the new action plan. Also the specific anti -
corruption measures from the action plan related to the most vulnerable sectors are still to 
be implemented to tackle corruption more effectively. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were implemented only to a limited 
extent and remain largely valid. In the coming year, Serbia should in particular:

→ improve its track record on investigations, prosecutions and final convictions in high -
level corruption cases, in particular the seizure and confiscation of criminal assets and

Fundamental rights 
Although Serbia’s institutional framework for upholding fundamental rights is broadly in 
place, the implementation of this framework needs to be improved as a matter of priority. 
Independent bodies have issued only little, or late, public reaction to human rights violations 
in the context of protests and other sensitive cases. While protests have been allowed to 
take place, the safety of participants has not always been guaranteed. Moreover, several 
violent incidents against demonstrators and journalists ha ve been recorded. To date, only a 
few perpetrators have been prosecuted. As regards reports of excessive use of force by the 
police during protests, cases are ongoing although none of the police officers charged have 
been convicted so far. In relation to the European Court of Human Rights’ interim measure 
regarding the alleged use of a sonic weapon for crowd control at demonstrations in 
Belgrade in March 2025, the Belgrade Higher Public Prosecutor’s Office stated in September 
2026 that no sound cannon or LRAD device had been used. The announcement that 
individuals would be prosecuted for engaging in public debate on the alleged use of sonic 
weapons has had a chilling effect on freedom of expression and open debate in a 
democratic society. 
While new action plans have been adopted in the areas of anti-discrimination, Roma 
inclusion, de-institutionalisation and national minorities, other Commission 
recommendations from last year have not been implemented and remain valid. In the 
coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ amend the legal framework related to freedom of assembly and ensure its enforcement 
in line with European standards and case law, including interim measures; 
→ demonstrate progress in addressing the recommendations of the Committee for the 
Prevention of Torture and Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishments (CPT) and 
ensure effective investigations of cases of police ill-treatment; 
→ allocate sufficient resources and effectively implement action plans in the area of 
fundamental rights, notably personal data protection, anti -discrimination including rights 
of LGBTIQ+ persons and of people with minority racial or ethnic background, gender 
equality, violence against women, violence against children, rights of persons with 
disabilities, victims of crime, Roma 3 and other national minorities; actively counter hate 
offences and build up a track record of investigations and convictions.

Serbia is between an early stage of preparation and having some level of preparation in the 
area of freedom of expression. There was further backsliding during the reporting period, 
with the environment for journalists, media professionals and outlets continuing to 
deteriorate, media legislation remaining inconsistently implemented, and the media market

change of ownership, and at the time of the electoral campaign, raise questions about 
pressure on the editorial independence of its m edia outlets. Cases of strategic lawsuits 
against public participation (SLAPP) persist, increasingly hindering the work of investigative 
media and CSOs. Since November 2024, there had been no Council of the Regulatory body 
for electronic media (REM). However, in July 2026, the process of electing a new REM

expanding the list of media outlets to be subject to more comprehensive and systematic 
monitoring of the electoral campaign, nor to rule on complaints regarding violations of 
media regulation during the campaign. In its statement of preliminary findings, the 
international election observation mission (IEOM) found that “overall, the REM failed to 
provide effective oversight and timely remedies for election- related violations, which 
contributed to the advantage of incumbency and was at odds with international standards 
and good practice.” IEOM also found that “all monitored broadcasters failed to provide 
voters with the balanced and substantive information necessary to make an informed 
choice. The systematic imbalance in exposure and treatment undermined legally prescribed 
equal campaign opportunities.”  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were not implemented and remain 
valid. In the coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ implement media legislation in a way that respects both its letter and spirit, including on 
the independence of the media regulator, and amend it, through a transparent and 
inclusive process, to align with the European Media Freedom Act and related European 
standards; 
→ strengthen the protection and safety of journalists, notably by ensuring that: (i) high -level 
officials stop verbal attacks on journalists and civil society representatives involving 
intimidation, stigmatisation and de -legitimisation; and (ii) swiftly follow up any threats, 
cases of physical and verbal violence or spyware attacks and publicly condemn, 
investigate or prosecute any such cases, as appropriate; 
→ ensure transparent and equitable co -funding for media content serving the public 
interest, and full transparency in media ownership and advertising. 
Chapter 24: Justice, freedom and security 
Serbia is between having some and a moderate level of preparation in the field of justice, 
freedom and security and has made limited progress in this area. Serbia continued to 
contribute to the management of mixed migration flows to the EU by playing an active and 
constructive role and by cooperating effectively with its neighbours, EU Member States and 
EU agencies. The Law on internal affairs remains to be amended to strengthen the 
operational autonomy of the police from the Ministry of the Interior during the pre -
investigation and investigation phases. Serbia adopted a Law on weapons and ammunition, 
as well as a new strategy and action plan for the control of small arms and light weapons. 
There was no progress on visa alignment in 2025 and Serbia has visa -free arrangements 
with 12 countries on the EU’s list of non- EU countries whose nationals require a visa to enter 
the EU. Serbia’s granting of citizenship and, by extension, EU visa -free travel, to Russian 
nationals, continue to pose potential security risks to the EU.

Fight against serious and organised crime 
Serbia has some level of preparation and has made limited  progress in the fight against 
serious and organised crime. Serbia notably adopted a new Law on preventing and 
combating trafficking in human beings and on protecting victims, in April 2026, as well as a 
Law on weapons and ammunition and amendments to the Criminal Code to effectively 
criminalise the trafficking of weapons,  in July 2026. The authorities also continued to 
cooperate actively with the European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Training, Eurojust, 
Europol and Interpol, notably on drugs trafficking and the fight against organised crime 
groups. However, no steps were taken to phase out the role of the security services in 
investigations of serious and organised crime cases. Deterioration of cooperation between 
the Prosecutor’s Office for Organised Crime and the police and the large- scale personnel 
changes within the  police hindered investigations. The Law on the organisation and 
jurisdiction of state authorities in the suppression of organised crime, terrorism and 
corruption has not yet been amended, and the draft operational plan for financial 
investigations and for the seizure and confiscation of proceeds of criminal offences, as well 
as the Law on seizure and confiscation of the proceeds of crime remain to be adopted. 
Serbia has also not finalised the signature of the Memorandum of Cooperation with the 
European Public Prosecutor’s Office and cooperation with Eurojust was disrupted by delays 
in renewing the secondment of a Serbian liaison prosecutor to Eurojust and in signing joint 
investigation team agreements.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented and remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ further improve the efficiency of prosecuting serious and organised crime cases, 
including money laundering cases and the seizure and confiscation of criminal assets by 
the systematic tracking of money flows, effective use of financial investigations and 
special investigative measures, and systematic use of extended confiscation, while 
ensuring sufficient investigative and prosecutorial capacity and resources; 
→ implement the amendments to the Criminal Code to effectively criminalise the trafficking 
of weapons, implement the new Law on weapons and ammunition, and step up the fight

ECONOMIC CRITERIA 
The existence of a functioning market economy 
Serbia has a good level of preparation  but has made limited progress in developing a 
functioning market economy. Economic growth slowed considerably in 2025 due to 
domestic political instability and global uncertainties affecting economic confidence, 
investment and exports, before rebounding in the first half of 2026. The current account 
deficit increased slightly in 2025 and was no longer covered by foreign direct investment 
(FDI) inflows, which halved compared to 2024. The 2025 budget deficit turned out lower 
than planned and monetary policy remained relatively tight, which helped inflation stay in the 
target band. The banking sector remained stable amid increased lending. Serbia continued 
to postpone the full application of certain fiscal rules and the reform of the public sector 
wage system faced further delays. Limited progress was made in improving budget 
transparency. State aid rules are not always applied to financial support for state -owned 
enterprises (SOEs) and large foreign investors. SOEs continue to have a disproportionately 
large influence on the economy. The business environment is burdened by excessive red 
tape, rule of law weaknesses, and public administration inefficiency and lack of 
transparency. The government interfered significantly in the retail market by introducing a 
margin cap for a wide variety of goods from 1  September 2025 until February 2026. While 
intended to benefit consumers by lowering inflation, the measure risked causing market 
distortions and harming the business environment. 
Last year’s recommendations were addressed to some extent but remain mostly valid. To 
improve the functioning of the market economy, Serbia should in particular: 
→ keep an appropriately tight policy stance to maintain price stability amid the global 
energy price shock, including by containing current spending; 
→ move forward with reforming of the public sector wage system; 
→ strengthen SOE governance to reduce the associated fiscal risks, notably in the energy 
sector. 
The capacity to cope with competitive pressure and market forces within the Union 
Serbia is between having a moderate and a good level of preparation  and has made limited 
progress in coping with competitive pressure and market forces in the EU. The 
manufacturing and service sectors are well established, bolstered by close economic links 
with the EU. However, educational outcomes remain below the OECD average as highlighted 
in the 2025 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) and public funding for 
education and research is insufficient compared to the EU average. Infrastructure gaps are 
pronounced in certain areas, despite increased public investment. Digital transformation is 
progressing well. SMEs continue to encounter challenges, including an unfair competitive 
environment, when compared to large enterprises and foreign investors. Wage growth 
outperformed productivity growth, and the real exchange rate continued to apprec iate, 
putting pressure on Serbia’s cost competitiveness. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year have been partially implemented. Over 
the coming year, Serbia should in particular:

→ improve educational outcomes and skills, including by increasing enrolment rates, 
modernising vocational education and training, improving private sector consultation, 
and stepping up lifelong learning; 
→ harmonise public investment management, including by putting in place a single,

Chapter 5: Public procurement 
Serbia is moderately prepared in the area of public procurement. It made no progress in the 
reporting period. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were not implemented so remain valid. 
In the coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ further align its legislation with the 2014 EU directives on public procurement, in 
particular by amending the Law on public -private partnerships and concessions, and by 
ensuring that projects are subject to public procurement procedures; 
→ ensure that procurement rules under intergovernmental agreements with third countries 
comply with the public procurement principles, in line with the EU acquis; 
→ further strengthen the institutional capacity of the Public Procurement Office, the 
Commission for Public-Private Partnerships and Concessions, the Republic Commission 
for the Protection of Rights in Public Procedures and the Administrative Court. 
Chapter 18 - Statistics 
Serbia has a good level of preparation in the area of statistics.  Good progress was made 
during the reporting period, with the adoption of the new Law on official statistics, and 
improvements in the compilation of macroeconomic statistics, in line with the European 
System of Accounts (ESA 2010). 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were largely implemented. In the 
coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ continue to improve the compilation of macroeconomic statistics in line with the 
European System of Accounts (ESA 2010); 
→ continue efforts to strengthen the administrative capacity of the Statistical Office of the 
Republic of Serbia (SORS) to produce statistics in accordance with the EU acquis  and to 
meet other national needs. 
Chapter 32: Financial control

the reporting period, notably by keeping up the implementation of the managerial 
accountability roadmap and by directing efforts on improving the consistency of the Public 
Internal Financial Control (PIFC)’s legal basis with the general legal framework.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were partially implemented but remain 
largely valid. In the coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ strengthen risk management and the reporting of irregularities, notably by expanding the 
use of risk registers;

Serbia has between a moderate and a good level of preparation  in education and culture.

Chapter 31 - Foreign, security and defence policy 
Serbia is moderately prepared in the field of foreign, security and defence policy.  Overall, it 
made no progress over the reporting period, aligning only partially with the common foreign 
and security policy (CFSP). Serbia continued to resort to retroactive alignment. Serbia 
retroactively aligned with some Council decisions related to Russian individuals, Iran, and 
some EU Statements on Ukraine, which represents a positive change in terms of patterns of 
alignment. Nevertheless, some of Serbia’s actions and statements went against key EU 
positions, especially on Russia and China. It is currently not aligning with the E U’s restrictive 
measures against Russia, Belarus and Iran taken in the context of Russia’s war of 
aggression against Ukraine. Combined with recurrent anti- EU narratives, this raised 
questions about Serbia’s strategic orientation. 
High-level and frequent bilateral contacts with Russia continued, including in the context of 
Serbia’s continued dependence on Russian energy. Notwithstanding the fact that Serbia has 
not yet aligned with EU sanctions against Russia related to its war of aggression against 
Ukraine, Serbia is cooperating with the EU on countering sanctions circumvention and has 
provided substantial support to and intensified its cooperation with Ukraine, including 
through high- level exchanges. Serbia continues to contribute substantially to EU crisis 
management missions and operations under the Common Security and Defence Policy 
(CSDP). 
During the reporting period, Serbia deepened its comprehensive strategic partnership with 
China. The procurement of supersonic air- to-surface precision strike missiles from China, 
which are mounted to Russian -produced fighter jets, deepened Serbia’s strategic 
dependencies further and in a manner inconsistent with its commitment to the EU path. 
The recommendations issued in 2025 were not implemented and remain valid. In the 
coming year, Serbia should in particular: 
→ improve, as a matter of priority, its alignment with the EU common foreign and security

2. CLUSTER 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS OF THE ACCESSION PROCESS 
2.1. FUNCTIONING OF DEMOCRATIC INSTITUTIONS AND PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION 
2.1.1. Democracy 
Sustained political polarisation at both national and local level continues to pose a challenge 
to the functioning of democratic institutions. Less frequent mass student and citizen 
protests continued across the country during the reporting period. There were cases of 
violent incidents and attacks against students, activists and peaceful protesters, with 
institutions such as the police failing to take follow-up action in most cases. There were also 
some violent incidents against pro -government gatherings. The police entered the Rectorate 
of the University of Novi Sad in September 2025 and remained on site for several weeks. In 
March 2026 following the death of a student, the police raided the premises of Belgrade 
University, including its Rectorate. In March 2026, on the day of partial local elections, the 
main office of an opposition party in Belgrade was raided. In several cases where activists, 
media workers and domestic election observers were attacked, the police were present but, 
according to local NGO observers, failed to react. There have been very few public report s of 
measures being taken against officers allegedly involved in cases of excessive use of force 
by the police. State officials continue to frame protests as being part of a foreign backed 
‘colour revolution’, allegedly aimed at overthrowing the constitutional order of Serbia, without 
substantiating evidence. The announced interrogation and prosecution of individuals 
engaging in public debate on the alleged use of sonic weapons at the 15 March 2025 
protest, despite an interim measure issued by the European Court of Human Rights in April 
2025 in this respect, has had a chilling effect on freedom of expression and open debate. 
Elections 
The general framework regarding elections still requires further comprehensive reform and 
more efforts are needed to ensure alignment with all relevant EU acquis  in this area, 
including Regulation (EU) 2024/900 on the transparency and targeting of political 
advertising, taking into account European and international democratic standards.  
Several laws aiming to implement some of OSCE/ODIHR’s recommendations were adopted 
throughout the reporting period. Following an amendment to the Law on the unified voter 
register which was adopted in November 2025, a Commission for auditing the voter register

On 7 September 2026, upon proposal of the government, the President of Serbia dissolved 
the Parliament and called for early parliamentary elections which were held on 25 October 
2026. The President resigned as president on 27 September. The former President Vučić 
then ran as the first candidate on the list of the ruling SNS party and for the post of Prime 
Minister. The elections were observed by an international election observation mission 
(IEOM), which included a full -scale OSCE/ODIHR Election Observation  Mission deployed in 
September, the European Parliament, the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe 
and the OSCE Parliamentary Assembly. As per its preliminary findings and conclusions, “the 
25 October early parliamentary elections saw active citizen engagement with high voter 
turnout and vigorous competition between two main contenders, but unfolded in an 
atmosphere of profound polarization, with the equality of campaign opportunities seriously 
compromised.” The management of the election preparat ions was deemed as “overall 
efficient” and the assessment of all stages of the election day process in the vast majority of 
polling stations as “positive”. However, according to IEOM elements, such as entrenched 
advantages enjoyed by the ruling party, including misuse of state resources, “pronounced 
bias in monitored media coverage in favour of the incumbent party, intimidation and 
pressure on voters, candidates, and civil society, as well as disruptions of campaign 
activities marred the electoral environment. Reported incidents of physical violence also 
raised serious concerns and some procedural shortcomings were noted.” Widespread 
mistrust in the integrity of the voter register persisted.   
Regular local elections in several municipalities also took place in November 2025 and 
March 2026. They were marked by irregularities, an uneven playing field, reports of violence, 
attacks on observers, journalists and representatives of political parties, and a failure by 
police to act. Pressure on female activists, including gender -based digital violence, 
increased in the run- up to local elections in some municipalities in March 2026. The CoE 
Congress of regional and local authorities’ observers of the Ma rch elections noted that while 
procedures inside polling stations were largely in line with legal provisions, acts of violence 
and the threatening presence of unidentified and sometimes masked groups were

the December 2023 early parliamentary and local elections. 
Despite the introduction of measures to achieve a more balanced representation, including a

Parliament 
Parliament’s effectiveness, autonomy and transparency still need to be strengthened. The 
effective exercise of parliamentary powers is constrained by parliamentary sessions often 
being called as late as legally possible, the absence of an annual work plan, and an agenda 
almost entirely driven by the government and the ruling party. The 2026 regular spring sitting 
started with a 45- day delay. Plenary and committee sessions continued to be marked by 
tensions and offensive behaviour, including from some minist ers. Furthermore, the Code of 
Conduct was not applied consistently, with fines being mostly imposed on opposition MPs. 
At times, the Speaker has engaged in direct exchanges with the opposition and failed to 
provide impartial rulings in line with the procedural rules of the parliament. 
Most laws adopted by Parliament originated from the government. Still, a number of 
problematic judicial laws was adopted on the motion of one ruling MP without prior public 
consultation or consultation of the European Commission. Furthermore, some important 
electoral laws were tabled by a ruling party MP, which had an impact on the transparency 
and inclusiveness of the legislative process. The practice of calling sessions at short notice 
and of amalgamating unrelated agenda items continued. The State budget  was debated 
alongside 57 other items in December 2025 while the pre -election budget revision was 
considered alongside 58 separate items in August 2026. A no -confidence motion from 
February 2026 was placed on the agenda in July 2026, despite rules stipulating that it should 
have been debated at the next parliamentary session after it was tabled. Only one session 
for MP questions to the government was held in the course of 2026, despite clear rules for 
such sessions to be regularly held. The use of urgent procedure increased to 18% between 
September 2025 and September 2026. 
EU integration 
In January 2026, Serbia established an operational team for EU accession, replacing the 
previous broader coordination structures. In March 2026, the Head of Mission of the 
Republic of Serbia to the EU was appointed as chief negotiator and head of the operational 
team. While this served to consolidate the operational leadership, it blurred the division of 
responsibilities between the Minister for European Integration and the chief negotiator. 
Under the new set up, technical work on legislative alignment and on planning accelerated 
although it is yet to be matched by genuine political commitment to meet all criteria for EU 
membership. The national programme for the adoption of the EU acquis (NPAA), last 
amended in August 2025, remains the central planning instrument, supported by quarterly 
and annual monitoring reports. However, implementation challenges persist and recent 
monitoring cycles show only a partial fulfilment of planned legislative measures. The new 
operational team has developed its own parallel planning for meeting the outstanding 
opening and interim benchmarks and for prioritising the legislative agenda. 
The bodies set up by Serbia to support EU accession negotiations also oversee the 
implementation of the Reform and Growth Facility, with the Minister for European Integration 
assuming the role of the national coordinator of the Reform and Growth Facility (RGF).

The Ministry of European Integration still is the formal focal point for consultations and 
engagement with civil society, the business community, local self -government and other 
stakeholders. However, the practical allocation of responsibilities has yet to be clarified 
following the establishment of the operational team. The National Convention on the EU 
(NCEU), an umbrella platform engaging CSOs in the accession process, continues to act as 
a public watchdog for the accession negotiations.  
However, since the NCEU decided to suspend its participation in processes involving 
cooperation with political decision -makers in February 2025, engagement with civil society 
has been limited and inconsistent. While cooperation continues at a technical level, notably 
within the negotiation groups and in relation to the RGF Monitoring Committee and biannual 
trilateral RGF policy dialogue meetings, civil society continues to report a deteriorating 
environment, insufficient transparency and access to informati on, and limited influence on 
decision-making, with consultations perceived as procedural rather than meaningful. 
As the Serbian government continues to declare EU membership a strategic goal, the 
national authorities must prioritise unambiguous, proactive communication about EU 
accession and implement EU-related reforms consistently and effectively. 
Governance 
Concerns persist that political debate and decision -making are de facto  steered by the 
President in a way not intended under the Constitution. Following previous pardons granted 
in 2025, the announcement of the President in September 2026 of new pardons granted to 
49 individuals raised further concerns in terms of lack of transparency and whether the 
applicable legal procedure was correctly followed. There were no changes in the 
composition of the government during the reporting period. In July 2026 the Parliament 
debated on and voted down a motion of no confidence that had been tabled by the 
opposition in February 2026.   
Legal requirements for public debate were scaled back by way of an amendment to the 
government’s rules of procedure in November 2025, while adequate conditions for genuine 
public participation were not always ensured. Some members of the government made 
inflammatory, insulting and potentially threatening statements against students and the 
student protest movement. Independent bodies must be allowed to act entirely without 
outside influence, and their recommendations must be followed up more thoroughly. 
The Prosecutor for Organised Crime initiated a criminal case against the Minister for Culture 
on the grounds of ‘irregularities during the process of removing cultural heritage status from 
the army's general staff building. The case is ongoing; the Minister did not invoke immunity 
from proceedings as a member of government. 
In the area of local self -government, the Law on financing the autonomous province of 
Vojvodina has still not been adopted despite a constitutional requirement to do so. Local 
administrative capacity is limited, with significant disparities between municipalities. The 
programme to reform the local government system (2021 -2025) was implemented to a 
moderate extent. The Commission for Financing Local Self -Governments has not met since 
2022, in breach of its legal obligations. 
Civil society 
Civil society, including human rights defenders,  has been subject to increased pressure and 
attacks, including from high- level officials, further impacting what already was a restricted 
civic space. Smear campaigns have been orchestrated by the tabloids and government -
organised non- governmental organisations (GONGOs) . The  police search of four CSO 
offices in February 2025 has resulted in no indictments so far and was widely perceived as 
an act of intimidation against civil society. CSOs have not been informed of the outcome of

a request made in January 2025 by the Administration for the Prevention of Money 
Laundering for the confidential bank data of five CSO employees.  There have been new 
reports of spyware attacks targeting human rights defenders, student activists and 
opposition politicians, but little judicial follow- up so far . Strategic lawsuits against public 
participation (SLAPPs) persist, increasingly hindering the work of civil society actors. In this 
overall context of civil society environment, formal cooperation with the authorities has not 
resumed, except for their participation as members of the RGF Monitoring Committee in a

implementation of Serbia’s strategy and action plan for civil society.  There have been no 
specific improvements in the transparency and fairness of public funding for CSOs. 
According to public data collected by investigative journalists, some public funds have been 
allocated to ghost organisations or GONGOs.  For example, a case filed by CSOs back in 
2023 on alleged corruption in public calls implemented by the Ministry of Demography and 
Family Care is still in the pre-investigative stage. 
Civilian oversight of the security and intelligence sector 
The Parliamentary Committee for the Oversight of Security Services remains responsible for 
civilian oversight of the security sector. The Committee reviews periodic activity reports by 
the civilian and military security agencies and by the Inspector -General of the Ministry of 
Defence. There have been reports that the civilian security agency (BIA) summoned 
students, activists and prosecutors for informative talks. The Law on access to state 
security files is still to be adopted. 
2.1.2. Public administration reform 
Strategic framework for public administration reform (PAR) 
A comprehensive PAR strategy (2021- 2030) is in place, however implementation remains 
inconsistent and needs to be improved. The new PAR action plan 2026 -2030 was adopted in 
September 2026. Underlying reform programmes for 2026- 2030 have not yet been adopted, 
with the exception of the Public Finance Management Reform Programme, which was 
adopted in May 2026. The 2025 PAR implementation report shows slow progress overall 
with 49% of measures and 51% of activities implemented. Monitoring reports are still bein g 
adopted too late to provide timely information to decision makers. PAR coordination 
mechanisms at political and administrative levels are formally in place, but their impact is 
limited due to the lack of clear ownership and political commitment. Financial sustainability 
remains an issue of concern due to reliance on donor funding. 
Policy development and coordination 
The regulatory framework for policy development and coordination has been weakened by 
the fact that quality assurance checks were further reduced, undermining evidence -based 
policymaking. In March 2026, implementing legislation was amended, exempting all 
European integration-related draft regulations and public policy documents from mandatory 
impact assessments –  which is against EU standards on better regulation –  and from the 
opinion of the Public Policy Secretariat. The streamlining of sectoral strategic  frameworks 
remains slow, with only six out of 23 sectors optimised so far. The national development 
plan, due for adoption in 2020, is still not in place. The adoption rate of mid -term plans 
remains low (65% for the period 2026 –  2028 – as of September 2026), continuing to lag far 
behind the 2020 deadline set for central level institutions. Government decision- making 
transparency remains limited, with no publication of meeting agenda or minutes, only press 
releases providing partial information. Moreover, the strategic framework does not envisage 
any improvements. The share of published annual reports on the implementation of sectoral 
planning documents is still limited (around 60%, despite this being a legislative 
requirement).

The legal framework for public consultations  has deteriorated. In November 2025, the 
government adopted amendments to its rules of procedure, lowering the standards 
applicable to public debate by introducing several exemptions which are not in line with 
primary legislation . Public consultations were carried out in 2025 on 51% of draft laws 
(down from 58% in 2024) and on 30% of draft regulations, with no systematic follow- up. 
There is still no central body in charge of oversight and quality control of public 
consultations. Use of the e -consultations platform, although mandatory, is insufficient. Only 
32% of public consultations on legislation adopted by the government were performed using 
the platform while no more than 15% had their mandatory reports published on the platform 
(see civil society under 2.1.1 Democracy). 
Public financial management 
Public financial management (PFM) is broadly in place but needs to be improved. As per the 
strategic framework, 72.7% of the activities of the 2021- 2025 PFM reform programme were 
completed. The new PFM reform programme 2026 -2030, adopted by the government in May 
2026, is less dependent on donor funding but continues to lack ambition, notably with regard 
to budget planning, execution and public investment management. Despite a well -defined 
methodological framework, the link between strategic priorities and budget programmes 
remains inconsistent. Programme budgeting needs to be better integrated and 
implemented, in p articular for central government. Gender- responsive budgeting and the 
green tagging methodology are continuing to find greater application. 
A new public expenditure and financial accountability assessment (PEFA) and a Fiscal 
Transparency Evaluation were conducted in 2025. The latter assessed Serbia as advanced 
in fiscal reporting, forecasting and budgeting, while weaknesses were recorded in fiscal risk 
management. Macroeconomic and fiscal forecasting remain adequate. On budget 
management, budget revenue and expenditure are realistically forecast. Fiscal risks 
monitoring continues to improve, with risks identified in the medium -term fiscal strategy. 
However, the Ministry of Finance still does not publish a stand -alone and comprehensive 
fiscal risk statement. With full implementation of the general fiscal rules having been 
postponed, fiscal policy credibility may be undermined. The relatively large contingency 
budgetary reserve lacks sufficient accountability, as it continues to be used without a clear 
purpose and transparent reporting. 
The financial autonomy of local self -government units remains highly dependent on 
intergovernmental transfers and has been affected by a decrease in the share of non-
earmarked transfers and the lack of a standardised methodology for assessing their 
financial needs. 
While the country has yet to develop a genuine single pipeline for capital investment 
management, reforms of public investment management are ongoing. Existing exemptions 
are being addressed with the gradual inclusion of projects of special importance in the 
standard procedure. However, public -private partnerships continue to be managed 
separately without transparent data on their total costs and obligations. While a Serbia 2030 
strategy has been announced, there is no public development plan and no investment plan. 
Limited progress was made in improving budget transparency.  Both the 2025 and 2026 
budgets were adopted in line with the budget calendar, but the qualitative assessment and 
debate among stakeholders should be substantially improved.  A public hearing on the draft 
budget law was held a day before the start of the parliamentary debate and with limited 
public participation. A citizen’s budget portal was introduced in October 2025, with a 
mechanism for the public to submit proposals and comments. Budget oversight by the 
legislature needs to improve. As noted in the Open Budget Survey 2025, a mid -year report 
has still to be produced and published. Budget execution reports contain basic information 
and do not analyse divergence from set targets. Inconsistencies in the accounting and

Public service and human resources management 
The regulatory framework of public service and human resources management (HRM) is 
satisfactory, but its effectiveness continues to be undermined by a lack of consistent 
enforcement. The horizontal and vertical scope of the civil service is adequate, except for 
several (regulatory) public agencies with a higher degree of autonomy that are excluded 
from the civil service system with no justified reason. Serbia’s public service and HRM 
legislation provides for merit-based recruitment and dismissal procedures but leaves too 
much discretion to the heads of institutions in setting up selection committees. In May 
2026, the Director of the Public Policy Secretariat was dismissed without sound justification. 
Amendments to the Law on civil servants adopted in 2025 and early 2026 introduced 
positive regulatory changes with the potential to make public sector employment more 
attractive and recruitment procedures more efficient.  A requirement to assess at least one 
functional competence was incorporated into the hiring procedure for fixed -term staff. 
Around 10% of all civil servants continue to be hired on a fixed -term basis without passing a 
competition as the adoption of a legal provision to that effect has been postponed once 
again until January 2027. Administrative bodies have limited autonomy to launch new 
recruitment procedures based on their staffing plans, due to recruitment restrictions under 
the Law on the budget system. The new HRM information system is not yet operational. 
HRM units continue to have limited capacity and do not play a strategic role. No progress 
was made on salary reform aimed at ensuring equal pay for equal work in the public 
administration. Serbia maintained an effective gender balance in the public administration, 
including in top managerial positions, with 49% women and 51% men. 
The percentage of senior civil servant  posts occupied in an acting capacity is excessively 
high and increased during the reporting period (72% as of September 2026 compared with 
62% in September 2025), undermining merit -based and legally compliant appointment 
procedures and contributing to prolonged instability in senior management. Transparency in 
the appointment of candidates –  as recommended by the High Civil Service Council –
continues to be lacking, which is cause for serious concern. Moreover, most acting 
managers are not civil servants despite there being a legal provision to prevent such 
appointments. No credible steps have yet been taken or planned to address these issues. 
The National Academy for Public Administration continued to improve the scope and quality 
of training at central and local levels. Almost all (99.8%) institutions that have the obligation 
to adopt integrity plans have done so. Further efforts are needed to fully implement the 
current integrity plans for the judiciary and public administration, including at local level. 
Accountability of the administration 
The accountability of the administration still needs to be improved. No progress was made 
in addressing long -standing shortcomings in the organisation of the central state 
administration, oversight of subordinate bodies, or delegation of decision -making powers to 
lower management levels. The managerial accountability roadmap is being implemented 
slowly and with no formal mechanism to report on its implementation. According to the 
Ombudsman’s report for 2025, 61% of complaints were related to violations of economic, 
property and social rights (see Chapter 23 - Fundamental rights). On citizens’ right to access 
public information , the Commissioner for Information of Public Importance and Personal 
Data Protection resolved 16  602 complaints in 2025 (up from 15  282 in 2024) while 17 401 
cases remained pending. The issue of legal professionals potentially abusing legal remedies 
remains to be addressed while not jeopardising the right to access information. The 
execution rate of Commissioner decisions was 79.8% (2024: 77.98%). On citizens’ right to 
administrative justice , the establishment of a two -instance administrative justice system 
continues to be delayed. The Administrative Court received fewer cases and increased its

takes to resolve a case) remain high. The jurisdiction of the Court was extended again,

Service delivery to citizens and businesses 
Service delivery to citizens and businesses, in particular e- services, continued to improve. 
Further developments continued to be hampered by the fragmentation of responsibilities 
and mandates among institutions and the lack of a dedicated coordination system. 
Amendments to the Law on e- government were adopted in August 2026. Progress on key

place. More databases were connected to the improved government service bus (an

quality standards for service provision. The legal framework for simplifying administrative 
procedures is in place. Work to simplify and digitalise administrative procedures continued 
with the roll -out of the e -paper programme. Serbia continued to improve the portal of the 
register of administrative procedures, which now includes more than 7 500 procedures. 
 
2.2. RULE OF LAW AND FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS 
2.2.1 Chapter 23: Judiciary and fundamental rights 
The EU’s founding values include the rule of law and respect for human rights. An effective 
(independent, high -quality and efficient) judicial system and an effective fight against 
corruption are of paramount importance, as is respect of fundamental rights in law and in 
practice. 
Functioning of the judiciary 
Strategic documents 
Some strategic documents are in place, but their implementation needs to be improved. The 
main strategic documents, namely the action plan for Chapter 23 and the 2020- 2025 judicial 
development strategy, have expired. A new Judicial Development Strategy is under 
preparation but has not been adopted yet. No new action plan for Chapter 23 has been 
developed. Instead, an operational plan, only covering the adoption of legislation and 
strategic documents linked to Chapter 23 interim benchmarks, was adopted in June 2026. 
Once drawn up in line with EU standards and adopted, fu ll implementation of these laws and 
strategies will be key, also to establish a strong track record in this field. Implementation of 
the human resources strategy and the information and communications technology (ICT) 
strategy – which were both evaluated alongside the now expired judicial development 
strategy – remains weak, in part due to a lack of coordination and adequate monitoring 
mechanisms.  
Management bodies 
Management bodies are in place although their independence and capacity need to be 
improved. 
As with magistrates, members of the High Judicial Council (HJC) and High Prosecutorial 
Council (HPC) are required under the Law on the prevention of corruption to declare their 
assets and interests when taking office and must update these declarations regularly under 
the oversight of the Agency for the Prevention of Corruption. 
The blockade of the HPC exposed persistent weaknesses in its independence. Elections

delayed and subject to procedural challenges. Objections by unsuccessful candidates were

The legal framework governing the fight against corruption is in place, but needs

Freedom of expression and media freedom 
The environment for media and its free, independent and professional operation remains 
constrained. 
Intimidation of journalists 
Cases of physical attacks, threats, intimidation and hate speech against journalists have 
continued to rise, with the authorities failing to consistently condemn them . Local journalists 
and female journalists were among the worst affected by threats and attacks, in particular in 
the online space.  Recurrent verbal attacks  by high- level officials against  journalists 
continued to have a chilling effect on freedom of expression, with  674 verbal attacks by 
officials reported by civil society in the last five months of 2025. One of the main media 
associations reported that attacks, threats and pressure against journalists  had more than 
doubled in 2025 (427 cases compared with 168 in 2024), including 117 physical attacks and 
174 threats.  Several journalists have been relocated out of Serbia due to security reasons 
resulting from targeting campaigns. In 2025, the prosecution recorded the highest number 
of attacks against journalists since recording of media -related cases began. Only a few of 
the reported cases resulted in final convictions (four out of 140).  Several serious attacks 
against journalists were reported in the context of the local elections in March 2026, but 
there was no adequate response by police or follow -up, despite video reports from the sites. 
A series of documentary-style videos broadcast on a number of media services, including TV 
channels broadcast on national frequencies, contributed to a smear campaign against 
journalists without being publicly condemned by the authorities . Strategic lawsuits against 
public participation (SLAPPs) persist, increasingly hindering the work of investigative media,

database of the National Anti- SLAPP Working Group (a group comprising CSOs), compared

1994, 1999 and 2001, one of which has already been barred by the statute of limitations. In 
the case of the murder of Slavko Ćuruvija, the Supreme Court ruled that the 2024 decision of 
the Belgrade Court of Appeal was reached despite significant violations of the provisions of 
criminal procedure. However, the final acquittal remains in force and no further steps have 
been taken by the Court of Appeal to establish the potential accountability of those involved. 
In a first -instance verdict, the Slavko Ć uruvija Foundation was found guilty in a defamation

Economic factors 
Political and economic influence on the media, including on editorial policy, is a source of 
increasing concern. Following the 2023 amendment to the Law on public information and 
media that allowed state -owned companies to be  media owners, the media market has 
become more concentrated. Since a leaked phone conversation in August 2025 between the 
CEOs of a private media group and the majority state -owned Telekom Srbija regarding 
personnel changes at the former, concerns have increased about the exertion of political 
pressure on media outlets perceived as critical towards the authorities.  In February 2026, 
new governance and editorial boards of the aforementioned private media group were 
announced, and the CEO of a subsidiary company and the director of its news channel were 
dismissed. In May, an agreement on the sale of this media group was announced and, in 
August, the announced new commercial owner appointed a new director of the publisher of 
these media in Serbia while required approvals were still pending. This was followed by 
further personnel changes among this company's media, critic ised by part of their staff and 
by media associations as going against the editorial independence of these media, and 
raising questions of compliance with ‘Commission Recommendation 2022/1634 on internal 
safeguards for editorial independence and ownership transparency in the media sector’, 
notably safeguards to promote the participation of journalists in decision -making of media 
companies. Also in August, another commercial cable provider launched a pilot customer 
offer, which involved the removal of two ca ble channels perceived as critical towards the 
authorities. Several local media services, owned by  the same few economic entities, were 
launched or acquired in municipalities, including some prior  to partial local elections held in 
March 2026. The election observation mission of the Council of Europe Congress of Local 
and Regional Authorities reported that the state of local media has been adversely affected 
by concentration of ownership and self-censorship.

Freedom of assembly and association 
While Serbia provides adequate legal guarantees with respect to freedom of association, the 
legal framework governing freedom of assembly needs to be further aligned with European 
standards. Serbia has yet to align further with the joint Venice Commission and 
OSCE/ODIHR Guidelines on Freedom of Peaceful Assembly and with the Conclusions of the 
UN Human Rights Committee concerning the notification requirements in the Law on public 
assembly. Serbia also needs to comply with the case law of the ECtHR, notably Serbian-
Chinese Friendship Society FDH v. Serbia  of 2 June 2026, including the obligation to carry out 
a specific security assessment before deciding on a potential assembly ban, the obligation 
to ensure an effective domestic remedy, as well as the positive obligation to try to ensure 
the safety of all persons concerned. 
Student and civic protests  demanding accountability and more effective action against 
corruption are continuing, albeit less frequently. The Security Information Agency continued 
to hold ‘informative talks’ with protesters, which were perceived as a form of intimidation, 
and plainclothes police officers continued to arrest protesters, which sometimes made it 
difficult for protesters to identify who had arrested them. Overall, several hundred individuals 
were arrested, some of whom were detained or placed under house arrest on grounds 
including ‘invoking a violent change to the constitutional order’ , though few of those cases 
have resulted in indictments. Violent  incidents were recorded against demonstrators and 
journalists reporting on the protests, although only some of the perpetrators have been 
prosecuted so far and several others were previously pardoned from criminal prosecution by 
the President. In September 2026, the President announced further pardons of protesters 
and activists facing criminal charges as well as individuals convicted for violence against 
protesters. Those raised further concerns including in terms of a lack of transparency, and 
as to whether the applicable legal procedure was followed.  Excessive use of force by the 
police during the protests, including against journalists and minors, has been reported, with 
prosecution cases either ongoing or dismissed. There have been reports of the police 
protecting pro -government groups while failing to respond to violence committed by the 
latter, allegedly involving individuals with a criminal background. In relation to  the European 
Court of Human Rights’ interim measure concerning  the alleged use of a sonic weapon for 
crowd control at  the demonstrations in Belgrade  in March 2025, the Belgrade Higher Public 
Prosecutor’s Office stated in September 2026 that no sound cannon or LRAD device had 
been used. The announcement that individuals would be prosecuted for engaging in public 
debate on the alleged use of sonic weapons has had a chilling effect on freedom of 
expression and open debate in a democratic society.

Business environment 
Despite progress in reducing regulatory and administrative burden on businesses, the 
business environment still has weaknesses affecting small and medium -sized enterprises 
(SMEs) in particular, while shortcomings related to the rule of law and corruption remain a 
concern. The business environment is hampered by red tape, political interference, 
weaknesses in the rule of law and limited efficiency and transparency in public 
administration. SMEs are among the worst affected by this, while large companies, with 
significant transactional capacity, are less impacted. Businesses continue to be affected by 
a lack of transparency and predictability regarding the adoption of business -related 
legislation, including unexpected and non -transparent amendments to the legal framework, 
often with limited or no consultation, and reports of the burden of regulatory compliance 
being unevenly distributed. Despite the implementation of Single Euro Payments Area 
(SEPA) transactions and improvements to foreign transaction processing which have 
reduced processing times significantly, the Law on foreign exchange transactions continues 
to impose excessive administrative requirements on entities engaging in foreign exchange 
operations. Serbia ranked 116th out of 182 countries in the 2025 corruption perception index 
by Transparency International, registering its lowest score and ranking in two decades, 
behind all other Western Balkan countries. Efforts to reduce the size of the informal 
economy continued, although several factors contribute to its persistence: high taxes and 
social security contributions on low wages (which have been recently pushed up by rapid 
minimum wage increases), red tape, and shortcomings in addressing corruption. In addition, 
there is insufficient capacity in key inspection departments. A legal basis has been 
established for the creation of a register of non- tax levies; public bodies are currently in the 
process of entering these in the register. Further efforts are needed to improve the quality, 
independence and efficiency of the justice system. 
State influence on product markets 
The state systematically intervened in product markets. The margin cap, applied between 
September 2025 and February 2026, impinged on the ability of retailers and supply chains to 
operate freely, negatively influencing the business environment while failing to address 
underlying longer- term price pressures. In addition, weekly price caps continue to restrict 
market-based pricing in the fuel retail segment. Efforts to further align the legal framework 
for competition and State aid with the EU acquis continue, while the implementation of the 
framework still needs to be further aligned. The Commission for Protection of Competition 
(CPC) and the Commission for State Aid Control (CSAC) are legally independent bodies. 
However, the CSAC needs to prove its operational independence by building a credible track 
record of enforcement. State aid rules are not always implemented consistently due to 
strong political pressure to provide financial assistance to SOEs, large foreign investors and 
companies with state c onnections. Transparency over State aid schemes is often lacking, 
including in the 2026 budget. In addition, temporary and ad hoc trade restrictions continue to 
be introduced at very short notice and without clear justification, with negative impacts on 
both local and foreign companies. Public investment management and public procurement 
procedures still use exemptions from standard procedures, undermining transparency and 
fair competition. 
Privatisation and restructuring 
The significant economic presence of state -owned enterprises continues to hinder 
competition and private sector growth. In Serbia, SOEs operate in key sectors such as 
energy, transport, manufacturing, telecommunications and finance, employing a large share 
of the workforce. Their dominant presence affects resource allocation, competitiveness and 
public finances, and is also attributed to the country’s poor record on investment, innovation,

SOEs. According to a recent OECD estimate 5, the net budgetary cost of SOEs was 
approximately 0.8% of GDP in 2023, primarily due to the largest energy -related SOEs. Efforts 
to improve SOE governance are ongoing, supported by the adoption of the 2021- 2027 
strategy on state ownership and management of SOEs and the introduction of the Law on 
SOE governance in September 2024, which was followed up by a number of related 
secondary laws intended to enhance the efficiency and transparency of SOEs. To mitigate 
SOE-linked fiscal risks, the government has somewhat reduced the number of new 
guarantees issued for SOE operations. While the legal basis is now largely in place, ongoing

establishing independent internal audit bodies to enhance the transparency and quality of 
SOE governance, restructuring SOEs where necessary, addressing public procurement 
irregularities, and systematic disclosure and analysis of financial data. 
Functioning of the financial market 
The banking sector remains stable. The sector’s capital adequacy was 19.5% as of Q1-2026, 
well above the 8% minimum set by the central bank. Sector -wide liquidity remains high. 
Profitability increased, with the return on assets remaining relatively high, easing from the 
historic peak of 3.1% in Q1- 2025 to 2.5% in Q1- 2026. The non-performing loan ratio fell to a 
record low of 2.1% in Q1-2026, down from 2.5% at the end of 2024. Bank lending growth has 
been on an upward trend since 2023, accelerating to 16.6% year- on-year in June 2026 (20% 
for households, 11.7% for businesses) amid eased credit standards. Bank financing 
remained the dominant form of lending, with non- banking financial institutions still 
underdeveloped. A legal framework for microfinance institutions is yet to be drawn up. 
Backed by the capital market development strategy, Serbia is working to further develop the 
domestic corporate bond capital market. The availability of venture capital in Serbia is very 
limited. During the reporting period, the central bank intervened in the consumer and 
mortgage loan market. Under the new Law on the protection of financial service consumers, 
which was enacted in 2025, differentiated interest rate caps may be applied on various loan 
types. The central bank also directed commercial banks, citing supervisory expectations, to 
offer reduced -interest loan options for low -income households, young people and 
pensioners. The authorities’ goal is to increase access to finance, alleviate the burden of 
excessive interest rates on households, and encourage th e uptake of fixed -rate loans given 
the relatively high loan margins in Serbia compared to neighbouring countries. However, 
these measures may interfere with market forces, thereby affecting mortgage and consumer 
credit rates while potentially creating moral hazard and exacerbating risks related to a rapid 
growth in loans.

Education and innovation 
Education and training are not of sufficient quality to meet labour market needs. Public 
spending on education remained below the EU average at 3.46% of GDP in 2025 and the 
results of the 2025 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) show lower 
educational attainments than in the OECD and the 2022 PISA. The school -to-work transition 
in Serbia needs to be improved, as confirmed by a reduction in the employment rate of 
recent graduates (70.1% in 2025 compared with 73.1% in 2024). By contrast, the ‘not in 
education, employment or training’ (NEET) rate among people aged 15 -29 is relatively low, at 
15.1% in 2025. Efforts to modernise vocational education and training (VET) are ongoing but 
VET students continue to face uneven opportunities for practical workplace training. The 
government has taken action to diversify VET programmes and promote 
reskilling/upskilling. Participation rates in lifelong learning remain low, partly linked to 
insufficient funding and a limited offer. Investment in research remained low at 0.93% of 
GDP in 2024 (compared with an EU average of 2.24%). The sha re of private sector 
investment in R&D stagnated at 45% of total financing. Implementation of Serbia’s Smart 
Specialisation Strategy and the Law on innovation activity is ongoing. New calls for start -up 
funding were launched in 2025 but the capacity of enterprises to engage in public innovation 
programmes remains limited.

Sectoral and enterprise structure 
Services dominate the economy while the contributions from industry and agriculture 
continue to decrease. The services sector provides more than two thirds of economic value 
added. The industrial sector’s contribution to GDP declined to 20.3% in 2025 (from 21.5% in

construction sector’s contribution increased to 6.1% in 2025, driven by large -scale

workforce outside of the financial sector and generating 58.9% of total turnover in the 
economy. They remain particularly active in construction, retail, housing and food services. 
However, two thirds of exports remain concentrated in larger foreign- owned companies. 
Serbia is continuing to implement programmes to support SMEs, including digitalisation

remain modest and subject to ad  hoc adjustments. Efforts should continue to improve the 
quality and frequency of consultations with SMEs in policymaking and programme design 
processes. Loans to SMEs represented around 67% of total corporate loans in 2025, 
although SME borrowing costs remained significantly higher than for large firms. Public 
credit guarantee schemes are underdeveloped, exacerbating collateral constraints. Large 
companies and foreign investors often benefit from direct government engagement and 
state subsidies, creating an uneven playing field.

Chapter 5 - Public procurement 
EU rules ensure that the public procurement of goods, services, and works in any Member State 
is transparent and open to all EU companies on the basis of non -discrimination and equal

Institutional set-up and legal alignment 
The legal framework of Serbia is broadly aligned with the EU acquis . The Law on public -
private partnerships and concessions still needs to be amended to align with Directive 
2014/23/EU on the award of concession contracts. In March 2026, Serbia adopted the 2026 
action plan related to the 2024- 2028 Programme for the development of the public 
procurement. 
Serbia continued to circumvent its public procurement legislation  through intergovernmental 
agreements and special laws, including for the implementation of the international 
specialised exhibition EXPO Belgrade 2027. The Law on special procedures for the 
implementation of EXPO Belgrade 2027 and its accompanying decree continue to exempt 
projects from the application of the Public Procurement Law (PPL) and persistently raise 
concerns over their compliance with the EU  acquis. Serbia continued to introduce project-
specific exemptions from the public procurement legislative framework, by expanding the 
scope of the Special law on Morava corridor to another highway section and by restricting 
competition for the pre -selection of suppliers involved in the Djerdap 3 pumped storage 
hydropower plant. Other special laws and decrees exempting projects from the PPL remain 
in place. Transparency of projects contracted under intergovernmental agreements 
improved but remains insufficient.

Chapter 32 - Financial control 
The EU promotes the reform of national governance systems to improve managerial 
accountability, sound financial management of income and expenditure, and external audit of 
public funds. The financial control rules further protect the EU’s financial interests against 
fraud in the management of EU funds and protect the euro against counterfeiting. 
Public internal financial control (PIFC) 
The legal, strategic and institutional framework is in place. The are currently no major 
contradictions between the PIFC legislation and the general legal framework. Efforts to 
ensure the coherence of these frameworks should continue. Weaknesses in performance 
management and lines of accountability between independent bodies and their parent 
institutions persist. The central harmonisation unit continued to focus on PIFC monitoring 
and development, provided trainings and disseminated methodological guidance. 
Internal control  standards, including risk management, must be integrated in the 
administrative culture and capacities to implement them have to increase at both central 
and local government level. 75% of public funds beneficiaries have adopted risk 
management strategies but only 64% have a risk register, although both practices are 
increasing. The framework for irregularity reporting for budget beneficiaries is in place but 
needs to be properly implemented. The work, scope and capacities of the centralised budget 
inspections have been strengthened. 
The optimal number of key spending units needs to be reviewed to enhance policy -based 
budgeting, spending efficiency and institutional accountability. After earlier delays, 
the implementation of managerial accountability roadmap has intensified  but needs to be 
closely monitored. Amendments to the Budget System Law adopted in August 2026 
strengthen the link between internal financial control and managerial accountability. While 
analytical units are yet to be set up in all ministries and government agencies, the 
government adopted guidelines for their establishment in January  2026, and their piloting 
has started in three ministries. 
The internal audit  practice is broadly in line with international standards but quality 
assurance mechanisms should be improved. During the reporting period, 12 quality 
assessments were conducted, of which two used the peer- review method. Despite an 
increase of internal audit units across public institutions and in the number of internal 
auditors and of those certified, not all institutions required to establish an internal audit unit 
have done so. While 79% of ministries have a functional internal audit, only 56% of public 
enterprises have one. Many internal audit units do not have a sufficient number of auditors. 
Despite an existing legal framework and mandate for relevant institutions, a public track 
record of financial control on public funds used for the organisation of EXPO 2027 has been 
very limited and fragmented. An effective and transparent financial oversight on the use of 
public funds for EXPO 2027 needs to be ensured and carefully monitored.

4. NORMALISATION OF RELATIONS BETWEEN SERBIA AND KOSOVO 
The EU -facilitated Dialogue on the normalisation of relations between Serbia and Kosovo, 
while heavily impacted by domestic political developments, continued during the reporting 
period with regular meetings at chief negotiator and technical levels in bilateral and trilateral 
formats. While some progress was achieved, implementation of outstanding Dialogue 
commitments remained limited, compounded by unilateral actions and rhetoric not 
conducive to normalisation. In April 2026, Kosovo appointed Ambassador Ag ron Bajrami as 
its chief negotiator replacing former Deputy Prime Minister Besnik Bislimi. High 
Representative/ Vice President Kaja Kallas continues to stand ready to hold a high -level 
Dialogue meeting once the situation allows. 
Both Serbia and Kosovo are expected to implement all of their respective obligations 
stemming from the Agreement on the path to normalisation and its implementation annex 
(2023 Ohrid Agreement), as well as all past Dialogue agreements, in line with their official 
recommitments in September and October 2024, without delays or preconditions. This 
includes self -management arrangements for the Kosovo Serb community. Kosovo is 
expected to start the process leading to their establishment, while Serbia is expected  in 
parallel to initiate the recognition of Kosovo’s documents, symbols and institutions. Neither 
Party has made sufficient progress. The 2023 Ohrid Agreement is valid and legally binding 
on the Parties, and any formalities, including those related to its adoption or “formalisation”, 
should not impede implementation. Normalisation and implementation of Dialogue 
commitments remain essential conditions on the European path of both Parties, and both 
risk losing important opportunities in the absence of progress. 
During the reporting period, the situation in the north of Kosovo remained calm but fragile. 
Actions and rhetoric not conducive to normalisation increased. Kosovo repeatedly took 
actions contrary to its Dialogue obligations, or actions undermining the normalisation 
process. These included police and administrative actions targeting Serbia -supported 
structures and services, arrests of Kosovo Serbs, and the rejection of the withdrawn 
resignations of Kosovo Serb judges and prosecutors, undermining their reintegration. The 
EU expressed deep concern on 21 July and 28 August, including over the recurrent lack of 
coordination by Kosovo and the negative impact on local communities and their access to 
basic public and social services. Serbia, for its part, repeatedly acted contrary to its 
obligations under the 2023 Ohrid Agreement, including by lobbying against Kosovo’s 
membership in international organisations, notably the Council of Europe and Interpol. 
These actions and recurrent inflammatory rhetoric by Serbian of ficials, continue to 
undermine normalisation. Serbia has not taken any significant step to ensure accountability 
for the violent attack by armed Kosovo Serb assailants against Kosovo Police on 24 
September 2023 in Banjska/Banjskë.

5. RESILIENCE, SECURITY, DEFENCE 
Serbia remains central to the stability and security of the Western Balkans. Serbia’s 
cooperation with the EU continued in several areas, including through contributions to EU 
CSDP missions and operations. However, important challenges remain, notably as regards 
Serbia’s strategic orientation as an EU candidate country.  
Serbia’s unresolved relations with Kosovo represent one of the most serious challenges to 
regional stability and security, which is the subject of the EU -facilitated Dialogue on the

=== PAGE 83 ===
82 
 
normalisation of relations between Serbia and Kosovo.  
Democratic resilience 
Serbia has been facing a deep political and societal polarisation and low level of trust in the 
conduct of elections. While the mass student and civic protests were largely permitted, 
some actions by the authorities in relation to the protests, including instance of excessive 
use of force, lack of follow -up to cases of violence, as well as targeting of activists by state 
representatives and tabloids, the framing of protests as a foreign- backed coloured 
revolution, measures eroding academic freedom and a dif ficult environment in which the 
civil society operates, exposed weaknesses with regard to the country’s democratic culture,  
and its system of checks and balances. 
There are persistent challenges as regards establishing an enabling environment for free 
and independent media, countering foreign information manipulation and interference 
(FIMI), including disinformation and lack of political will to address those issues. TV stations 
holding national broadcasting frequencies remain the primary sources of information 
manipulation and disinformation, also targeting the EU. The same media outlets are also the 
primary recipients of public co -funding of media content, and thei r ownership structures are 
closely intertwined with State -owned companies. The Serbian territory and/or citizens 
continued being used by third countries, such as Russian Federation, to conduct hybrid 
operations and there continue to be reports of cooperation between Serbian security and 
intelligence service and the Russian security and intelligence apparatus. Russia’s state -
owned RT Balkans operates on state operator Telekom Srbija, but its reach has so far been 
limited compared to domestically produced pro -Kremlin media. Its presence, as well as that 
of Sputnik Serbia nonetheless reflect a permissive environment for Kremlin- aligned 
narratives. The concentration of editorial and financial power in the hands of a few media 
actors hostile to democratic norms, combined with an absence of effective FIMI 
countermeasures, undermines both the integrity of the information space and Serbia’s EU 
accession process. 
Economic security and resilience 
From the perspective of the EU’s economic security framework, Serbia is a key partner 
interconnected with several European value chains. The EU has remained Serbia’s largest 
trading partner, and investor. Serbia has a key role to play in the diversification of regional 
supply chains, particularly in sectors like industrial production, transport infrastructure and 
energy. The presence of significant investments from third countries, including China, in key 
sectors such as mining, steel production, infrastruc ture and high tech, expose Serbia’s 
dependency on imported technology and on foreign capital. Serbia’s continued dependence 
on Russian energy imports, particularly of natural gas, and Russian ownership stakes in 
Serbia’s critical infrastructure further expose critical energy dependency and makes the 
country prone to Russian leverage and impact of international sanctions against Russia. 
Such dependencies underline the importance of diversification of energy sources and 
routes, enhanced energy efficiency, and strengthened governance of critical infrastructure to 
reduce vulnerability to external shocks and potential economic coercion. 
Defence and defence industrial dimension 
Serbia inherited a substantial defence industry from former Yugoslavia, regaining a 
prominent position in the region and on the world market. The predominantly state- owned 
defence industry counts around 200 companies with 20 000 employees and is the largest in 
the Western Balkans. The state -owned Yugoimport –  SDPR specialised in manufacturing 
and trading of defence equipment (armoured vehicles, artillery systems, drones) and 
ammunition represents the core of Serbia’s defence industry. Development of new def ence 
capabilities is supported by domestic sources. At the same time, Serbia is entering in

=== PAGE 84 ===
83 
 
defence industry partnerships with other non-EU countries, such as China and Israel.  
Due to its insufficient level of alignment with the EU’s CFSP, Serbia does not receive 
assistance under the European Peace Facility. Following allegations in June 2025 by the 
Russian foreign intelligence agency that Serbia was selling artillery ammunition to Ukraine 
through intermediaries, a moratorium on all exports of arms and ammunition was declared 
by the Serbian National Security Council, citing national security and economic interests. 
Serbia’s military spending has increased in the past years to USD  2.7 billion in 2025. While 
significant military procurement has been agreed with suppliers from EU Member States, 
Serbia’s military cooperation with China has increased over the past year. China is Serbia’s 
largest defence equipment supplier, accounting for 61% of Serbia’s total arms imports 
between 2021 and 2025, followed by France (12%) and Russia (7%). 
In the framework of the European Defence Fund (EDF) and the European Defence Industrial 
Programme (EDIP), entities established in the EU or in an associated country and subject to 
control by Serbia or by a Serbian entity could only be eligible for funding if guarantees 
provided by the EU Member State or associated country of establishment are made available 
to the Commission. So far this has not materialised. 
Civil protection and disaster resilience  
Serbia has continued improving its resilience through enhancing its civil protection system 
and has capacity to provide important cross -border assistance. Serbia continues close 
cooperation with the EU through the Union Civil Protection Mechanism (UCPM), it has both 
provided and received assistance under the mechanism during the reporting period.

Chapter 1 - Free movement of goods 
The free movement of goods ensures that many products can be traded freely across the EU 
based on common rules and procedures. Where products are governed by national rules, the 
principle of the free movement of goods prevents these rules from creating unjustified barriers

General principles 
Serbia has adopted its revised strategic documents to implement EU acquis in this chapter. 
Serbia should speed up implementation of these strategic documents. Serbia has applied 
for an Agreement on Conformity Assessment and Acceptance (ACAA) with the EU, covering 
low voltage equipment, electromagnetic compatibility, machinery, and personal protective 
equipment and the relevant assessment processes are ongoing. 
Non-harmonised area 
Serbia should continue to implement its action plan to ensure compliance with Articles 34-
36 of the TFEU. Serbia remains partly aligned with the EU acquis on mutual recognition. 
Harmonised area: quality infrastructure 
Serbia has a developed quality infrastructure system, overseen by the Ministry of Economy 
and supported by various key entities and market surveillance bodies. The quality 
infrastructure institutions still require additional budget and human resources. On 
standardisation, the national legislation is highly aligned with the relevant EU acquis,  and 
Serbia has aligned with 99% of European harmonised standards (EN). Serbian legislation 
remains partly aligned with the EU acquis on accreditation.

Chapter 2 - Freedom of movement for workers 
Nationals of one Member State have the right to work in another Member State and must be 
given the same working and social conditions as other worker nationals of that Member State. 
Regarding access to the labour market, the legislative framework is partially aligned with the 
EU acquis as EU citizens are currently covered by rules for third -country nationals. In 2025, a 
total of 1 190 work permits were issued to EU citizens. The working permit procedure has 
been simplified by the new system of single permit for residence and work. 
Serbia’s National Employment Service has continued with the preparations for joining the 
European Employment Services Network ( EURES) upon accession, including on IT 
infrastructure, roadmap and legislative alignment.  
Regarding the coordination of social security systems , no new bilateral agreements were 
concluded over the reporting period but there are ongoing negotiations with the Netherlands 
and Portugal. Although specific activities in the area of Electronic Data Exchange are being 
developed and continued, overall, legislative and technical conditions for social security 
institutions to cooperate with their counterparts in EU Member States need to be further 
improved. 
There were no developments on the European Health Insurance Card (EHIC). Serbia 
currently applies agreements on the use of EHIC with nine EU Member States.

The EU has common rules on the formation, registration and disclosure requirements of a

Chapter 8 - Competition policy 
EU rules protect free competition. They include antitrust rules against restrictive agreements 
between companies and abuse of a dominant position, and rules on concentrations between 
companies which would significantly impede effective competition. EU rules also set out a 
system of State aid control. Governments are only allowed to grant State aid if restrictive 
conditions are met, with a view to preventing distortion of competition. 
Antitrust and mergers 
The legislative framework is broadly in line with Articles 101 and 102 in the Treaty on the 
Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) and related provisions of the Stabilisation and 
Association Agreement  (SAA) but needs to be fully aligned with EU guidelines and 
communications. Progress was made on three block exemption regulations, which were 
adopted in March 2026. Work is ongoing with respect to amending and aligning Article  9 of 
the Law on multilateral interchange fees and special operating rules for card -based 
payment transactions with the EU acquis and the SAA. 
Regarding the institutional framework , the Commission for Protection of Competition 
(CPC) has a reputation as an operationally independent institution. However, it remains to 
be seen how the CPC’s financial independence, resources and ability to conduct its 
competition enforcement responsibilities will be impacted by the introduction of new 
responsibilities under the Law on Unfair Trading Practices, adopted in April 2026. The level 
of fines decreased to around EUR  0.18 million in 2025, from over EUR 0.5 million in 2024.  
Work should focus on sustaining a credible enforcement record, decisions should be 
systematically published, and the transparency of the CPC’s work should increase. 
The enforcement capacity of the CPC is adequate. Consulting the CPC on all relevant 
legislation should continue and its opinions should be systematically taken into account. 
The CPC should continue to step up its advocacy activities. The capacity and specialisation 
of the judiciary for complex competition cases needs to be improved significantly.

Chapter 16 - Taxation 
EU rules on taxation cover value -added tax, excise duties and aspects of corporate taxation. 
They also cover cooperation between tax administrations, including the exchange of

In the area of taxation, the institutional framework is in place. Serbia has committed to 
greater transparency by preparing a first tax expenditure report, which initial findings show 
that value added tax (VAT) represents the largest fiscal cost. 
Regarding indirect taxation, the legislative framework remains partially aligned with the EU 
acquis. The Law on VAT was amended in December 2025 and introduced periodic invoices

Chapter 26 - Education and culture 
The EU supports cooperation in education and culture through funding programmes and the 
coordination of Member State policies through the ‘open method of coordination’. The EU and 
the Member States must also prevent discrimination and ensure quality education for children 
of migrant workers, including those from disadvantaged backgrounds. 
In the area of education and training , the policy framework and the institutional framework 
are in place, but their implementation deteriorated during the reporting period. The level of 
public spending on education was modest in 2025, around 3.46% of GDP, which is below the 
EU average of 4.7% of GDP (2023). Underinvestment in certain sectors constrains the 
administrative and delivery capacity of the system. The implementation of the 2030 
education strategy and its action plan is delayed. There is vulnerability to undue interference 
and corruption in the sector, particularly in higher education, which materialised during the 
reporting period through untransparent decisions on appointments, dismissals and 
restructuring of institutions. Serbia’s National Education Council and the National Council for 
Higher Education have not been operational since February 2026, due to the authorities not

efforts are needed to increase participation and equitable access for the most 
disadvantaged children, including Roma children and children with disabilities.  
The rate of early school leavers dropped to 5.1% in 2025 (6.2% in 2024). Revised curricula 
for the first grades of all three cycles of pre -university education were adopted but will only 
apply as of the 2027/2028 school year. 
In December 2025, Serbia adopted amendments to the Law on textbooks which give 
exclusive rights to a public publisher for producing textbooks for subjects of geography and 
history in primary and secondary education as of 2027/2028 school year. This reduces 
pluralism, may impact the quality of education and politicise the curriculum. 
At the beginning of the 2025/2026 school year, a significant number of principals were

Chapter 29 - Customs union 
All Member States are part of the EU customs union and follow the same customs rules and 
procedures. This requires legislative alignment as well as adequate implementing and 
enforcement capacity, and access to and interoperability with  the EU electronic customs 
environment. 
Serbia’s customs legislation is largely aligned with the EU acquis. In November 2025, Serbia 
further amended its Regulation on customs relief, while in February 2026, Regulation on 
customs procedures and customs formalities was amended with a view to further aligning 
with the acquis . Serbia is a party to the Common Transit Convention and to the Pan -Euro-
Mediterranean Convention on rules of origin and applies the revised rules in trade with the 
EU since end of 2021. Serbia regularly harmonises its customs tariff nomenclature with the 
EU combined nomenclature, including for 2026. Serbia has an authorised economic operator 
programme and has issued a total of 72  certificates for authorised economic operators. Full 
alignment with the EU  acquis is still needed concerning free zones . Fees are still charged to 
allow lorries to enter certain customs terminals to discharge customs obligations, which is 
not in line with Serbia’s obligations under the  Stabilisation and Association Agreement 
(SAA). 
In terms of the administrative and operational capacity, during the reporting period, the 
customs administration adopted a new action plan (2025- 2027) for implementing the 
integrated border management strategy (2022 -2027), a new business strategy (2025- 2029) 
and a new IT strategy and implementation plan (2025- 2029). For 236 announced vacancies, 
214 new staff members were hired. Work is ongoing to fill the remaining vacant positions. 
Development of the Integrated Tariff Management System (ITMS) software has begun. The 
customs administration successfully launched the New Computerised Transit System 
(NCTS – Phase 6) with an opt- in approach, in May 2026. The customs laboratory remains 
underequipped, and the risk management system must be further strengthened. To fully 
align on security aspects, Serbia should consistently carry out pre- arrival/pre-departure risk-
based analysis, in line with the Union Customs Code. In 2025, total revenues co llected by the 
customs administration increased by 5.5% year- on-year. The administrative capacity for the 
control of illicit tobacco trade is insufficient. Serbia should continue to strengthen 
combating customs fraud, in particular smuggling of tobacco products, by cooperating with 
the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF). 
Serbia is a party to the Protocol to Eliminate Illicit Trade in Tobacco Products of the WHO 
Framework Convention on Tobacco Control but has yet to fully comply with its provisions, 
including by establishing a track and trace system of tobacco products. 
Serbia continued to cooperate with EU Member States and OLAF under Protocol  6 of the 
SAA on mutual administrative assistance in customs matters, particularly in order to 
investigate suspected cases of circumvention via its territory  and/or by legal entities

Chapter 27 - Environment and climate change 
The EU promotes strong climate action, sustainable development and protection of the 
environment. EU rules contain provisions addressing horizontal legislation, climate change, 
water quality, air quality, waste management, nature protection, industrial pollution, chemicals, 
noise, and civil protection. 
Environment 
The Environmental Protection Strategy was successfully adopted in August 2026, 
establishing the strategic framework for environmental protection until 2033. However, 
coordination should be improved as the environmental sector competencies are scattered 
across several Ministries and local self -governments. Better enforcement of the 
environmental legislation is needed, as well as enhanced environmental inspections and 
effective sanctions for polluters. 
On horizontal legislation , the alignment of the legislative framework with the EU acquis is 
high but further adoption of implementing legislation is necessary for its completion. Three 
by-laws linked to legislation on environmental impact assessment (EIA) and two rulebooks

consultation process, judicial efficiency, building capacities to strengthen environmental 
inspections and ensuring more effective sanctions for polluters. The implementation of the 
principles of the Aarhus Convention, namely the right of citizens to information, 
participation, and access to justice in environmental matters, remains a concern (see also 
Public Administration Reform).  
Alignment with the Environmental Liability Directive and the Environmental Crime Directive 
and their implementation need to be improved.  
On air quality, there is a high level of alignment with the EU acquis . Some implementing 
legislation was adopted, including a Regulation on the list of air quality categories by zones 
and agglomerations and a Regulation on the air quality control programme within the State 
network. Serbia has also begun implementing the new European air quality index. Three new 
air quality plans were approved in the reporting period. Progress was made on requirements 
and standards in reducing emissions of volatile organic compounds  through the adoption of 
a new rulebook. However, the reporting on the annual air quality is delayed, affecting the 
necessary timely actions to respond to the reported pollution. 
On waste management , Serbia has a good level of alignment with the EU acquis , however

Chapter 13 - Fisheries and aquaculture 
The common fisheries policy lays down rules on fisheries management, protects living

Chapter 22 - Regional policy and coordination of structural instruments 
Regional policy is the EU’s main investment policy aiming at reducing  economic, social, and 
territorial disparities between regions and promoting balanced development across the Union . 
It is implemented through ‘shared management’ between the Commission and EU Member 
States. The implementation of cohesion policy programmes requires appropriate 
administrative structures and capacity on programme and project level, the establishment of 
systems of sound financial management and control, and the fulfilment of other EU acquis 
elements such as environmental or public procurement legislation. 
On legislative framework , Serbia adopted in April 2026 the L aw on cohesion policy,

implementing legislation remains to be adopted. The adoption of a national development 
plan for reducing regional and local disparities remains outstanding , with limited progress

The institutional framework for cohesion policy is functional, with regular meetings, mostly

The administrative capacity is marked by a stable administrative framework but is 
increasingly affected by the absence of a staff retention policy, delays in recruitment and an

avoid signiﬁcant decommitment of EU funds. 
Serbia needs to enhance the implementation of the Reform Agenda under the Reform and

The EU has a common commercial policy towards third countries, based on multilateral and

Serbia participates in the Nuclear Suppliers Group, but not yet in other multilateral export 
control regimes. Serbia last updated its National Control List of Arms and Military Equipment 
in July 2026, alongside its National Control List of Dual- Purpose Goods. After Serbia 
suspended the export of arms and military equipment produced in Serbia and made it 
subject to approval of the National Security Council in June 2025, partial exports to a 
selective number of countries resumed during the reporting period. 
Serbia has a transparent reporting system on its strategic export controls, in line with the 
Arms Trade Treaty and other international obligations , but allegations of corruption in its 
arms trade continue and need to be investigated in order to establish whether the national 
measures to prevent diversion of arms and ammunition are adequate and effective. Serbia is 
a party to the Arms Trade Treaty and further measures to prevent diversion of arms and 
ammunition should be developed to support compliance. Serbia is yet to systematically 
align with EU statements in multilateral non -proliferation, disarmament and arms control 
fora. In general, Serbia alig ned with the EU on technical issues, but not on political issues 
(e.g. Russia, Ukraine, China, Iran, DPRK). Occasionally it supported positions which were 
opposed to those of the EU, by consistently voting in favour of the UNGA resolution 
"Promoting International Cooperation on Peaceful Uses in the Context of International 
Security.". 
In terms of security measures, Serbia continued implementing its Security of Information 
Agreement with the EU on procedures for exchanging and protecting classified information. 
On common security and defence policy (CSDP) , Serbia continued to actively participate 
and contribute substantially to EU military crisis management missions and operations  in 
Africa (EUTM Somalia, EUTM RCA and EUTM Mozambique) and to EUNAVFOR Atalanta in 
the Western Indian Ocean and in the Red Sea. The institutional framework enabling Serbia’s 
participation in military crisis management is in place, but Serbia still needs to put in place 
relevant legislation to implement the 2023 law on the participation of civilians in 
international missions and operations, as well as the institutional framework required to 
become eligible to contribute to civilian CSDP missions. Serbia contributes significantly to 
cooperation in military medicine through the Balkan Medical Task Force.  Serbia participates 
in training activities of the European Security and Defence College (ESDC). 
On hybrid threats, no progress has been made on foreign information manipulation and

ANNEX I - RELATIONS BETWEEN THE EU AND SERBIA 
As part of the accession negotiations , 22 chapters have been opened, two of which have 
been provisionally closed (Chapters 25 and 26). In line with the revised enlargement 
methodology, which Serbia accepted, negotiation chapters are grouped in six thematic 
clusters, and negotiations should be opened for each cluster as a whole – after fulfilling 
opening benchmarks – rather than on individual chapters. All chapters of cluster 1 
(fundamentals) and cluster 4 (green agenda and sustainable connectivity) have been 
opened. Cluster 3 (competitiveness and inclusive growth) is the most advanced after those 
and in line to be opened next. Serbia tabled its negotiating positions on Chapters 2, 3, 10, 16, 
19 and 28. 
Serbia is participating in the Stabilisation and Association Process . Serbia continued to 
build a track record in implementing the obligations of the Stabilisation and Association 
Agreement (SAA). However, Serbian legislation still does not comply with the SAA in a 
number of areas, including ad hoc trade restrictions, on restrictions on the acquisition of real 
estate and on discriminatory practices in issuing payment cards by banks. On public 
procurement, intergovernmental agreements concluded with third countries and their 
implementation do not follow the EU principles of equal treatment, transparency, non-
discrimination and competition for most of the value of public procurement contracts. As a 
result, EU companies are prevented from participating in large- scale infrastructure projects 
in the country. 
Regular political and economic dialogue between the EU and Serbia has continued. 
Stabilisation and Association committee and subcommittee meetings were held, together 
with a meeting of a special group on public administration reform. Serbia also participates in 
the ministerial dialogue between the economic and finance ministers of the EU and 
candidate countries, where joint recommendations are adopted. The most recent meeting 
was held on 5  May 2026. A joint EU -Serbia Working group on energy was set up in Oc tober 
2025 and has met 5 times since. 
Economic integration with the EU remains deep and structurally anchored. The EU 
continues to be Serbia’s largest trade and investment partner, accounting for 58.2% of total 
trade in goods and 72.2% of FDI inflows in 2025. Serbia’s other main trading partners in 
2025 included China (11%), CEFTA countries (9%) and Türkiye (4.5%). Russia accounted for 
2.7% of total trade flows in 2025. China continues to consolidate its position as Serbia’s 
second-largest trading partner, driven primarily by Serbian imports of industrial goods and 
exports of copper ore. Despite continued efforts t o facilitate trade, including through the 
digitalisation of customs procedures and initiatives such as the EU -Western Balkan Green 
Lanes, persistent bottlenecks at border crossing points with EU Member States and non-
tariff barriers continue to affect trade flows. 
Serbia’s pattern of alignment with the EU’s common foreign and security policy remained 
largely unchanged. Serbia continued to participate actively in EU crisis management 
missions and operations under the common security and defence policy. 
The Balkans Medical Task Force, in which Serbia participates, benefits from a EUR  6 million 
2022 support measure under the European Peace Facility. In October 2026, further regional 
EPF measure in support of Balkan Medical Task Force (EUR 10 million) was adopted. 
Visa-free travel for citizens of Serbia travelling to the Schengen area has been in force since 
December 2009. A readmission  agreement between the EU and Serbia has been in force 
since 2008. The Commission’s December 2025 eighth report under the visa suspension 
mechanism concluded that Serbia continues to meet the visa liberalisation requirements. 
However, the European Commission recommended that Serbia refrain from granting 
citizenship under accelerated or simplified procedures to nationals of countries that pose

Under the Instrument for Pre -accession Assistance 2021 – 2027 ( IPA)7 the EU funding for 
Serbia reached EUR  1 014.4 million. The implementation of the IPA 2025- 27 package

development, and acquis  alignment, started along with the implementation of the two

This set of programmes is complemented by significant funding under the multi -country and

Adriatic and Ionian Region and the EU Strategy for the Danube Region . The new activities

Implementation Annex and all past Dialogue Agreements and engage in negotiations on the 
Comprehensive Agreement on normalisation of relations. As of September 2026, the 
European Commission has released EUR 167.6 million to Serbia, including EUR 111 million in 
pre-financing. 
Serbia participates in a number of EU programmes: Horizon Europe, Erasmus+, Creative 
Europe, the Digital Europe Programme, the Citizens, Equality, Rights and Values Programme, 
the Single Market Programme, the European Social Fund Plus / EaSI strand, the Customs

Innovation Hubs network, the EU Digital Agenda for the Western Balkans, EU4Digital, 
WiFi4WB, the Connective Europe Facility and the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking. 
To improve public administration reform and policy alignment, Serbia also participates as an 
observer in two pilot projects under the Technical Support Instrument, including on green 
budgeting and on improving labour market relevance.
