Commission’s assessment of the preconditions for Union support 
 
Effective democratic and rule of law mechanisms 
Moldova continues to implement justice sector reforms,  in particular through the vetting 
process, strengthening the integrity and independence of the judiciary. The fight against 
corruption has also advanced, with a clarification of the roles and cooperation between relevant 
institutions and stronger anticorruption mainstreaming.  
 
The functioning of democratic institutions is satisfactory. Moldova shows a continued 
commitment to implementing reforms, despite hybrid attacks from Russia and its proxies on 
democratic institutions, that aim to undermine social co hesion in the country. The capacity of 
public institutions, including of the Bureau of European Integration, continued to strengthen to 
help deliver the work of the government on the EU integration.  
 
Following the resignation of Prime Minister Munteanu, the change of government was swift 
and orderly, in full respect of constitutional procedures. On 21 July 2026, the Parliament 
approved the new government led by Prime Minister Tofan. The new government's programme 
continues to prioritise Moldova's EU access ion, reforms, strengthening of democratic 
institutions, and economic growth. 
 
The Parliament has an oversight role over the executive. Accountability lines between the 
Parliament and other relevant institutions are established, with the Parliament appointing state 
officials at the head of central administrative institutions and independent institutions. Further 
efforts are needed to increase the Parliament’s transparency, accountability, integrity, and 
overall effectiveness. This includes enhancing the trac k record of parliamentary oversight of 
independent institutions and ensuring a timely and comprehensive publication of parliamentary 
activities and decisions.  While Moldova’s legal framework requires at least 10 working days’ 
notice, this requirement is n ot always complied with, and more timely publication of public 
consultations and parliamentary documents is needed to enable meaningful stakeholder 
participation and strengthen the inclusiveness and transparency of the legislative process. 
 
The legal elect oral framework provides a sound basis  for conducting democratic 
elections. During the last elections, the national authorities worked actively and in a 
coordinated manner to prevent, detect and counter foreign interference attempts, including 
illicit financing and vote-buying schemes, with the aim of safeguarding electoral integrity and 
democratic process. Nonetheless, some OSCE/ODIHR and Venice Commission

recommendations remain unaddressed. Moldova should ensure its legislative framework 
pertaining to elections, including the Law on combating electoral corruption, is brought fully 
in line with European standards. The upcoming regional elections in Gagauzia should be 
organised in line with EU and international standards, including the Venice Commission’s 
recommendations. 
 
Freedom of expression is generally guaranteed in Moldova, and significant progress has 
been made in aligning the country’s legislative framework with the EU acquis.  Moldova 
adopted a Law on the Media, which extended ownership transparency requirements to print 
and online media and further harmonised the legal framework with the European Media 
Freedom Act. Amendments to the Audiovisual Media Services Code were also adopted to bring 
the legislation fully into line with European standards. Moldova should now ensure the 
effective implementation of these laws by all relevant parties. Through the Media Subsidy 
Fund, 22 new media projects were supported in 2026, helping to bolster a sector that continues 
to face structural economic difficulties. Progress was made in reinforcing the protection of 
journalists against intimidation, notably through the development of an Action Plan on 
journalists’ safety and amendments to the Cri minal Code which enabled the prosecution of 
cases of cyberviolence against media workers. Systematic law enforcement investigations and 
consistent judicial follow-up of cases of threats against journalists need to be ensured. 
 
Respect for all human rights obligations 
 
The legislative and institutional framework on fundamental rights is largely in place, and 
Moldova continues to meet its international obligations on human rights. Implementation 
of the national human rights action programme is ongoing. Institutional capacity and 
implementation efforts need to be strengthened to guarantee the full enjoyment of human 
rights. Mechanisms for preventing and countering gender -based violence are in place. 
Detention conditions, progress on deinstitution alisation and access to specialised support 
services for victims of gender -based violence, including shelters and psychological support, 
should be improved. The legislative framework safeguards the rights of persons belonging to 
minorities. Moldova should continue to support intercultural dialogue and efforts to include 
ethnic minorities in public administration. 
 
Based on the above assessment, Moldova continues to fulfil the preconditions for Union 
support under Article 10 of Regulation (EU) 2025/535. 
 
General conditions 
 
Macroeconomic and financial stability 
 
Following a stagnation in 2024, real GDP reached 2.4% in 2025 and became increasingly 
broad-based towards the end of the year. While in the first quarters of 2025 growth was 
mainly driven by a recovery of agricultural output following the 2024 drought and construction, 
the last quarter outturn was marked by a strong pick up of the IT and manufacturing

=== PAGE 4 ===
4 
 
sectors. Real GDP slowed to 0.4% in the first quarter of 2026, as slowing consumption and a 
contraction in investment outweighed robust export growth. On the supply side, gains in 
manufacturing and retail trade were offset by sharp contractions in real estate, IT, and 
construction. Preliminary data from the second quarter of 2026 suggest that the real G DP 
increased by 0.9%. 
  
The external sector continues having persistent structural challenges. In 2025, the current 
account deficit increased to 19.7% of GDP, up from 16.6% in 2024. This reflects a strong 
deterioration in the trade balance, mainly on accou nt of a surge in imports due to robust 
demand, as well as a sharp increase in electricity imports after Russian gas to the Transnistrian 
region was cut off. During the first quarter of 2026, the current account deficit narrowed by 
17% year-on-year but rema ined elevated at 20.9% of GDP, reflecting a lower trade deficit 
supported by higher exports and lower imports. Foreign currency reserves increased during the 
first quarter by 3.2% to EUR 5.3 billion, equivalent to almost six months of import coverage.   
 
Monetary and Fiscal Policies  
Inflation has picked up again in 2026. It briefly returned to the Central Bank’s target range 
in January 2026 before re-accelerating on the back of a sharp increase in global energy prices 
following the conflict in Iran and the wider Middle East, peaking at 6.8% in April 2026 before 
stabilising at 6.3% in July. Renewed inflationary pressure prompted a policy rate increase to 
6.5% in May and further to 7% and 7.5% in June and August respectively.   
  
The predominantly foreign-owned banking sector demonstrated continued stability with 
strong capital adequacy, liquidity, and profitability. Non-performing loans edged up again 
during the first months of 2026 but remain relatively low at 5.3%. The impact of a proposed 
temporary increased income tax on banks remains to be assessed.  
  
The 2025 fiscal deficit reached 4% of GDP, lower than the 5% of GDP target and broadly 
unchanged from previous year’s outturn of 3.9% of GDP  reflecting capital expenditure 
under-execution and slightly higher than expected revenues. The 2026 deficit is projected 
at 5.7% of GDP driven by a shift towards investment -led spending. However, mid -year data 
suggests an under-execution of investment expenditure.   
  
In 2025, the public debt -to-GDP ratio increased by 1 percentage point, reaching 39.2% 
by the end of the year, primarily driven by the high fiscal deficit, partly offset by favourable 
stock flow adjustments due to exchange rate developments.  The IMF's latest public debt 
sustainability analysis confirmed  a low risk  of external debt distress and moderate risk of 
overall public debt distress. Most external state debt is owed to multilateral lenders, with the 
EU’s (including EIB) share accounting f or 29% of the total in mid -2026. Sovereign credit 
ratings from Fitch (B+), S&P Global (BB-), and Moody's (B2) all carry stable outlooks.   
  
Vulnerability to External Shocks and Resilience Efforts  
Moldova faces substantial external vulnerabilities. These include structural current account 
imbalances, mainly due to  heavy reliance on volatile agricultural exports, and energy import

=== PAGE 5 ===
5 
 
dependency, and low levels of preparedness against extreme weather eve nts and climate 
change. At the same time, Moldova remains dependent on concessional external financing, 
while FDI levels remain low at 1.8% of GDP in 2025. 
  
Existing resilience-building efforts by the government include maintaining foreign reserves 
above five months of import coverage, implementing targeted energy support programs, and 
support measures for broadening the export base. Services export expansion provides some 
diversification, while stable remittance flows continue supporting the economy. The banking 
sector's strong fundamentals provide financial stability.   
  
The international support framework  remains robust. Following Moldova’s request in 
January 2026 for a new IMF programme, a staff -level agreement for a three -year non -
disbursing IMF Policy Coordination Instrument (PCI) was reached with the previous 
government on 20 May. Negotiations for the programme contin ue bilaterally with the new 
government. Moldova participated in the economic and financial dialogue with the EU in May 
2026, following the submission of its third economic reform programme. Implementation of 
the 2025 policy guidance was partial. 
  
Policy Mix Assessment and Medium-term Stability  
Moldova remains committed to macroeconomic stability and economic reforms despite a 
difficult external environment. The macroeconomic policy mix has contributed to sustaining 
macroeconomic stability amidst the sever al recent crises, including the latest energy price 
increase, but public investment remains very low, and the country is vulnerable to external 
shocks. The May 2026 Economic  Policy Dialogue joint conclusions provided concrete 
recommendations for macroecono mic and financial stability, fiscal sustainability, and sound 
monetary policies.  
 
Moldova’s SOE sector remains economically significant, while the implementation of the 
triage results has been slow. There are around 900 SOEs registered at central and local level, 
with roughly one third under central government. In 2025, around 220 SOEs were reported 
active at the central level. In 2024, the authorities completed a triage of 238 enterprises: 66 
were deemed unsuitable for privatisation, 55 proposed for conversion into another legal form, 
20 for privatisation and 97 for liquidation. However, the implementation has been slow, with 
privatisation process launched for only five SOEs; another 10 SOEs should be liquidated and 
15 reorganised by end 2026. Financial performance and fiscal risks are concentrated in a small 
number of large enterprises, notably in energy, transport, communications and public services 
of which some record significant losses. Although the legal framework for corporate 
governance has impr oved, ownership responsibilities remain fragmented, the separation 
between ownership and policymaking is weak. 
 
The fiscal policy contribution to medium -term stability presents mixed signals.  With 
structural fiscal deficits expected to remain elevated over the medium term, the public debt-to-
GDP ratio is projected to be on a rising trajectory, albeit still relatively low. This is subject to 
several risks, including foreign exchange developments, contingent liabilities stemming from

=== PAGE 6 ===
6 
 
government guarantees and  potential reclassification of several indebted state -owned 
enterprises into the general government sector.   In addition, the persistent under -execution of 
capital expenditure suggests implementation challenges. 
  
Monetary policy has generally supported stability despite external shocks . Inflation rose 
above the National Bank of Moldova's target range during the first half of 2026, driven mainly 
by energy price shocks, before returning within the target range in July. Nonetheless, 
inflationary risks remain elevated, driven by the conflict in the Middle East, uncertainties 
related to the agricultural recovery and the fiscal stimulus provided by the Growth Plan. 
Banking sector stability persists with strong fundamentals. The increase of the NPL ratio 
warrants monitoring but remains manageable.  
   
The policy mix appears broadly coordinated and generally conducive  to 
stability. Engagement with the IMF and EU provides external discipline and support, 
reinforcing domestic policy efforts and sending positive signal s to the market. The key 
challenge is balancing growth support with deficit reduction while addressing structural 
vulnerabilities, particularly the large current account deficit.   
  
The policy mix is  generally conducive  to stability , but its effectiveness  will ultimately 
depend on consistent and high-quality implementation of structural reforms, diversification of 
the economic base, and strengthening institutional capacity, particularly in managing state -
owned enterprises and improving public expenditure  efficiency and increasing public 
investment implementation. 
 
Based on the above assessment, Moldova continues to fulfil the general conditions related to 
macro-financial stability.  
 
Public financial management (PFM) 
 
Moldova is implementing a comprehensive PFM Reform Strategy 2023 -2030, developed 
based on lessons learned from the previous strategy covering 2013-2022 and findings from the 
2022 PEFA assessment. The strategy addresses identified weaknesses in budgetary discipline, 
public investment management, capacity constraints, and ext ernal audit independence. 
SIGMA’s mid-term review of the strategy was finalised in June 2026 at the request of the 
Ministry of Finance. The review noted a high rate of implementation of reform actions but 
recommended updating the strategic framework to include a robust monitoring framework with 
measurable indicators and to strengthen its linkage with the EU acquis. The new government 
remains committed to PFM reforms and implementation of the Public Administration Reform 
roadmap. However, sustained implementation requires strengthened capacity. 
 
Credibility and Relevance of PFM Reforms 
The reform strategy demonstrates strong credibility and relevance through its evidence-
based approach, drawing from multiple diagnostic assessments. The reforms appropriately 
target the identified weaknesses. Legislative proposals have been introduced to institutionalise

=== PAGE 7 ===
7 
 
spending reviews into the budget process, alongside a methodology, enhancing systematic 
evaluation of public expenditure. In November 2025, a comprehensive legislative package was 
introduced to amend the Law on Public Finance and Fiscal Responsibility, establishing 
mandatory budget reporting for extra-budgetary entities from 2027 onwards. This change will 
enhance budget transparency by incorporating additional units partially funded by the budget 
but not currently included in national public budget reporting.  
 
Main Achievements Since Previous Assessment 
Moldova has demonstrated tangible progress through several key achievements . The 
Single Project pipeline approach to capital investments was consistently implemented in the 
first half of 2026. The new methodology on spending reviews, approved in June 2026, sets the 
framework for conducting these reviews, including the responsibilities o f the institutions 
involved. Budgetary transparency improved through legal amendments that reclassify public 
institutions under self -management, establishing a more rigorous accounting and reporting 
requirements. The qualifications of public procurement specialists improved significantly with 
the introduction of a certification requirement, and trainings began in March 2026 under the 
new scheme. The establishment of public investment management focal points and 
subdivisions in line ministries strengthened p ublic investment management implementation, 
ensuring proper review and approval mechanisms for new public capital investment projects. 
All new projects included in the 2026 State Budget were appraised on the new IT platform.  
 
The Court of Accounts reforme d its internal governance . To address overlapping 
responsibilities, the Court of Accounts clarified the roles of its President, Members, and Head 
of Office. Each member was assigned responsibility for one element of the Quality 
Management System, ensuring quality management became a collective leadership 
responsibility, rather than a separate technical function. In addition, the Court of Accounts 
financial autonomy was significantly enhanced in June 2026 with legal amendments, 
strengthening the role of the legislative branch and substantially limiting the role of the 
executive branch in approving the budget of Court of Accounts.  
 
Progress with budgetary reforms has been mixed. The 2026 Budget Law improved budget 
transparency by reducing the General Actions section of the state budget to below 30%, 
primarily by reassigning transfers to the Mandatory Medical Insurance Funds and State Social 
Insurance Budget to their respective min istries. Even so, this share remains high. Progress in 
other areas has been more limited. The medium -term budgetary framework 2026 -2028 was 
prepared and submitted for approval at the ministerial level but has not been adopted. Likewise, 
spending reviews f or health and social sectors have not been endorsed by the government, 
despite efforts to improve spending efficiency in both sectors . By end July 2026, the 2026 -
2028 medium-term budgetary framework has not been adopted, held up by pending tax and 
salary r eforms and a change of government. Managerial accountability requires continued 
strengthening, and the authorities are taking concrete steps to address this. Government 
Decision No. 284/2025 established a clear delimitation of responsibilities between mini stries 
and subordinated authorities and the Ministry of Finance is developing a Guide on the

delegation of responsibilities within a delegated management system, with a pilot exercise 
planned by the end of 2026.  
Anti-Fraud Measures and Response to Corruption Cases 
Moldova has established a robust anti-fraud system. The anti-fraud unit (State Inspectorate 
of Financial Control) signed fourteen cooperation agreements with network members, 
establishing clear regulatory frameworks and functional institutional m echanisms. These 
agreements ensure clear allocation of responsibilities, standardisation of reporting and 
intervention processes, and coherent information flow among relevant authorities. The network 
brings together the State Chancellery, the Ministry of F inance, the Court of Accounts, the 
National Anti -Corruption Centre, the central bank, prosecution authorities, and other key 
institutions. Moldova has submitted the draft anti-fraud programme to OLAF for review.  
 
Capacity to Manage and Monitor Reforms 
The institutional capacity to manage and monitor reforms presents mixed results.  While 
the key institutions remain committed to reform implementation, significant capacity 
constraints persist. The shortage of qualified staff in internal audit units represents  a critical 
weakness affecting reform sustainability. The loss of human resources poses challenges to 
maintaining the pace and quality of reforms, requiring urgent attention to recruitment and 
retention strategies. Moreover, the mid -term review of the PFM reform strategy 2023 -2030 
noted that its action plan lacks a result monitoring framework . However, the authorities are 
addressing capacity constraints in internal audit through targeted recruitment, restructuring and 
professional development measures. The restructuring of ministry units increased auditor 
positions by 73% (from 33 to 57), with 37 recruitment competitions held between January 2025 
and April 2026, alongside strengthened certification and training. 
 
Transparency and oversight of the budget 
 
Between 2023 and 2025 the Government continued making publicly available the 
budgetary documents. The latest Open Budget Survey (OBS) for 2023, published in 2024, 
scored Moldova Budget Transparency 81/100, in ranking significantly better than its peers and 
improving the 2019 score. The budget related documents are accessible online with all relevant 
laws and regulations. The Ministry of Finance has prepared and published annual Citizens’ 
Budgets based on the enacted budgets since 2015. The Medium-Term Budget Framework for 
2025-2027, the 2026 State Budget Law, timely approved in December 2025, as well as the 
budgets of the State Social Insurance Budget and the Mandatory Health Insurance Fund are 
made publicly available in full, and easily accessible.  
 
Budget oversight continued to improve . In October 2025, the Court of Accounts (CoA), 
Moldova’s Supreme Audit Institution, extended its Development Strategy by two years, until 
2027. Additionally, a 2025 -2027 Audit Strategy was approved in December 2024 with the 
involvement of the EU and SIGMA . The first international peer review was successfully 
organised in 2022, and a follow up performance report was published in December 2025 to 
assess progress. The report notes that the CoA operates under well -developed legal and

=== PAGE 9 ===
9 
 
institutional arrangements. The CoA’s Functional independence has improved, notably with 
regards to self -organisation without undue influence and with increased freedom over 
organisation structure. Furthermore, amendments to strengthen CoA’s constitutional and 
financial independence have been adopted in June 2026.The lack of capacity hinders the work 
of the institution. However, the Court of Accounts filled 13 positions in 2025, including 7 audit 
positions, and strengthened professional development, with 119 employees participating in 45 
training activities between May and August 2026.  The dedicated Public Financial Control 
Committee in the parliament is functioning properly; however, it could benefit from further 
support and empowerment. Ethical and integrity safeguards were reinforced through adoption 
of the Code of Ethics and establishment of an Ethics Committee, aligning with ISSAI 130 and 
INTOSAI-P 20. Furthermore, Moldova established a standardised mechanism for following up 
on the Court of Accounts’ recommendations, with 31 central authorities addressing outstanding 
recommendations, 23 adopting action plans and 8 reporting no delays in implementation. The 
Court of Accounts has also strengthened public communication and transparency through live-
streamed meetings, information campaigns and cooperation with media platforms, resulting in 
more than 500 media materials and journalistic investigations based on its work. 
 
Based on the above assessment, Moldova continues to fulfil the macro-financial stability, 
PFM, and budget transparency criteria.

In accordance with the legal framework in place (Law No 100/2017 
and No. 1104/2002), the National Anticorruption Centre (NAC) 
ensures the ex -ante evaluation of risks of corruption for draft 
legislations.  
In 2025, NAC monitored 46 Government meetings durin g which 
1 072 draft normative acts were approved, out of which 694 were 
subject to the mandatory anticorruption expertise. For these 694 draft 
acts, NAC issued 699 recommendations on risk factors linked to 
corruption and included them in the anticorruption expertise reports. 
All recommendations were addressed by the authorities. Out of the 
699 recommendations, 590 were accepted and integrated into the 
final versions of draft normative acts (84,5%), and 109 were not 
accepted (15,5%). All of them were examined and included a written 
justification.  
From January to June 2026, 27 Government meetings were 
monitored, during which 521 draft normative acts were approved, 
out of which 297 were subject to the mandatory anticorruption 
expertise. NAC issued 263 recommen dations on risk factors linked 
to corruption and included them in the anticorruption expertise 
reports. All recommendations were addressed by the authorities. Out 
of 263 recommendations, 224 were accepted and incorporated into 
the final versions of the draft normative acts (85%), and 39 were not 
accepted (15%). All of them were examined and included a written 
justification.
