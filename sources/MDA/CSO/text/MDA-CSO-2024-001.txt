OVERVIEW AND METHODOLOGY
OVERVIEW 
Moldova’s internet is relatively free, though the government has imposed some limitations in recent years, particularly during 
states of emergency. Internet access has increased, but penetration rates remain lower than other countries in the region. 
The Moldovan government blocks certain news websites that allegedly disseminate fake news. Russian actors have facilitated 
the spread of disinformation on messaging applications and social media platforms. Additionally, the government, oligarchs, 
and other powerful figures have reportedly surveilled politicians, civil society actors, and businesspeople. Cyberattacks on 
both state entities and media outlets remain a significant issue. The government recently adopted a cybersecurity legislative 
package to combat these problems.
METHODOLOGY NOTE
This report assesses internet freedom in Moldova using a modified version of the publicly available Freedom on the Net 2024 
methodology (see Appendix A). Because this report is distinct from Freedom on the Net , there are no scores associated 
with this report. Additionally, the alphabetized and numerical indicators in the Freedom on the Net methodology have been 
converted to topical subheadings. 
In line with Freedom House’s Freedom in the World report, this report does not reflect conditions in Transnistria. Freedom 
in the World sometimes assesses disputed territories separately if they meet certain criteria, including boundaries that are 
sufficiently stable to allow year-on-year comparisons.

I. INTERNET ACCESS, COST, AND EQUITY 
I.1 Internet Access 
The reliability and speed of Moldova’s internet connections have generally improved. Moldova has fairly extensive internet 
coverage due to the country’s compact size and highly concentrated population.1
According to 2022 data from the International Telecommunications Union, 63.5 percent of Moldovans use the internet, 
both on fixed or mobile networks. 2 Moldova’s fixed internet service penetration rate was 33.5 percent for individuals, and 
69.7 percent for households, on par with other European countries, per data from the National Regulatory Agency for 
Electronic Communications and Information Technology (ANRCETI).3 Urban areas have greater access to the internet than 
rural locations.4 For more information on ANRCETI’s role and responsibilities, see Oversight below.
Mobile internet usage continues to grow in Moldova. ANRCETI’s 2023 report estimates that 72.5 percent of Moldovans use 
mobile internet, with its penetration rate reaching 115 percent.5 Although mobile internet connections are more accessible, 
they are slower and of lower quality than fixed-line internet connections. According to May 2024 data from the Speedtest 
Global Index, Moldova has a median mobile internet connection speed of 34.47 Mbps, and a fixed broadband speed of 128.39 
Mbps.6 Moldova implemented 3G networks in 2007 and 4G in 2014, but the move to 5G has been postponed from 2022 to 
2024 by ANRCETI, due to Moldova’s economic crisis and low levels of 5G-compatible mobile phones. 7 At the same time, 
ANRCETI has initiated the preparatory process to issue licenses for the use of frequency bands necessary to develop 5G 
networks, which may take place in late 2024.8
The development of fiber optic networks in Moldova is facilitating widespread migration to faster connections with speeds 
of 100 Mbps and above. According to ANRCETI’s 2023 data, 51.8 percent of fixed internet subscribers enjoy such speeds.9 
The national operator Moldtelecom held a monopoly on accessing international content until 2010, when other operators gained 
access to Moldtelecom’s network and received licenses to create their own gateways.10 After Moldtelecom provided operators 
with gateways to the global internet in 2010, several providers subsequently built their own international fiber connections, 
reducing service costs and increasing competitiveness.11
Moldova’s Digital Transformation Strategy of 2023-2030, 12 approved in September 2023, aims to promote sustainable 
development, build a digital society, and bolster the ICT sector.13 
I.2 Cost and Equity 
There are no significant barriers to internet access in Moldova, though low-income households have trouble accessing 
higher-speed internet.14
According to 2023 data from the UK-based company Cable, Moldova has the cheapest mobile internet among Eastern 
European countries.15 Based on ITU data from 2022, a fixed-broadband internet basket of at least 5 GB costs 1.99 percent 
of gross national income (GNI) per capita.16 However, many low-income households still cannot afford high-speed fixed or 
mobile connections. 
Since the beginning of 2024, roaming services in 30 EU and European Economic Area countries have become more 
accessible to Moldovan citizens, as all mobile operators have launched offers with significantly reduced tariffs. 17 Moldova 
and the EU agreed to roaming liberalization in 2023,18 and the European Commission accepted a proposal to include roaming 
in the Association Agreement between the EU and Moldova. 19 In February 2022, Moldova and Romania signed a bilateral 
agreement liberalizing roaming tariffs. 20 The report of the national regulatory agencies of the two countries showed a 
significant increase in roaming internet traffic in 2022.21 According to the report, the cost of the average roaming basket has 
fallen by almost 70 percent for users in Moldova.

Though there is no major gender gap among Moldovans who regularly use the internet, there are differences in terms of 
age, geographic location and income level, and those between the ages of 25–34 years make up a quarter of the country’s

Of the active internet audience in Moldova, 60 percent of users live in urban areas, and Moldova’s capitol, Chișinău, accounts for

communities. As of 2024, about 370 villages in the country do not have the technical ability to connect to optical internet.23
I.3 Internet Disruptions, Shutdowns, and Backbone Centralization 
Internet shutdowns and the blocking of social media networks are not common in Moldova. 
In April 2009, Moldova saw large-scale protests—known internationally as the “Twitter Revolution”—to reject the results 
of parliamentary elections.24 At the height of the demonstrations, several major internet providers, including the national 
operator Moldtelecom, cut off users’ access to the network. Access to some popular social platforms was prohibited for 
several days, and several web-portals were also temporarily blocked. 25 Service providers cited technical problems and 
network congestion, denying they had received any orders from the government.
In general, Moldova’s regulatory framework does not provide for any restrictions on connection. However, in accordance 
with the Law on Electronic Communications, ANRCETI is granted broader powers “to meet the needs of the state—defense 
and national security, ensure public order, prevent emergency situations and eliminate their consequences in the event of 
irreparable interference.”26 The introduction of special rules for the use of communication means is also allowed as one of 
the measures taken during a state of emergency.27 See “Suspensions under States of Emergency” below.

II. TELECOMMUNICATIONS AND INTERNET SERVICE PROVIDER

II.1 Market for Telecommunications and Internet Service Providers
The telecommunications sector in Moldova has been liberalized. There are few regulatory or legal barriers that limit the 
diversity of service providers, but market concentration does inhibit competition. There are 110 fixed internet service 
providers, three of which also provide mobile internet services.28 
Moldtelecom, the national operator, holds more than 50 percent of the broadband internet market.29 The government plans 
to reorganize Moldtelecom after encountering privatization issues due to lower-than-expected offer prices.30 Moldtelecom 
has also faced corruption scandals in recent years. 31 Based on a 2020-2022 audit, Moldova’s Court of Accounts concluded 
that Moldtelecom has been ineffectively managed, with several violations in property management and signs of fraud in 
contracts. 32  
According to 2023 data from ANRCETI, the telecom sector contributes to 2.1 percent of Moldova’s GDP. 33 Approximately 
77.2 percent of total revenues collectively belongs to the companies Orange Moldova (35.4 percent), Moldtelecom (26.2 
percent) and Moldcell (15.6 percent).  Orange Moldova owns around half of the mobile telephone market, followed by 
Moldcell (34 percent) and Moldtelecom (16 percent). In 2023, Orange Moldova’s earnings from providing fixed internet 
services grew due to its takeover of Sun Communications. 34 Moldova’s Competition Council approved the transaction, 
determining the acquisition did not pose significant obstacles to effective market competition.35 
There are no significant restrictions on entering Moldova’s telecom and information technology market. Telecommunications 
companies must obtain permission from ANRCETI before offering services, although the process is relatively simple. Service 
providers are also required to take measures that facilitate government oversight to prevent and combat cybercrime, which 
poses a cost.36 
Moldova’s legislative framework in this field is largely harmonized with EU law. The Law of Electronic Communications, in force 
since 2017, provides the overall framework for the country’s provision of telecommunication services and contains a number 
of EU directives covering the sector, including competition and market regulation. 37 The Law Regarding Access to Ownership 
and Sharing of Infrastructure Associated with Public Electronic Communications Networks, implemented in 2016,38 regulates 
service providers’ access to infrastructure and stimulates the development of competition.39 
The Law on Information Society Services 40 (until 2023, called the Law on Electronic Commerce) establishes the principles 
of regulation and conditions for the provision of information society services, the responsibilities of service providers and 
intermediaries, as well as the principle of supporting cooperation between the Republic of Moldova and other countries in 
the process of providing information society services.  The Law applies to service providers registered and/or established in 
Moldova, service providers whose activity is directed to Moldova, as well as to the services they provide, including internet 
access or permanent storage of information services.
For information regarding the 2023 Laws on Cybersecurity and the Center for Strategic Communication and Countering 
Disinformation, please see Cybersecurity and Disinformation and Manipulation of the Online Environment below respectively.
II.2 Oversight
The Law on Electronic Communications governs Moldova’s internet and telecommunications, and it appoints ANRCETI as 
the sector’s regulator.41 ANRCETI ensures the implementation of development strategies for relevant industries, protects 
the rights and interests of end users, and monitors compliance with the law. ANRCETI also regulates the management of the 
top-level domain in Moldova (.md) in accordance with internet regulations and decisions.42

ANRCETI’s activities must be independent from providers, telecom equipment manufacturers, and the government, though 
the government does have the ability to appoint ANRCETI’s leadership for four-year terms and sets term limits to eight

public services are subject to public hearings. ANRCETI’s website publishes draft acts as well as information about public

Moldova’s Ministry of Economic Development and Digitalization is charged with optimizing the regulatory framework 
in information technology, creating preconditions for technological development, promoting demonopolization of 
the domestic market and eliminating anti-competitive practices. The ministry is responsible for regulating information 
technology and the digital economy, electronic and postal communications, infrastructure and market surveillance, and 
consumer protection policies.44
In January 2025, a new Law on Cyber Security will come into force in Moldova, establishing the Agency for Cybersecurity, 
which will be responsible for the protection of critical information infrastructure, identification and accounting of service 
providers, implementation of a mandatory mechanism for reporting cyber incidents, as well as for supervision and state 
control of service providers’ compliance with the cybersecurity regulatory framework (see also “Cybersecurity”) .45

III. BLOCKING AND CONTENT REMOVAL 
III.1 Website Blocking 
The Moldovan government sometimes orders the blocking of websites. 
In recent years, orders to block websites have been issued by decisions of the Commission for Emergency Situations (CSE), 
which operates under the Law on the State of Emergency, Siege and Martial Law.46 The CSE has the right to instruct intelligence 
services and other government bodies to apply special rules to the use of communications and take certain measures to 
control and monitor the information space, including determining whether an online source is engaging in “information 
warfare” against the state.47 
The blocking of online resources continued, in October 2024 SIS ordered the blocking of, among others, the Yandex and 
RuTube platforms (Yandex subsequently moved its services to yandex.md, a domain owned by the Russian company since 
2003).48
Moldova declared a state of emergency on February 24, 2022, when Russia began its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. 49 The 
state of emergency and ANRCETI, with input from the Security and Intelligence Services (SIS), obliged internet providers 
to block websites disseminating allegedly fake news that impacted Moldovan national security, as well as those inciting 
hatred and violence.50 In March 2023, the SIS announced that it had requested the blocking of five websites publishing “false 
information in areas affecting national security,” referring to clones of Sputnik, a Russian state-owned news agency.51 
In October 2023, the CSE released a decision suggesting the introduction of new legal mechanisms for blocking online 
sources.52 The CSE determined that further websites, beyond those potentially affecting national security, could also be 
blocked if they feature “online resources that are used in the information war against the state.” The CSE’s decision required 
providers to immediately block access to the sources specified by ANRCETI and content inciting hatred and violence. 
At the request of the SIS, the CSE also suspended the license of twelve television channels (though not their online presence) 
for allegedly providing a lack of reliable information when covering events affecting national security, such as the war in 
Ukraine.53,54 The CSE also cited their ownership by persons or entities subject to international sanctions as further cause for 
suspension. At the end of May 2024, three more companies lost their broadcast licenses on financing grounds, leaving five 
more TV channels without licenses— Primul în Moldova, Accent TV, Orizont TV, Canal 2 and Canal 3. The Council for the 
Promotion of Investment Projects of National Importance of the Investment Agency determined that the audiovisual media 
whose activities had been suspended could not resume their work even after the state of emergency is lifted.
III.2 Suspensions under States of Emergency
The state of emergency following Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine was lifted at the end of 2023. However, at the SIS’s 
request, ANRCETI ordered that the blocked websites remain inaccessible, given their continued threat to national security.55 
The list of blocked sources is available on the websites of some service providers.56 
During the COVID-19 global pandemic, from March 2020 to April 2022, Moldova was also under a state of emergency. During 
this period, authorities blocked online content in a manner that was often perceived as disproportionate and arbitrary. 
In March 2020, the SIS ordered the blocking of 52 websites that allegedly spread COVID-19-related disinformation, and 
throughout the pandemic requested several other websites blocked as well. 57 These were not clearly justified by law and 
drew criticism from media observers. 58,59 In April 2021, the CSE ordered service providers to immediately block online 
content sources listed by SIS as spreading false news about COVID-19.60
Moldova’s government continues to block illegal online gambling operators. The Public Services Agency regularly updates 
the list of unauthorized websites, platforms and applications with gambling, after which ANRCETI sends requests to service

III.3 Civil Society Responds to Blocking
Civil society groups like the Internet Freedom Platform, The Internet Freedom Platform of Eastern and Central Europe and 
Eurasia, and Reporters Without Borders have voiced concerns about the integrity of these blockings. 62,63 In October 2023, 
Moldova’s Ombudsman also expressed concern about suspending television channels and blocking websites. 64 In January 
2024, media and human rights organizations issued a statement condemning a new mechanism for blocking media activities 
without requisite judicial intervention, the government’s decision to continue blocking information and media resources 
after the state of emergency issued at the beginning of the Russia-Ukraine War had expired, and the lack of transparency 
in the legislative process. 65 Media outlets have filed lawsuits to appeal the CSE’s decision, but they have faced delays and 
dismissals by trial courts.66,67 
III.4 Content Removal 
Courts in Moldova can issue orders to remove online content, especially in cases concerning the dissemination of false or 
defamatory information.
The government infrequently issues content removal requests to platforms. According to Google’s Transparency Report, since 
2011, Moldova’s government has issued eleven removal requests for 86 pieces of content.68 The majority of these requests 
occurred in 2022. From July 2020 to December 2023, Facebook removed content 91 times in response to government 
requests, with most occurring between July 2021 and June 2022 and from July–December 2023.69 Additionally, in recent years, 
the government has introduced special emergency measures granting authorities greater latitude to ban or remove content. 
Moldovan media websites have also faced pressure from Russian government agencies to delete content. The Federal 
Service for Supervision of Communications, Information Technologies and Mass Communications (Roskomnadzor) has 
repeatedly tried to influence Moldovan media coverage of Russian military operations in Ukraine. Moldovan publications 
including tv8.md, Ziarul de Garda, and Newsmaker have received demands from Roskomnadzor to remove content deemed 
as containing “false information” on the war, but their editorial boards refused to comply.70,71,72
Regional authorities in Moldova also issue content removal orders. In January 2024, activist Olga Parzany published a video 
recording of a Supervisory Board meeting of the regional television and radio company Gagauziya Radio Televizionu (GRT), 
which demonstrated elements of censorship experienced by GRT journalists to avoid critical topics.73 A former GRT website 
editor had criticized the company’s executive director for allegedly requesting the removal of content calling Gagauzian 
authorities who promised to offer residents low price natural gas false and manipulative. Additionally, the Gagauzia Executive 
Committee’s regularly sends “requests” for the publication of press releases. Moldovan NGOs condemned these activities for 
illegally interfering with the media’s editorial activities and demanded that the GRT be investigated.74 
III.5 Legal Landscape for Website Blocking and Content Removal 
Moldova’s legal framework allows filtering of internet content under certain conditions, including child sexual abuse material, 
material of an extremist or terrorist nature, or material that violates intellectual property rights. Restricting content typically 
requires judicial approval and can be challenged in court by content owners, who may seek compensation if a judge finds 
the blocking illegal and unjustified. An appeal is possible both to higher authorities and then to the European Court of 
Human Rights. During a state of emergency, tighter restrictions can and have been enforced by the government.
Courts issue warrants to authorize the majority of counterintelligence activities, including blocking access to certain 
electronic communications services, surveillance, and interception of correspondence and wiretapping. However, courts 
are limited by the 2022-2023 SIS legislative initiatives when issuing permits for special investigative measures. The SIS

Between 2022 and 2023, the Moldovan parliament’s agenda included several legislative initiatives related to tightening

of intelligence services. These include the new Law on the Information and Security Service, 76 the Law on the Exercise of 
Counterintelligence and External Intelligence Activities,77 as well as an adjustment to the Law on the Status of Information 
and Security Officer,78 which were adopted in their final readings in 2023. Some measures in the laws mirror controversial 
draft laws introduced between 2014 and 2018 known as the “Security Mandate” and “Big Brother.”79
International and national experts have repeatedly criticized these reforms for enabling the SIS to further interfere with 
citizens’ rights and freedoms without providing effective oversight. 80 The Council of Europe’s Venice Commission has 
criticized the draft Law on the Information and Security Service, warning that it grants the SIS overly broad powers with 
little democratic oversight during emergencies. 81 The Commission also warned that the draft Law’s provisions may have a 
“chilling effect” on the freedom of expression. Pressure from civil society and public consultations prompted Parliament’s 
removal of certain provisions that could harm fundamental human rights and finalized the law. However, experts point out 
that fragments of the old draft and inappropriate SIS functions remain.82 
The Law on the Exercise of Counterintelligence and External Intelligence Activities provides for prosecutorial supervision, 
which is carried out either on the prosecutor’s own initiative or on the behalf of a person who believes their rights were 
violated.83 The law also establishes parliamentary control of the SIS, charging a subcommittee with the role of initiating 
investigations or petitioning the attorney general. 
In June 2022, Moldova’s parliament passed most measures in the information security and combating disinformation bills,84 
but in the form of amendments to the Code on Audiovisual Media Services 85 aimed at countering disinformation. The law 
introduces the concept of “disinformation” and institutes fines ranging from 40,000 to 100,000 lei for TV channels and 
radio stations disseminating false information aiming to harm a person, group, or state security. Under the law, the state 
can also suspend a broadcasting license for up to seven days, subject to the prior imposition of fines. In September 2023, 
the Audiovisual Council adopted methodological standards aimed at identifying and assessing cases of disinformation. 86 In 
April 2024, the Audiovisual Council levied fines against Gagauzian regional television channels GRT and ATV for spreading 
disinformation and inciting hatred.87  
In July 2024, Parliament approved in final reading amendments to the Law on Preventing and Combating Cybercrime, 88 
which, according to the authors, aims to implement the Convention on Cybercrime ratified in 2009. After November 15, 
2024, when the updated law89 enters into force, the heads of the cybercrime units of the Ministry of Interior and SIS will be 
able to issue orders to restrict access to a web page or to remove online content from the source in accordance with the 
criteria specified in the new article 4/2 of the Law on Preventing and Combating Cybercrime. The Ministry of Interior has 
the right to order the immediate preservation of computer data or data on information flows by the service provider, where 
there is a risk of destruction or damage, in case of requests from competent authorities of other states. 
The law obliges the authorities to observe certain principles including proportionality, application of the least stringent 
technical measure to restrict access, informing about the reasons for restricting access and the appeals procedure, periodic 
review of the need to further restrict access to a certain web page, respecting the right of service providers to apply other 
measures on their own initiative to prevent abuse of their services. The relevant order will be published on the official webpage 
of the competent authority. Suspension of access to online content may be ordered for a period of 30 days with the possibility 
of extension, but not more than 90 days. An order to terminate access to a web page or to remove online content from the 
source may be appealed in court by the service provider, including the provider of online content hosting services, and/or the 
owner of the web page (online content provider). The filing of an appeal does not suspend the validity of the relevant order.

IV. ONLINE MEDIA AND THE INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT 
IV.1 Self-Censorship 
The Republic of Moldova dropped in the 2024 Global Expression Report ranking for freedom of expression to 41 out of 161, with 
77 points. The report assesses indicators such as freedom of the internet from censorship, freedom of government regulation 
of citizens’ behavior on social media, freedom from persecution for expressing one’s position on social media, freedom from 
government censorship. Freedom of expression in Moldova was categorized as “less restricted” rather than “open,” as it was a 
year ago. In 2024, Moldova became the only state previously “free” to record a deterioration in the situation.91
According to Reporters Without Borders’ 2024 Press Freedom Index, the Republic of Moldova dropped its position from 
28 to 31.92 The report noted that oligarchs and political leaders strongly influence their editorial stances. While some media 
risk tackling issues that are embarrassing for the authorities, many limit themselves to following the political agenda of the 
party to which they are affiliated.
According to interviews conducted for this report, individual online journalists engage in some self-censorship, though it 
is not widespread. In some cases, online journalists are wary of criticizing the owners of their companies or the people on 
whom they depend for funding. There has been a recent trend toward increased self-censorship, partly due to the chilling 
effect of increased website blocking and audiovisual media restrictions.
Cornelia Cozonac, president of the Center for Investigative Journalism of Moldova (CIJM), believes that the closure of 
TV channels and online media has led to a decrease in the level of criticism of the authorities, the emergence of taboo 
topics against the government and, in general, the development of self-censorship in the media, which continue to operate 
because they fear that they will be subject to similar measures. In her opinion, this carries a certain danger, as the example 
of some post-Soviet countries shows, where journalists said they were free as long as they did not criticize the government, 
the top leadership of the country. She said: “There are also known cases when the level of self-censorship increases in the 
context of prosecution of journalists after investigations, especially for young journalists who lack sufficient experience and 
the necessary legal support.”
Ivan Sveatchenko, journalist, author, and host of the Nonfiction Project on YouTube also said, 
Over the past four years, Russian propaganda has bought up Moldovan oligarchs and organized a kind of united front 
of struggle—a struggle against Moldova’s European integration, against citizens and their future, against common 
sense. That is why I feel some influence of self-censorship. I don’t want to join the huge army of trolls and bots, 
pseudo-politicians and hired civil activists who scold the authorities 24 hours a day. Sometimes the criticism is fair, 
but in the vast majority of cases it is not criticism, but an elaborate PR strategy of the Kremlin. My self-censorship 
does not mean limiting freedom of speech and freedom of the press. This is journalistic, political, and civic hygiene.
Viorica Zaharia, journalist and president of the Press Council of the Republic of Moldova, notes that in the last two years, 
journalists, especially TV journalists, have started to complain more often about self-censorship due to sanctions. However, 
in her opinion, sanctions for violating the legislation have made journalists more responsible and cautious:
Personally, I don’t notice any manifestations of self-censorship. Every time journalists ask the representatives of 
the authorities uncomfortable questions that even make them angry. I also see that in investigative media there 
are topics related to representatives of the authorities, such as Dorin Recean, 93 Mihai Popșoi,94 Natalia Gavrilița.95 
There were also cases where the EU, which is a donor, appeared in a bad light in investigations.96,97 Also in one case, 
the CU SENS team investigated how the money provided as compensation by People in Need was used by the 
Government, and People in Need was their partner in the project.98
On the eve of the presidential elections and the constitutional referendum in the fall of 2024, some media outlets refused 
to cover the election campaign, which experts explain by the tightening of legislation on disinformation and political control

The online media landscape self-censors more extensively during states of emergency, when the authorities require restraint

IV.2 Disinformation and the Manipulation of the Online Environment 
Online platforms, including social networks, are used by various political forces to disseminate disinformation and manipulate 
public opinion, especially during election campaigns and political crises. Trolls, employed by various entities, influence online 
discussions. A significant amount of misinformation is spread online through an ecosystem that includes social media 
accounts, especially Facebook pages, as well as Telegram, TikTok and YouTube channels.
President Maia Sandu has been repeatedly targeted by disinformation campaigns, including AI generated deepfakes. 102 
Additionally, a February 2024 report revealed that individuals enlisted celebrities to create a series of videos denigrating 
President Sandu through the Cameo app, which allows anyone to pay a popular person to record a personalized video. 103 
The videos were widely shared on TikTok, Facebook and Telegram, receiving hundreds of thousands of views.104
In 2023, the Stopfals.md portal reportedly debunked more than 150 cases of information manipulation in the public space, 
which primarily concerned pro-Kremlin propaganda targeting the social, security, and defense sectors, as well as Moldova’s 
process of European integration.105
Moldova is also targeted by networks of inauthentic accounts linked to oligarchs. In the first quarter of 2024, Meta removed 
more than 1,326 Facebook accounts, 80 pages, a group and an Instagram account that targeted users from Moldova and

IV.3 Online Media Environment and Market
Though online media outlets are not required to formally register with the government, they suffer from a lack of advertising 
and control by oligarchs. The 2023 State of the Press Index highlights that outlets linked to powerful oligarchs are better 
resourced than their competitors. 127 Additionally, Reporters Without Borders notes that the country is facing economic 
difficulties caused by the collapse of the advertising market following the Kremlin’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine.128 According 
to experts, Moldovan outlets struggle to diversify funding through subscriptions, advertising, private donations, or reader 
donations. 
According to the 2023 Vibrant Information Barometer (VIBE),129 advertising placements remain politicized, and Moldova’s very 
modest advertising market is concentrated in the capital, Chișinău. Advertising revenues for all traditional media decreased 
significantly as advertisers shifted their focus to social networks. 
At the national level, online media are not required to register with the authorities, unlike “traditional” media. However, in 
February 2024, officials in Gagauzia introduced a bill that would require regional media outlets to apply for a permit (the so-
called Izin), a move that was widely criticized by civil society as an attempt to impede journalists through arbitrary mechanisms 
and demanded the bill’s immediate withdrawal.130,131
Questions have also been raised about the independence of the national public television and radio broadcasting company. 
In March 2024, the Council of Europe’s Committee of Ministers called on Moldovan authorities to immediately remove the 
public broadcaster Teleradio-Moldova (TRM) from parliamentary control.132 The Council requested that Moldova “urgently” 
review its audiovisual legislation, which returned the control of public media to Parliament in 2021, leading to interference 
in the mechanisms guaranteeing the independence of the Audiovisual Council and the TRM.

IV.5 Online Organizing 
There are few restrictions on online gatherings, and digital mobilization is a common feature of political life in Moldova.

effectively to organize protests and mobilize around a range of issues. In addition, ethnic and linguistic minorities have 
actively campaigned online and offline to advocate for various changes in government policies.
In recent years, parliamentary and extra-parliamentary opposition, represented mainly by political forces supported by

published online. The Ombudsman condemned the authorities’ actions against the protesters.142 
Online mobilization has played an important role in organizing demonstrations in support of Moldova’s integration into

V.1 Constitutional Protections for Individuals Rights Online 
Moldova’s constitution guarantees freedom of expression (Article 32), the right to access information relating to public 
affairs, and the prohibition of media censorship (Article 34).145
The 2010 Law on Freedom of Expression aims to guarantee the exercise of the right to freedom of expression, as well as the 
balance between ensuring this right and protecting the dignity and privacy of a person and their privacy.146 Moldova’s national 
legislation does not clearly define who can be considered a journalist and does not indicate whether these rights extend to 
online journalists and digital influencers.
Although Moldova’s constitution and laws nominally protect digital rights, the judiciary lacks the independence to 
consistently and fairly enforce these protections for online freedom of expression. According to a February 2024 national 
survey conducted by the Institute for European Policy and Reform (IEPR) and Magenta Consulting, only 22 percent of 
respondents trust Moldova’s judicial system. 147 Judicial independence in Moldova remains an issue due to corruption and 
selective justice, where the law is applied unequally due to political motivations.148 
Moldovan media and journalists continue to face obstacles accessing information. In June 2023, Ialoveni Online news reporters 
attempted to obtain copies of draft decisions of Costesti’s village council, but Costesti mayor Vasile Borta instructed the 
council not to provide the documents. 149 Nine Moldovan civil society groups released a statement condemning Borta and 
imploring local officials to follow the law requiring draft decisions to be published in advance of meetings. Public officials have 
also invoked the Law on Personal Data Protection, State and Commercial Secrets to avoid providing information to outlets and 
journalists not deemed as part of the “official” media.150 Media representatives and civil society organizations have repeatedly 
criticized the limitations on access to data on the national courts’ information portal. 
Moldova’s Law on Access to Information of Public Interest entered into force in January 2024, introducing multiple changes 
to how the public can access information managed by public institutions. The law incorporated proposals from civil society 
and media organizations, including extending the list of groups obliged to provide information of public interest and 
shortening the deadline for providing information to media representatives. 151 The new law aims to harmonize national 
legislation with the standards of the Tromsø Convention, or the Council of Europe Convention on Access to Official 
Documents, which was ratified by Moldova in 2013.152 
As part of measures taken by the Commission for Emergency Situations (CES), media and online resources faced a number 
of restrictions during and after the states of emergency for COVID-19 (2020-2022) and Russia’s full-scale war against Ukraine 
(2022-2023) (see the corresponding section).
V.2 Laws Criminalizing Online Speech and Activity 
There are several laws that assign penalties for online activities. 
Although the Moldovan government nominally decriminalized defamation in 2004 per Council of Europe recommendations, 
individuals can still be found guilty of posting defamatory statements online and receive civil and criminal punishments. 153 
The Law on Freedom of Expression allows for injured parties to receive financial compensation for defamation. 154 Article 
70 of the Contravention Code provides punishment for slander with community service and fines increasing in severity if 
the crime involves an accusation for a penal offense or is motivated by bias, though they do not include prison time.155 The 
Contravention Code also stipulates fines and restrictions on certain activities for insults, including written expressions and 
images (Article 69) and actions inciting discrimination (Article 70/1). Likewise, violators can be fined or required to perform 
community service, but not imprisoned. The law also includes penalties for harassment (Article 70/2) the failure to comply 
with personal data processing and use laws (Article 74/1), violations of copyright (Article 96), and the dissemination of

VI.1 Legal Penalties for Online Speech and Activities 
Moldova’s constitution and laws prohibit arbitrary arrest and detention and provide for the right of any person to challenge the 
lawfulness of their arrest or detention in court. According to the U.S. Department of State, Moldova’s government generally 
observes these protections, and individuals are rarely detained for online activities protected under international human rights

have been detained in Moldova, outside of Transnistria, since 2019.164 
Moldovan citizens are generally free to express their opinions online without fear of legal penalties, but several recent 
prosecutions for online activity have raised concerns. Non-governmental media organizations have reported several cases 
of libel sanctions against journalists. Additionally, since the Kremlin’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the Prosecutor’s Office 
for Combating Organized Crime and Special Cases (PCCOCS) has launched criminal investigations into war propaganda and 
actions aimed at stoking national, ethnic, racial and religious division. 165 In one such case, the Prosecutor’s Office, the SIS, 
and the Internal Security and Anti-Corruption Service announced in March 2022 that they had opened an investigation into 
the actions of individuals “disseminating biased and fabricated information to the public” regarding the war in Ukraine.166 
Moldovan authorities also pressure journalists through administrative cases. To illustrate, in January 2022, the Police 
Inspectorate of Chișinău’s Buiucani sector opened an administrative libel case against Jurnal TV, a Moldovan news channel, 
after it aired an investigation about a former police officer. The Chișinău Court fined the channel’s Managing Director, Val 
Butnaru, and banned Butnaru from holding leadership positions in the media for six months. Civil society groups criticized 
the ruling as disproportionate, considering it a dangerous precedent for press freedom. 167 In February 2023, the Chișinău 
Court of Appeal overturned the decision, and the case was sent for retrial with a different panel of judges.168 
In another instance, in February 2024, the SIS and PCCOCS revealed they had investigated the activities of a Telegram group 
that posted fascist and anti-Semitic messages denying the Holocaust throughout 2023.169 The Telegram channel was deleted 
after criminal proceedings began and the PCCOCS had summoned the suspects. In its press release concerning the case, the 
SIS stated that it is observing a trend of “irresponsible” online behavior with a risk of “undermining the peaceful coexistence 
of the multinational and multilingual people of the Republic of Moldova.”170
Digital influencers whose content elicits negative reactions from the public also garner law enforcement attention. In May 
2024, TikTok user Ecaterina Gogolev posted erotic photos of herself posing in a Chișinău cemetery. The pictures sparked

Article 54 of the Criminal Code, which prohibits the desecration of graves and revered objects.171 In another case, three young 
people were detained after they tried to sleep in a coffin in a Chișinău cemetery during a TikTok livestream. According to 
police, they were fined for hooliganism and then released.172 GenderDoc-M Information Center also reports that a precedent 
was set in Moldova when, after the Chișinău Pride March 2024, the police fined two TikTokers who published hate speech 
videos against the LGBTQI+ community—one charge for hooliganism, the other for inciting hatred.173

VII.1 Restrictions on Encryption and Anonymity 
There is no legal requirement for website owners, bloggers, or users to register with Moldova’s government.187 In April 2022, 
Moldova’s parliament considered amendments to the Law on the Intelligence and Security Service that aimed to place new 
restrictions on electronic communications.188  The amendments would have required webpage owners to display their contact 
information—including their first and last names, state ID numbers, phone numbers, email addresses and location—or have 
their page blocked. The amendments were ultimately not adopted due to difficulties in implementation and concerns for 
rights. 
Anonymous communication and encryption tools are not prohibited or restricted in Moldova. As of March 2023, Moldova 
does not have any SIM-card registration requirements.189 
VII.2 State Surveillance and Legal Protections for Privacy  
Moldova’s legislation provides for the protection of privacy from unlawful and unjustified intrusion and the arbitrary surveillance 
of citizens. Moldova’s constitution guarantees the protection of privacy (Article 28), as well as the privacy of correspondence, 
telephone conversations and other legal types of communication (Part 1 of Article 30). 190 Under Article 54, privacy protections 
may be restricted in cases concerning national security, the country’s economic well-being, public order and the prevention 
of crime. 
Despite constitutional guarantees, the Moldovan government’s surveillance of online activities often violates users’ right to 
privacy. Recent reform, which was accompanied by criticism from civil society, expanded the internet surveillance powers 
of the SIS (see related section 3) . A special technical unit of the SIS intercepts electronic communications, which are 
transmitted to units of other agencies.191 By law, the SIS should only listen to conversations if it is expressly ordered by the 
court or the prosecutor in the case of obvious crimes and urgent circumstances.
Internet and mobile service providers are required to install SIS-compatible equipment in their ICT infrastructure to provide 
seamless access to internet traffic and subscriber information (see the following section). Evidence of surveillance abuse by 
the government has been repeatedly uncovered in recent years. In multiple cases, Moldovan authorities illegally recorded 
conversations and correspondence between civil society activists and political figures, some of which were published online. 
In October 2021, the Anti-Corruption Prosecutor’s Office charged five employees of the Fifth Directorate of the National 
Investigation Inspectorate, including the former head of the directorate, for illegally wiretapping and surveilling opposition 
representatives, civil activists, and journalists.192 The ongoing criminal case is based on a 2019 investigation by Rise Moldova, 
which revealed that law enforcement agencies wiretapped and monitored more than 50 individuals as part of a surveillance 
operation between 2017 and 2019.193 Moldova’s current president, Maia Sandu was a target. 194 Some victims claimed police 
used wiretapping to open criminal cases against them.
In November 2021, non-governmental organizations signed a declaration calling on the Ministry of Internal Affairs to 
investigate Chiril Motpan, then-chairman of the parliamentary commission on national security, who claimed at a press 
briefing that Moldova’s Democratic Party ordered security forces to wiretap about a hundred people, including journalists, 
public figures and politicians. 195 Under the Democratic Party’s rule, authorities had carried out surveillance on activists, 
opposition leaders, and European experts. For instance, Irmantas Mikelionis, a consultant working on an EU-funded project 
to strengthen Moldova’s law enforcement capabilities, claimed he had received an envelope with his photographs and 
anonymous letter detailing his surveillance. The Anti-Corruption Prosecutor’s Office began a criminal investigation into this 
case in 2019, but never completed it.196

VII.3 Data Protection, Retention, and Access
Moldova adopted its Law on Personal Data Protection in July 2011. Under the law, the government formed the National 
Center for the Protection of Personal Data, a special supervisory body empowered to register data privacy violations, 
conduct inspections, and impose fines. 207 Moldova enforces the Council of Europe’s Convention for the Protection of 
Individuals with regard to Automatic Processing of Personal Data and other international agreements to which Moldova 
is a party. On July 25, 2024, Parliament approved the new Law on Personal Data Protection  208 to harmonize its national 
legislation with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).209 The law will enter into force on August 24, 2026.
Citizens, officials and legal entities may be held liable under Contravention Code Article 74/1 for the processing of personal 
data in violation of data protection laws. A separate provision (Article 74/3) establishes liability for failure to comply with 
the regulations of the National Center for the Protection of Personal Data, which prescribes periods for the restoration of 
personal data rights, including the blocking, suspension or termination of the processing of personal data.
Moldovan legislation does not require service providers to monitor traffic or content. Public access points are not required to 
identify, track or store customers’ personal data and information. However, according to Law on Electronic Communications, 
providers must facilitate the interception of electronic communications, in cases concerning national security, public order, 
and criminal prosecution, by law enforcement with an order from a judge. 210 Service providers are required to provide the 
SIS with free and prompt access to all equipment interfaces, their electronic communications networks, and the conditions 
necessary to connect special equipment to listen to user communications within court authorization.

the lawlessness in the Prosecutor’s Office and the Ministry of Justice.” 219 For three weeks, the anonymous site published
