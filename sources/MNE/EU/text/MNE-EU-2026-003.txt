1. INTRODUCTION1 
1.1. CONTEXT 
Accession negotiations with Montenegro were opened in June 2012. To date, all 33 
negotiating chapters have been opened, of which 21 have been provisionally closed . During 
the reporting period, Montenegro has provisionally closed 14 negotiating chapters. The 
Council established the  ad hoc working party on drafting the Accession Treaty with 
Montenegro in April 2026.  In June 2026, the Commission presented a draft financial 
package, followed by the adoption of conclusions by the Council on 12 October 2026. In the 
Commission’s assessment, all closing benchmarks have been met and Montenegro has 
therefore reached the final stage of accession negotiations. These important achievements 
are the result of the continued political commitment of the Montenegrin authorities to the 
strategic goal of European integration, which has been consistently stated as the key priority 
for the country, and to implementing the related reforms.  Building on a whole of society 
approach, i t is essential that the current momentum is sustained with focused and cross -
party efforts to ensure that preparations for EU membership are completed in time. 
Montenegro continued to implement the Stabilisation and Association Agreement (SAA), and 
meetings of the joint bodies under the agreement took place at regular intervals. 
Montenegro has actively engaged in and continued to deliver on the implementation of the 
Growth Plan for the Western Balkans across its four pillars of gradual integration with the EU: 
the single market, regional economic integration, fundamental reforms and increased 
financial support. Under the Reform and Growth Facility 2, a performance- based EU 
instrument supporting the EU accession process, Montenegro has been implementing its 
Reform Agenda adopted in September 2024. 
The Commission’s 2026 rule of law report3 covered Montenegro for the third time.

broadly aligned with European and international democratic standards, as well as the EU 
acquis. Some efforts are still needed to address key outstanding recommendations by 
the Organisation for Security and Co -operation in Europe / Office for Democratic Institutions

Public administration reform 
Montenegro has a  good level of preparation in the area of  public administration reform and 
has made good progress notably by adopting the Law on free access to information, the Law

responsibility as well as related secondary legislation  on the Law on civil servants and state 
employees for the central civil service. Montenegro needs to continue efforts in further 
strengthening the management and planning of human resources for regular filling of 
vacancies and the application of a new competency framework.   
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were broadly implemented. In the 
coming year, Montenegro should in particular:  
→ further strengthen the coordination of  public administration reform through effective 
monitoring, and stronger political steering, implement effectively the Law on free access 
to information, and establish a functional system of administrative supervision;   
→ reinforce a professional, merit- based civil service by consistently applying merit -based 
recruitment throughout the new competence framework, reducing acting appointments 
and non- standard employment, improving human resources planning and monitoring, 
and strengthening performance management and professional development systems;

Chapter 23 - Judiciary and fundamental rights 
Montenegro has a good level of preparation in applying the EU acquis  and European 
standards in this area, and has made good progress overall. Particular focus should now be 
placed on the effective enforcement of the legislative reforms, further strengthening 
administrative capacity in the areas of the judiciary, the fight against corruption, media 
freedom and fundamental rights.  
Functioning of the judiciary 
Montenegro’s judicial system has  a good level of preparation  and has made good progress. 
Montenegro adopted constitutional amendments, strengthening the independence of the 
Judicial and Prosecutorial Councils, fully in line with EU and Venice Commission 
recommendations. I n addition, Montenegro adopted amendments to the Law on the Judicial 
Council and Judges, the Law on the State Prosecution Service, and the Law on the Special 
State Prosecutor’s Office, addressing recommendations from the European Commission, the 
Venice Commission and GRECO. Montenegro appointed judges to the Constitutional Court, 
restoring its full composition and strengthening its effective functioning. Furthermore, two 
lay members to the Judicial Council and three lay members to the Prosecutorial Council were 
appointed, improving effective functioning.  The institutional stability and leadership of the 
Supreme Court, Supreme State Prosecution Office, and Special Prosecution Office  was 
further improved through targeted reforms.  
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were mostly implemented. In the 
coming year, Montenegro should, in particular: 
→ enforce effectively the constitutional amendments by harmonising secondary legislation, 
namely the Law on the Judicial Council and Judges and the Law on the State Prosecution 
Service, and appoint members of the Judicial and Prosecutorial Councils;  
→ continue to strengthen judicial independence and ensure full and effective 
implementation of the improved strategic and legal framework, including the 
Constitution, in line with the EU acquis  and European standards on judicial independence 
and accountability, irremovability, impartiality, integrity, efficiency and professionalism of 
the judicial system; 
→ continue improving the overall efficiency of the justice system, including by strengthening 
the human, financial, infrastructure and ICT capacity of the justice system and by 
ensuring the full implementation of the Plan for rationalisation of the court system and 
the Judicial Digitalisation Strategy.  
Fight against corruption 
Montenegro has a good level of preparation  in the fight against corruption. Good  progress 
was made on the implementation of reforms. The process to adopt two sets of amendments 
to the Constitution is ongoing. T wo out of three votes have taken place in Parliament  to 
adopt a constitutional amendment removing immunity for the Prime Minister and ministers 
covering all corruption- related offences. In addition, Parliament held a first of three required 
votes on a constitutional amendment responding to a key recommendation of the Venice 
Commission, by introducing prior integrity checks for prime ministerial and ministerial 
nominees. Both amendments have yet to be finally adopted by Parliament. This will be a 
major achievement, addressing longstanding GRECO and Venice Commission 
recommendations. Parliament adopted amendments to the legal framework regulating the

Fundamental rights 
The legislative and institutional framework on fundamental rights is largely in place and 
Montenegro continues to largely meet its international obligations on human rights. 
However, more effort is needed to fully implement this framework and to ensure access to 
justice and enforcement of rights in administrative and judicial proceedings, in particular for 
groups in vulnerable situation. Montenegro needs to continue aligning the legal framework 
with the EU acquis and European standards. The groups in most vulnerable situation in 
society (including Roma 4 and Egyptians, people with minority racial or ethnic background, 
persons with disabilities, LGBTIQ+ persons) continued to be subjected to discrimination, hate 
speech and hate crime.

remain valid. In the coming year Montenegro needs, in particular, to:

Freedom of expression and media freedom 
Montenegro has a good level of preparation in the area of freedom of expression. Good 
progress was achieved on the implementation of reforms. Montenegro continues to uphold a 
zero-tolerance policy on violence against journalists and media workers, while dedicated 
legislation on lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs) must be adopted. Media 
ownership transparency improved through the public registry, with gaps for online media.  
Parliament appointed new members to the Council of the Radio and Television of 
Montenegro (RTCG) and to the Council of the Agency for Audiovisual Media Services (AMU). 
A new Law aligning with the EU Digital Services Act Directive was adopted. Access to 
information must be effectively guaranteed through the full implementation of the improved 
legal framework. The national media strategy continued to be implemented, and Montenegro 
continues to enjoy a pluralistic media environment and provides adequate legal guarantees 
for the freedom of expression and an enabling environment for the media to operate freely, 
independently and professionally. While media ownership transparency improved, concerns 
about external strong influence over media in Montenegro have increased. 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were fully implemented. In the coming 
year, Montenegro needs, in particular, to: 
→ continue providing prompt and effective law enforcement and judicial protection for 
journalists against all forms of violence, while prioritising investigations and judicial 
follow-up in critical cold cases; 
→ ensure full implementation of the media legal framework, and ensure that any future 
legislative amendments preserve the progress achieved, particularly with regard to 
safeguarding the independence of the two Councils; 
→ ensure continued implementation of all media- related legislation and further align 
national media legislation with the EU acquis, including the European Media Freedom Act.

→ further strengthen border management capacity with the completion of border

ECONOMIC CRITERIA 
The existence of a functioning market economy 
Montenegro has made good progress and reached a good level of preparation in developing 
a functioning market economy.  
The recently announced ‘Euro Model’ reforms may pose significant risks to the sustainability 
of public finances and the external balance, which remain nonetheless to be assessed 
further, notably following on- going consultations with the stakeholders. Economic growth 
slowed down amid a continued weak export performance in 2025, before picking up in the 
second quarter of 2026. The budget deficit overshot the 2025 target, and fiscal 
vulnerabilities remain due to ineffective fiscal rules, a high level of mandatory spending and 
high debt rollover needs. The fiscal governance framework has been strengthened, notably 
through the establishment of the Fiscal Council. The large current account deficit persisted 
with only about a third of it covered by FDI inflows . The labour market situation continued to 
improve, even though structural problems persist, such as high rates of youth and long-term 
unemployment. The banking sector remained well capitalised and liquid while credit growth 
picked up. Good progress was made in improving the business environment and advancing 
the reform of state-owned enterprises (SOEs). 
The Commission’s recommendations from last year were mostly implemented. To improve

The capacity to cope with competitive pressure and market forces within the EU 
Montenegro has made good progress and is between a mod erate and a good level of 
preparation to cope with competitive pressure and market forces in the EU. The authorities

Efforts to advance the green and digital transitions have continued. The diversification of 
Montenegro’s narrow production base remains a challenge. The economy is driven by 
services, in particular tourism, which together with the small size of local companies and 
their low level of exports, constrain productivity growth and the competitivene ss of local 
firms.

Chapter 32: Financial control

2. CLUSTER 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS OF THE ACCESSION PROCESS 
2.1. FUNCTIONING OF DEMOCRATIC INSTITUTIONS AND PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION REFORM 
2.1.1 Democracy 
The functioning of Montenegro’s democratic institutions is generally stable. However, they 
remain vulnerable to political crises and potential institutional blockages. Some efforts are 
necessary to fully align the legal framework with democratic standards and the EU acquis, 
including on inclusive, transparent and resilient electoral processes.  
Elections

=== PAGE 23 ===
22 
 
No national elections took place  during the reporting period. The 2022 elections in 
Savnik have not been completed. The legal framework regulating the funding of political 
parties and electoral campaigns has been amended in July 2026 to address key GRECO and 
OSCE/ODIHR recommendations regarding its scope, clarity and implementation .  The 
amendments to the Law on financing of political entities and electoral campaigns increased 
the transparency and oversight of political parties' spending, including through dissuasive 
penalties. Amendments to the Law on the Election of Councillors and Members of 
Parliament further clarified the selection criteria for the chair and members of the Central 
Election Commission, introduced that a voter can support one or more electoral lists for the 
election of councillors and further increased the accessibility of persons with disabilities to 
elections. Furthermore, the amendments build on the 2025 legislative amendments 
introducing a 40% quota for the less -represented gender by further specifying that a 
candidate of the less -represented gender will be replaced by the next candidate of the less -
represented gender, even if the earlier candidate does not accept the mandate. Amendments 
to the law on the election

While Montenegro's legal framework protects the right of people with disabilities to 
participate in political and electoral life on the same basis as other people, there remain 
significant gaps in the implementation of these legal mandates. 
Parliament 
Parliament continued to function regularly and constructively for most of the reporting 
period, with intense legislative activity. During the reporting period, 37 sittings were held, 
inlcuding 18 regular, nine special and 10 extraordinary sessions. Several constitutional 
amendments and a total of 315 laws were adopted. To manage this workload, Parliament 
established a new Department for Legislation to review draft laws and their compliance with 
the EU acquis . However, the legislative process continued to rely on urgent procedures, 
particularly for EU-related legislation.  
The overall constructive relations between the parliamentary majority and the 
opposition deteriorated in mid -March 2026, following the adoption of the Law on internal 
affairs and the Law on the National Security Agency, which were not fully aligned with the EU 
acquis. This delayed decisions that required qualified majorities, most notably 
constitutional amendments and the two long -delayed appointments to the Constitutional 
Court. The opposition also repeatedly criticised the ruling majority for allocating insufficient 
time for debate. In July and August, following a cross -party agreement, Parliament 
overwhelmingly adopted amendments to the Constitution to enhance the independence of 
the judiciary and and the Central Bank of Montenegro as well as to advance various key

=== PAGE 24 ===
23 
 
reforms on a cross-party basis. Parliament appointed the two remaining Constitutional Court 
judges and completed several appointments to independent institutions, but has yet to fill 
certain long -standing vacancies, such as the chairperson and the members of the Central 
Electoral Commission and two lay members of the Judicial Council. Opposition parties 
suspended their cooperation with the government and ruling majority in September after 
Parliament Speaker sent condolences to the son of convicted war criminal Ratko Mladić 
following his death. A debate and vote for his dismissal were held in October, and the 
attempt to remove him failed. Parliamentary committees examined numerous reports 
submitted by independent bodies. Parliament held eight special sittings dedicated to

EU integration 
The body in charge of EU accession negotiations, headed by the Chief Negotiator, 
coordinates the country’s EU accession negotiations satisfactorily. Coordination of the 
Ministry of European Affairs was strengthened with the appointment of a second State 
Secretary.  
The acceleration of EU accession preparations resulted in the provisional closure of eleven 
additional negotiating chapters during the reporting period. In January 2026, the government 
adopted the Programme for the Accession of Montenegro to the European Union 2026 -2027 
and continued to submit quarterly accession reports to Parliament. The digital platform 
28by28.eu was launched to communicate on Montenegro's path to EU membership.  
Governance 
The government  functioned within a broadly stable political environment, despite the 
withdrawal of one coalition party from the ruling majority in January. This withdrawal 
prompted the resignations of the Deputy Prime Minister for Infrastructure and Regional 
Development and the Transport Minister.  In late 2025, acting on the Prime Minister’s 
proposal, Parliament dismissed the Minister of Sports and Youth. At the end of July, a 
government reshuffle took place, resulting in the appointment of a Deputy Prime Minister 
and three ministers.   
The government  has prioritised EU accession as its central objective. It remains essential 
that its established timeline does not impact the  quality and sustainability of reforms. 
Transparency remains a concern, given the government’s frequent use of conference calls to 
adopt decisions, bypassing formal cabinet sessions. Interministerial coordination  and 
cooperation between the President and the government should be further improved.   
In February, the government  submitted to Parliament a draft Law on government, which is 
still pending adoption by Parliament. In its follow -up opinion published in June, the Venice 
Commission acknowledged Montenegro’s government adopting several key 
recommendations in the revised draft law but emphasised the need for full alignment with 
the Constitution. In October, the government addressed the Venice Commission’s 
recommendation by preparing a constitutional amendment introducing mandatory prior 
integrity checks for the Prime Minister -designate and members of the government. The first 
of the three required parliamentary votes for its adoption took place in October 2026. 
In September 2026, Parliament adopted a proposal to amend the Constitution to remove the 
immunity of the Prime Minister and Ministers in cases of criminal offences in breach of 
official duties, covering all corruption -related offences. This proposal aims at addressing an 
existing recommendation from GRECO.  Following a second positive vote,  the amendment is 
currently progressing through the relevant constitutional procedures, which include  a public 
consultation process and a third and final vote to adopt the Constitutional Amendments with 
two-thirds majority of all members of Parliament.  
Montenegro’s decentralisation remains limited, largely restricted to utility services, water 
supply and the maintenance of public spaces. While local authorities formally possess

strengthens inter-municipal and central-local cooperation, enhances the accountability of the

Civil society 
Civil society, including human rights defenders, generally operates  in an enabling 
environment. An overall legal and institutional framework for civil society is in place.  
The mandate of the Council for Cooperation with NGOs was renewed and resumed its work 
in December 2025. While the government formally acknowledges civil society as a partner in 
policymaking, civil society organisations are not consistently included in consultations on 
key legislation despite repeated requests for involvement . Furthermore, during the reporting 
period, high-level public officials repeatedly launched public attacks, smear campaigns and 
discrediting narratives targeting human rights defenders.   
In late 2025, the government  established a working group to draft the Law on 
Representativeness of Organisations of Persons with Disabilities. The first meeting took 
place in December 2025.  
The practical application of the 2022- 2026 Strategy for Cooperation remains insufficient in 
ensuring adequate funding for NGOs and their meaningful participation in public 
consultations. Authorities have started preparations for the next strategy for 2027-2031. 
Although the Law on NGOs requires ministries to issue calls for proposals for funding, 
Montenegro must further enhance ministries’ capacity to strategically plan, monitor and 
evaluate financial support to civil society organisations as well as the co nsistency of 
implementation of the funding rules across ministries. 
2.1.2. Public administration reform  
Strategic framework for public administration reform  
The strategic framework for public administration is in place. Implementation of the public 
administration reform strategy improved at an accelerated pace in 2026 . The strategy is 
financed from the budgets of the institutions responsible for reform objectives and activities, 
ensuring the financial and fiscal sustainability of its implementation. The Public 
Administration Reform Council continued to act as a central political oversight mechanism. 
Policy development and coordination  
The quality of strategic documents improved and the execution rates of planned activities in 
internal government documents increased. The methodology for medium -term planning in 
the government and ministries has been finalised. The recently adopted Law on Budget and 
Fiscal Responsibility strengthens the links between strategic plans and the budget. The rate 
of implementation of the national plan on the EU acquis  has improved, reflecting the 
accelerated EU accession process . However, the inter- ministerial coordination in policy 
making remains to be improved. 
The regulatory framework on evidence -based policymaking, including regulatory impact 
assessments (RIAs) and public consultations, is in place. A new instruction on conducting 
RIAs and preparing RIA reports was adopted, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of

across line ministries. The e-Participation and e-Petitions portals are still not fully utilised.    
Public financial management 
Public financial management  continued to improve through reforms aligned with EU 
standards and accession requirements. Implementation of the Public Finance Management 
Reform Programme 2022- 2026 is on track, and a new Public Finance Management Reform 
Programme has been adopted. On fiscal risk  monitoring, the fiscal risks statement is 
currently prepared on a regular basis, its scope, however, needs to be further widened. The

through the adoption of the new Law on budget and fiscal responsibility, which strengthens 
the medium-term budgetary framework , improves fiscal discipline and sustainability, and 
introduces a mid-year budget review.  
Public investment management continued to advance through the development of new tools 
and enhanced appraisal and selection procedures  for major projects, although investment 
planning occurs outside of these procedures. These improvements follow the adoption of a 
revised annual procedure in December 2025, which has strengthened 
coordination, scrutiny and transparency in project evaluation. In May 2026, the government 
of Montenegro adopted the Master Plan for Infrastructure Development 2026- 2030, which 
represents the first single pipel ine of capital investment projects with national and 
municipality investments financed from the central budget, on a sectoral basis.  
Although a budget inspection function was established, its administrative capacity needs to 
be further strengthened, in line with regulatory improvements introduced by the recent 
amendments to the Law on budget inspection, adopted in December 2025. 
Budget transparency is largely ensured through the publication of all budget documentation, 
monthly reporting on the status of the budget execution, timely publication of annual 
financial statements and the audit report of the State Audit Institution. In 2026 the Ministry of 
Finance started preparation to issuing a citizen’s budget.  
Public service and human resources management  
The legal and institutional framework for a professional and merit -based civil service  is in 
place Key secondary legislation to implement the Law on civil servants and state employees 
was adopted in January 2026, together with the establishment of a new competency -based 
recruitment model for all job categories. The delayed adoption of staffing plans for 2025, and 
the adoption in April 2026 of a partial annual recruitment plan for the civil service (which 
excludes the Ministry of Interior), has delayed recruitments to the civil service and led to an 
increased number of acting appointments. Regarding the local level, the regulatory 
framework on local governance is completed, with the adoption of the Law on local civil 
servants and state employees and the Law on Local Self -government, together with related 
secondary legislation. Continuous  efforts are therefore 
needed in conducting recruitments most effectively,  planning open vacancies more 
credibly, filling those timely and reducing the number on acting appointments. Professional 
human resources management is broadly in place. However, the capacity of the Human 
Resource Management Agency’s needs to be further increased. While the process of job 
systematisation in the civil service has continued, its link to organisational reforms and the 
new competence framework still needs to be strengthened.  The information system for the 
management of human resources still needs to be improved, as does the reliability of data. 
A comprehensive salary reform is needed to ensure a merit- based remuneration system that 
matches job descriptions with appropriate salary structures across public sector. The 
government adopted as a first step a roadmap for linking the new competence framework

Accountability of administration  
There is a legal framework that sets good standards for the lines of accountability, the legal 
responsibilities of s upervisory and subordinated bodies, performance management and 
reporting requirements.  However, the practice of administrative supervision as per the 
relevant legislation still needs to be fully ensured. Specific guidelines on managerial 
accountability have been prepared, and the process needs to continue to be streamlined 
across all line ministries. [see more Chapter 32] 
To close the regulatory gap, Montenegro adopted the Law on free access to information in 
December 2025 and related amendments in July 2026 and has developed and adopted the 
relevant secondary legislation. The legal basis is in place to ensure the citizens’ right to good 
administration in administrative procedures and judicial review. The Administrative Court 
has been able to significantly reduce its backlog and has reduced the average duration of 
proceedings from 1 422 days in 2023 to 614 days in 2025, particularly in cases concerning 
access to information.  
Service delivery to citizens and businesses 
The Law on administrative procedures and the Law on electronic government provide a 
sound framework for a user -centric delivery of services. Harmonisation of ongoing 
amendments to sectoral primary legislation continues to be done in line with the standards 
of the General Law on Administrative Procedures. Public service delivery  has improved 
through the implementation of the Digital Services Plan 2025- 2027, which includes the 
expansion of digital services on the central e- government portal and continued data 
exchange through the Government Service Bus. The availability of digital services was 
further expanded by increasing the number of electronic services on the central e-
government portal. While the expansion of connections continues across institutions and 
electronic registers, meeting the 2026 target of 50 interconnected register pairs will require 
an increased effort to accelerate delivery (see more in Chapter 10). 
The more effective use and promotion of the electronic identification (eID) system still need 
to be ensured to enable the digital transformation. Citizens’ satisfaction with public services 
still needs to be measured in a systematic manner, so that user feedback can serve as a 
basis for improving services.

Management bodies 
The composition of and appointment procedures for the Judicial and Prosecutorial Councils

Montenegro adopted legal amendments to the Law on the Judicial Council and Judges, and

European standards. Some implementation challenges remain and must be addressed. 
These include delays in transparent and merit -based high-level appointments, administrative 
capacities and undue public comments on ongoing cases.

Freedom of expression and media freedom 
Montenegro provides adequate legal guarantees on the freedom of expression and the 
enabling environment for free, independent, and prefessional operation of media. 
Intimidation of journalists 
Montenegro continues to uphold a policy of zero tolerance of violence against journalists 
and media workers, while isolated incidents of attacks occur. In 2025, the authorities 
maintained a prompt and generally effective institutional and law enforcement response to 
new cases of violence against journalists,  most of which involved harassment and online 
threats. In 2025, monitoring organisations recorded at least 33 incidents of intimidation, 
most of which involved online threats, verbal intimidation and harassment related to political 
reporting, while a smaller number included physical attacks. Women journalists were 
disproportionately affected. Several cases in 2025 were characterised as alledgely strategic 
lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs) by defendants.  
In 2025, the prosecution authorities handled 35 cases and resolved 31, including 23 from 
that year. Indictments were issued in seven cases and misdemeanour proceedings initiated 
in nine cases, while the remaining cases were dismissed, closed due to lack of grounds, or 
transferred to another jurisdiction. However, little progress was recorded on old cases of 
attacks, with a prominent trial for the wounding of a journalist in 2018 prolonged again. 
In March 2026, the government established an interinstitutional Working Group to implement 
the Mechanism for the Safety of Media Workers. Between March and July 2026, the 
Mechanism received 22 reports of threats to journalists, mostly concerning online insults 
and threats, including one death threat. The Mechanism provided legal and psychological 
support, strengthened direct coordination with the police and prosecution, and established a 
free SOS hotline for reporting attacks and intimidation. Initial results indicate improved 
coordination among relevant institutions, access to support for journalists, and systematic 
monitoring of reported cases and their outcomes.  
Although the authorities widely condemned acts of violence against journalists, instances of 
strong verbal attacks on media representatives by public officials and political parties, 
including senior figures from the security and defence sector, continued. 
Legislative environment 
The national media legislation is largely aligned with the EU acquis  and European 
standards.With regard to free access to information, the regulatory framework was upgraded 
as the Parliament adopted in December 2025 a new Law on Free Access to Information, 
introducing a mechanism for inspections to monitor compliance with proactive publication 
obligations and bringing the legal framework into full alignment with the EU acquis . The 
relevant secondary legislation was also adopted, establishing the legal conditions for its 
effective implementation. Overall, challenges persist in its enforcement. Currently, the 
Agency for Personal Data Protection and Free Access is implementing various training 
programmes to ensure that authorities are adequately prepared to implement the new 
amendments. 
The legal framework is largely aligned with the European Media Freedom Act, and 
Montenegro must ensure that national authorities fulfil their regulatory function upon 
accession to the EU, as well as enable  civil society  to support implementation. Operational 
readiness will require institution-building, awareness-raising and stakeholder engagement. In 
October, Montenegro adopted a Law aligning with the EU’s Digital Services Act.  
Montenegro does not yet have specific legislation in line with the EU Anti -SLAPP legal 
framework, which could leave journalists vulnerable to legal intimidation and pressure.

4.  RESILIENCE, SECURITY, DEFENCE  
Montenegro has a clear strategic orientation anchored in the EU accession process, 
continues to be fully aligned with the EU’s common foreign and security policy, including 
restrictive measures, participates in EU crisis management operations and missions and is a 
reliable EU partner in multilateral fora. 
Montenegro’s advancement in economic security, and democratic resilience serves as 
additional indicators of its readiness for EU membership. These pillars not only define 
Montenegro’s capacity to align with EU standards but also its potential to contribute to the 
Union’s strategic autonomy and collective security.   
Democratic resilience  
The government increasingly recognised the threat posed by hybrid threats, in particular 
foreign information manipulation and interference (FIMI), including disinformation, which 
resulted in efforts to strengthen the institutional framework. Montenegro continues close 
cooperation with the EU on FIMI and strategic communication, leveraging the Hybrid Risk 
Survey to identify and tailor suitable responses to the increasing hybrid threats, including 
FIMI and disinformation in the final stages of EU accession ne gotiations and ahead of the 
June 2027 elections. Working together with the EU through regional and bilateral 
programmes efforts continue to reinforce media literacy, independent journalism, and civil 
society resilience and to counter external hybrid influence, while boosting preparedness to 
safeguard democratic processes.  
Economic security and resilience  
Montenegro’s economy remains vulnerable to external shocks due to its reliance on tourism, 
low diversification of economy and external financing. Tourism accounts directly for around 
20–25% of GDP and is a major destination for foreign investment, with annual FDI inflows 
exceeding 7% of GDP and largely concentrated in tourism, real estate and construction rather 
than productive sectors.  
Economic security faces risks from external dependencies, critical infrastructure gaps, and 
economic coercion, requiring stronger resilience measures.  
Trade dependencies are mainly concentrated in non- strategic consumer and manufactured 
products, many of which are sourced from China. More relevant vulnerabilities concern 
foreign-owned activities in ICT & Digital Technologies and Agrifood, where a relatively small 
number of investors have a significant presence. The energy system remains reliant on 
hydropower and coal, with lignite accounting for around 40% of electricity generation,

=== PAGE 73 ===
72 
 
exposing the economy to climate and energy -market volatility. Significant grid losses (11.3% 
in 2023) add to these challenges, while renewable potential offers opportunities for 
investment, provided grid capacity and system flexibility are strengthened. 
Montenegro holds known critical raw material resources. However, the size, value and 
commercial exploration feasibility for most of these deposits has not been assessed yet.  
Montenegro is making considerable efforts at national level on strengthening cybersecurity  
and has also developed an important role regionally. It hosts the Western Balkans Cyber 
Capacity Centre (WB3C), which provides the expertise and resources necessary to address 
the region’s growing cybersecurity challenges. Montenegro collaborates with the European 
Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) and is in the process of seeking access to the EU 
Cyber Security Reserve. Montenegro has made significant progress aligning its legislation 
with the EU legal framework on Cybersecurity. Montenegro has aligned with the NIS 2 
Directive, established the Cybersecurity Agency and Computer Incident Response Teams, 
and adopted the relevant policy frameworks. Montenegro’s emerging technology sector , 
particularly in AI and cybersecurity, presents an opportunity for deeper EU integration. 
Defence capacity building and defence industrial dimension  
In terms of defence capacity building, with assistance from the European Peace Facility , an 
off-budget instrument, Montenegrin armed forces are working towards strengthening 
capacities. In February 2025, a EUR  6 million assistance measure for Montenegro under the 
European Peace Facility was adopted to provide the Armed Forces with real life support 
equipment, equipment for chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear defence operations 
(CBRN), and equipment for the helicopter- borne search and rescue units. Montenegro also 
benefits from EPF support at regional level, as a non- EU participating nation in the Balkan 
Medical Task Force. 
Montenegro’s defence industrial base is modest in scale, comprising few SMEs, and focuses 
on small arms, ammunition and explosives.  This presents some opportunities for integration 
into European defence supply chains. In the framework of the European Defence Fund (EDF) 
and the European Defence Industry Programme (EDIP), entities established in the EU or in an 
associated country and subject to control by Montenegro or by a Montenegrin entity could be 
eligible for funding, provided that guarantees provided by the EU Member State or associated 
country of establishment are made available to the Commission. So far this has not 
materialised.  
Military mobility is also an area of interest. Montenegro is eligible under the Security Action 
for Europe (SAFE)  instrument and is adapting its defence procurement to align with joint 
procurement mechanisms and stockpile coordination efforts led by the European Defence 
Agency (EDA).  
Civil protection and disaster resilience  
Montenegro increasingly recognises the importance of strengthening civil protection and 
disaster resilience, which resulted in closer alignment with European preparedness and 
response frameworks. Montenegro continues close cooperation with the EU through the 
Union Civil Protection Mechanism (UCPM).

Chapter 17: Economic and monetary policy  
EU rules require the independence of central banks and prohibit them from directly financing 
the public sector. Member States coordinate their economic policies and are subject to fiscal, 
economic and financial surveillance. 
Monetary policy 
Montenegro does not have standard monetary policy tools at its disposal, as it uses the euro 
as legal tender, leaving fiscal policy as the main macroeconomic policy instrument. 
Montenegro’s use of the euro is fully distinct from membership of the euro area. In the 
framework of the accession negotiations, in May 2026, Montenegro accepted the solution 
developed by the EU on how to apply the EU’s framework for the adoption of the euro after 
EU accession.  
Montenegro has aligned its central bank framework with the EU acquis , particularly through 
amendments to the Law on the Central Bank of Montenegro and by adopting, on 24 August 
2026, a constitutional amendment ensuring the objective of price stability in the primary law. 
These changes ensure compliance with central bank independence, as outlined in the TFEU 
and the European System of Central Banks (ESCB)/European Central Bank Statute.

Chapter 11: Agriculture and rural development 
The EU’s common agricultural policy supports farmers and contributes to ensuring Europe’s 
food security. It helps tackle climate change and the sustainable management of national 
resources; maintains rural areas and landscapes across the EU; and keeps the rural economy 
alive by promoting jobs in farming, agri -food industries and associated sectors. This requires 
strong management and control systems. There are also common EU rules for marketing 
standards, quality policy and organic farming.  
On horizontal issues, staffing of the paying agency remains a critical issue. Recruitment of

facilitate progress with the setting up of the paying agency and the establishment of  an 
integrated administration and control system. For the land parcel identification system, new 
ortophoto maps are being delivered. Its update needs to proceed speedily, ensuring 
adequate staff resources are available for the task. Detailed steps for developing the area 
monitoring system must be elaborated and a pilot project launched without delay. There was 
no progress in the development of the farm sustainability data network. Farm advisory 
services remain to be strengthened.

Chapter 13: Fisheries and aquaculture

resources of the sea and limits the environmental impact of fisheries. This includes setting 
catch quotas, managing fleet capacity, rules on control and inspection, rules on markets and

programmes requires appropriate administrative capacity on programme and project level, the 
establishment of systems of sound financial management and control and also the fulfilment 
of other EU acquis elements such as environmental or public procurement legislation.

Chapter 33: Financial and budgetary provisions  
This chapter covers the rules governing the funding of the EU budget (‘own resources’). These 
resources mainly consist of: (i) contributions based on the gross national income of each 
Member State; (ii) customs duties; (iii) the non -recycled plastic resource; and (iv) a resource 
based on value-added tax. Member States must have the appropriate administrative capacity to 
adequately coordinate and ensure the correct calculation, collection, payment and control of 
own resources. 
Montenegro accelerated accession preparation and advanced in underlying policy areas  
affecting the own resources system (for progress in these areas, see chapters 16: Taxation, 
18: Statistics, 27: Environment and climate change; 29: Customs union, and 32: Financial 
control).  
On traditional own resources, in October 2025, the Customs Administration adopted a new 
rulebook on the internal organisation and systematisation of the Customs Administration. It 
established a Department of the Management of Traditional Own Resources and foresaw 
recruitment of three additional officials in this department for 2026. In December 2025, the 
government introduced amendments to the Customs Law, providing the legal basis to 
undertake enforced collection of customs debt, including collection from movable and 
immovable property. The authorities continued preparations to ensure full operationalisation 
of A/B accounts, including upgrading its relevant information system. 
As regards VAT- based resources, the tax administration continued improving its 
administrative capacities, standardising procedures and improving its information system to 
enable accurate data calculation and reporting . The Statistical Office continued preparatory 
work for correct calculation of the average weighted VAT rate.  
In the area of gross national income -based (GNI) resources, Montenegro prepared and 
submitted to the EC its GNI Inventory and a roadmap for outstanding methodological issues 
for statistics in line with the European System of Accounts (ESA) 2010 methodology, while 
the informal economy is calculated in accordance with the OECD and Eurostat manuals. 
On non-recycled plastic packaging waste own resource , Montenegro continued improving 
its regulatory and operational framework in the area of waste management and extended 
producer responsibility, providing for a normative basis for collection, processing, and 
control of data related to non-recyclable plastic packaging waste own resource. 
As regards administrative infrastructure , in March 2026, with government- adopted 
amendments an interinstitutional working group to coordinate work for the provisional 
closure of Chapter 33 was established. To ensure administrative capacity for own resources 
management, procedures for recruitment of 30 additional qualified staff have started, 
including 9 positions in 2026.  As of June 2026, only five positions were filled, thus the 
recruitment process needs to be accelerated.
