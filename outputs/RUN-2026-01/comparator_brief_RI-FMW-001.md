# Comparator brief: Nationality discrimination in access to employment

RI-FMW-001 · North Macedonia · **Status: Draft.** Prepared at stage 12 for sign-off at G7. It uses only judgements validated at G6 (GATE-0007) and, for comparator material, G6b (GATE-0011). Sections marked *Agent synthesis* are written by the tool from that material and add no new judgement. Numbers in square brackets refer to the evidence annex at the end. **Draft policy options at the end are internal drafts** and stay so after sign-off at G7 until a separate decision adapts them for an external audience.

## The issue in North Macedonia (validated at G6)

**Unresolved gap (open).** What remains is equal treatment of EU workers in the law on access to the labour market, including an end to posts in private companies reserved for nationals. The foreigners law is itself the barrier, the 2025 amendment only renames bodies and the further amendments are only proposed with unknown content (all statuses are proposals; the two government items are machine translated, so capped at Medium), while the country's general ban on nationality discrimination is its own account and does not close the gap. The Commission's statement that the law makes no EU distinction conflicts with the country's account on quotas, which is reported and partly explained under RI-FMW-002.

**Priority High, tractability Medium, direction of travel persistent.**

## Reading this brief (agent synthesis)

This brief compares North Macedonia's position with three countries that faced the same Commission finding: Croatia before its accession in 2013, Montenegro, which closed Chapter 2 provisionally in 2026, and Serbia, where the finding has persisted. Each case keeps apart what was reformed, how it was implemented, what resulted and whether the result can be attributed to the reform. None of the three cases shows what happened to EU workers in practice: Croatia's completed alignment is not linked by any source to its Aliens Act, Montenegro's reform is too recent and its closing terms are not public, and Serbia is a stalled case. The cases therefore show what was tried and what to avoid, not a proven route. The lessons and transferability judgements are the tool's synthesis, validated by the analyst at G6b as conditional hypotheses; no source states them as lessons for North Macedonia.

### CMP-0001: Croatia

**Comparator relevance: High.** Same issue and same mechanism (MEC-01): the Commission's 2007 wording on non-discrimination and permits for EU workers matches the requirement on North Macedonia, and Croatia is the only case where legal alignment was recorded as completed. The context differs: the evidence is from 2007 to 2015, Croatia was close to accession, and the EU worker provisions read here sit in one Aliens Act, while whether other laws were also changed is not known. Relevance is about the match only; the absence of outcome evidence is recorded in the case and not reflected in this rating.

**Problem addressed.** In 2007 Croatia's own account in the screening report was that EU migrant workers faced in principle the same work permit conditions as third-country nationals, with limited relief for key staff and permits granted outside the annual quota. The Commission found that Croatia had reached a satisfactory level of alignment in the chapter overall [1], but that important adjustments were needed on access to the labour market, mainly on non-discrimination against EU migrant workers [2], including that EU citizens should need no work permit and should not face nationality discrimination in access to employment. The permit elements also belong to RI-FMW-002 and are relied on here because they were the starting point of the reform.

**Reform or mechanism used.** The Aliens Act in Official Gazette NN 130/2011, passed on 28 October 2011, gives EEA nationals with a right of residence equal rights with Croatian nationals and lets them work without a stay and work permit (Article 153). Article 78(5) relieves EU nationals of the conditions of that article before accession, and Article 236 lets the Government keep earlier rules for nationals of Member States that restrict Croatian workers. Earlier planned amendments to the Act on Foreigners and to the Act on Employment Mediation are recorded in 2007, but their adoption is not stated.

**How it was implemented.** The Act took effect in two steps: it entered into force on 1 January 2012, but Articles 153 to 203 enter into force only on accession, and the Article 78(5) relief ceases on accession. The only applied step after accession in the evidence on the general labour market is a Commission statement in 2015 that Croatia requires a work permit from nationals of restricting Member States (an RI-FMW-002 element). In the public sector, NN 155/2023 provides from 1 January 2024 that EEA and Swiss nationals who know Croatian and the Latin script may be admitted to the civil service [3][4]; an RI-FMW-003 element). No extracted item shows the EU workers chapter being used.

**Evidence of results.** No data on EU workers' take-up or experience in the Croatian labour market generally: the extracted items from all eight Croatian sources were searched, not the full source text, and none gives it. The post-accession material is legal in kind: the 2015 permit statement [5], and a 2023 government bill reporting that the Ombudswoman had pointed to the inconsistency of the Civil Servants Act citizenship condition with EU free movement law and saying that the legislation is being aligned for that reason [6][7]. The Commission's ratings are legal assessments made before accession, not results: Croatia largely fulfilled the requirements on access to the labour market in 2011 [8], before NN 130/2011 was passed, and had completed alignment in 2012 [9]. A 2023 government text treating a citizenship condition as misaligned sits uneasily with the 2012 completed alignment rating; its scope is the public sector (RI-FMW-003), so this is reported for the analyst and not resolved here.

**Limitations of the evidence.** The evidence dates from 2007 to 2015 under logged exception LIM-0027, apart from 2023 civil service texts that belong to RI-FMW-003 and are used only for the post-accession statements above. The Commission never says which law closed the gap. Its 2011 report is dated 12 October 2011, while NN 130/2011 was passed on 28 October 2011, published on 16 November 2011 and is a new Act repealing NN 79/07 and 36/09 [10], so the amendments the report describes as adopted [11], under Chapter 23, with no year or Gazette reference) are an earlier unidentified text. The Act is machine translated and capped at Medium. An unofficial English text marked OG 74/13 exists, but only its free excerpt of Articles 1 to 4 was read [12] and the rest is on sale, so this use is outside LIM-0029 as worded, for the analyst to confirm at G6b. The 2023 bill's explanatory statement is machine translated and capped at Medium, reports the Ombudswoman second-hand, and is not a legal text, so it too is outside LIM-0029 as worded, for the analyst to confirm at G6b. Later amendments to the Aliens Act were not checked. Items in the unmatched queue on Croatian workers moving to other Member States are not used (G5b); one of them, [13], states a treaty end date of 30 June 2020 and is listed for the analyst at G6b. No Croatian item addresses private sector posts reserved for nationals. Filing note: [9] in implementation_items is an EU rating kept as context, not implementation.

**Practical lesson for North Macedonia (agent synthesis).** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: North Macedonia's stated position is a foreigners law that makes no distinction for EU citizens, with a requirement that runs to the time of accession and further amendments of unknown content. If it chose to, North Macedonia could use a general foreigners law to carry a separate regime for EU nationals, adopted before accession, as Croatia did. Whether commencing that regime on accession would be enough is untested: the case does not show that this design earned the Commission's rating or that EU workers then had equal access, and it says nothing on reserved private sector posts.

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** Two elements need separating. Equal treatment and work without a permit for EU nationals are what the Commission states the acquis requires [14][15]; this answers the same mechanism as North Macedonia's gap (MEC-01), and North Macedonia's law is within government control and was amended in 2021 and 2025. Cross-case point: commencement on accession is an untested design choice. Croatia's rating is unattributed [9], Montenegro's deferred clauses cannot yet have been applied [16], and Serbia's reported clause [17] coexisted with Commission findings of misalignment in 2015 and partial alignment in 2024 and 2025 [18][19][20].

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** Croatia's Act was passed with its accession treaty in view and the source ties it to that treaty, while North Macedonia is at an earlier stage, with its screening report dated 2025. The EU worker provisions read here sit in one Aliens Act and whether other laws were also changed is not known, while North Macedonia has a separate law on employment of foreigners. Reciprocity against restricting Member States depended on Croatia's Act of Accession, and no such terms exist for North Macedonia in the evidence. The evidence gives no measure of size or of EU worker numbers with which to compare the two countries.

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: a separate regime for EU nationals inside a general foreigners law, adopted before accession, answers the same mechanism as North Macedonia's gap (MEC-01). Conditional on North Macedonia also amending its separate law on employment of foreigners, and on the EU accepting commencement on accession, which this case does not test because Croatia's rating is unattributed.

### CMP-0002: Montenegro

**Comparator relevance: High.** Same issue and mechanism in a current accession country: new Article 178a gives EU nationals employment on the same conditions as nationals, against the requirement that North Macedonia ensures unrestricted access by accession, though the timing of Article 178a is unknown. The quota carve-out is only a neighbouring RI-FMW-002 match, on which the Commission's request [21] conflicts with North Macedonia's own account [22], and the Commission does not describe Montenegro's restrictions in detail. The context differs, with a closing benchmark whose terms are not public (LIM-0028) and one foreigners law; relevance is about the match only, and High or Medium is for the analyst at G6b.

**Problem addressed.** The Commission recorded no legislative developments on access to the labour market in its 2024 and 2025 reports and recommended finalising legislative alignment. In 2025 it reported early preparations for amendments that would revoke work and other restrictions for EU citizens. These items do not describe the restrictions in detail.

**Reform or mechanism used.** The Law on amendments to the Law on Foreigners, adopted on 31 December 2025, places the rules on EU nationals in a separate regime (Articles 150 to 178b). New Article 178a gives EU nationals the right to employment under the same conditions as Montenegrin nationals, and Article 152 gives a right to reside for work. Article 78(1)(7v) carves EU nationals out, which a law firm note reads as the annual quota exemption (an RI-FMW-002 element; the unamended Article 78 was not read).

**How it was implemented.** Under new Article 221b the quota exemption and the other listed articles apply only from accession, so those clauses cannot have been applied yet. Article 221b does not list Articles 150 to 178b, but the amending law does not say when Article 178a applies, and the base law's commencement clauses were not read. Secondary acts are due within 12 months of entry into force, and none is in the evidence. The Government's annex states that implementation of the law continued in the reporting period [23], which is an uncorroborated self-report naming no clause. The EU release reports that Chapter 2 was provisionally closed, without giving a date or a reason; the date of 15 June 2026 comes only from the Government's annex, which also says this confirmed fulfilment of the closing benchmark. The Government reports 638 residence and work permits to EU citizens from April to August 2026 (an RI-FMW-002 item), and the count does not name the law.

**Evidence of results.** No measured outcome found. The extracted items from all twelve Montenegrin sources were searched for data or findings on EU workers' access or treatment under the new law, and none gives any; the full text of the government annex was not read beyond its Chapter 2 section and keyword hits, apart from page 86, which the G6b quality check found. The only stated effect is the Government's claim that applying the new legal solutions further improved procedures, including the exercise of the rights of EU nationals and their family members [24]. It is one-sided, has no measure and is no proof of success. The clauses deferred to accession cannot yet have results, and for the other clauses the timing is unknown. Provisional closure is a negotiating step and the permit count is a government figure, so neither is an outcome.

**Limitations of the evidence.** What closed the benchmark is known only from public statements (LIM-0028): the EU common position was not used, the EU release does not say why the chapter closed, and the Government's statement does not say what the benchmark required. No extracted item links the law to the closure, and no Commission assessment of the law is in the evidence. The law is machine translated and capped at Medium, the base law was not read, and the law firm note omits the accession clause. The government annex is also machine translated and capped at Medium. It is proposal text and a self-report, and it conflicts with itself on the venue of the conference [25] says Luxembourg; [26] says Brussels). The closing benchmark statement, the 638 count, [23][24] rest on it; it is not a legal text, so this use is outside LIM-0029 as worded, for the analyst to confirm at G6b. The annex cites the Law on Foreigners as also amended by SL 33/2026 [27]; that text was not read and may bear on the EU regime or Article 221b. It also reports a Blue Card bill under access to the labour market (page 139, not extracted), treated as context. The closure is provisional and the EU may return to the chapter; public posts are RI-FMW-003 and are context only. Filing note: in implementation_items, [28][29][30][26] are negotiating steps, and [31] is a use count; all are kept as context, not implementation.

**Practical lesson for North Macedonia (agent synthesis).** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: the Commission says North Macedonia's foreigners law does not distinguish EU citizens from other foreigners [32] and that it needs to ensure unrestricted access by accession [15]. On quotas its sources conflict: the Commission asks for EU citizens to be excluded [21], while the country's own account says quotas do not apply to them following a reciprocity agreement [22], as validated judgement JDG-009552 reports. If North Macedonia chose this route, it could write an equal-treatment clause and a quota carve-out into its foreigners law, as Montenegro did about six months before the chapter closed, with at least the carve-out deferred to accession. The sequence is suggestive only: it does not show that this law met the benchmark, that deferral was accepted, or that anything changed for EU workers.

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** Two elements need separating. Equal treatment in the law is what the Commission asks of North Macedonia [15], and new Article 178a is a clause expressing it, with its timing unknown; a separate regime for EU nationals inside the foreigners law is a drafting option in principle. The quota element is a neighbouring RI-FMW-002 match, contested as set out in the lesson field, and the validated position is that the evidence shows no benchmark under this issue. Cross-case point: commencement on accession is an untested design choice. Croatia's rating is unattributed [9], Montenegro's deferred clauses cannot yet have been applied [16], and Serbia's reported clause [17] coexisted with Commission findings of misalignment in 2015 and partial alignment in 2024 and 2025 [18][19][20].

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** Montenegro's closing benchmark and common position set terms that are not public and may not apply to North Macedonia, which is at an earlier negotiating stage. Montenegro uses one Law on Foreigners, while North Macedonia has a separate law on employment of foreigners. Whether Montenegro's deferral would be accepted elsewhere cannot be judged from the evidence. Only rough measures of size exist, namely 2025 permit quotas of 28 988 in Montenegro [33] and 10 000 in North Macedonia [34].

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: an equal-treatment clause and a quota carve-out in the foreigners law, with named clauses deferred to accession, is a drafting option for North Macedonia. Conditional, because what closed Montenegro's benchmark is not public, the timing of Article 178a is unknown, and whether deferral would be accepted for North Macedonia cannot be judged from the evidence.

### CMP-0003: Serbia

**Comparator relevance: Medium.** The problem matches closely in substance: the Commission says Serbia's EU citizens are covered by the rules for third-country nationals and that North Macedonia's law does not distinguish them from other foreigners, though no Serbian item addresses North Macedonia's reserved private sector posts. No reform aimed at EU workers' access is visible in what was read, while the content of the 2017, 2018 and 2019 amendments is largely unread and a secondary study mentions posting of EU workers, so the case shows a stalled position and no route to closing the gap. Serbia's negotiating context on this issue is not in the evidence.

**Problem addressed.** In 2015 the Commission found that Serbia had reached a satisfactory level of alignment in the chapter overall [35], but that important adjustments were needed on access to the labour market, mainly on non-discrimination against EU migrant workers [36]. It said existing legislation in this area was not yet in line with the acquis, and that by accession EU citizens must not need a work permit. In 2024 and 2025 it found the framework partially aligned because EU citizens are covered by the rules for third-country nationals, which is close in substance to its finding on North Macedonia, though in different words. In 2025 it recommended that Serbia start preparing for alignment.

**Reform or mechanism used.** By Serbia's own account in 2015, the 2014 Law on Employment of Foreigners provides free access for EU citizens and their family members, entering into force on accession. According to one clause of a secondary study, amendments in 2017 and 2018 were made in order to simplify permit procedures. The Commission says the July 2023 amendments simplified the working permit procedure; the 2023 act sets out a single permit and a labour market test, and it has no separate rule for EU citizens. The Labour Law also prohibits discrimination on grounds of nationality, by Serbia's account. No reform aimed at EU workers' access is visible in what was read, while the content of the 2017, 2018 and 2019 amendments is largely unread and the same study says laws on foreigners were revised in all four countries it covers to include the posting of EU workers [37].

**How it was implemented.** The 2023 act applied from 1 February 2024; the Commission said in 2024 that the new system had yet to be fully operational and in 2025 that the procedure had been simplified. The Commission reports work permits issued to EU citizens in both years, 2 071 in 2023 and 526 in 2024, so they remain within the permit regime (RI-FMW-002 items); the counts do not name the law they were issued under. No extracted item says whether the deferred free-access clause still stands, and the consolidated law was blocked.

**Evidence of results.** No outcome evidence found on access or treatment for EU workers. The extracted items from all eight Serbian sources were searched, not the full source text, and the only stated effect is the Commission's statement that the permit procedure was simplified, for foreigners generally and with no processing data, which is treated here as implementation. The permit counts are not outcomes: totals fall from 50 397 to 17 251, the reports do not explain why, and no extracted item links them to a reform.

**Limitations of the evidence.** What stalled is clear from the Commission's wording, but no extracted item says why alignment on EU workers has not moved. The 2015 report relays Serbia's account and describes the 2014 law, and the consolidated text (SRB-GOV-2023-003) is inaccessible, so only the 2023 amending act was read, machine translated and capped at Medium. The content of 113/17 and 50/18 is known from one clause of a secondary study, and the 31/19 amendment listed in the register was not read. The amending act's silence on EU citizens does not show that the base law is silent. No Serbian item addresses private sector posts reserved for nationals, and citizenship conditions for public posts are context only (RI-FMW-003). Filing note: in implementation_items, [19][20][38][39] are EU assessments, and [40][41] are use counts; all are kept as context, not implementation.

**Practical lesson for North Macedonia (agent synthesis).** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: Serbia is a stalled case with a closely similar Commission finding in different words, covering the permit regime but not reserved private sector posts. It suggests that if North Macedonia amended its foreigners law only to simplify permits for all foreigners, this could earn a chapter-level finding of limited progress while the gap the Commission names remains: permit simplification was one of two stated grounds in each year, with EURES preparations in 2024 and electronic data exchange in 2025. Serbia also reported a general ban on nationality discrimination in the same report that found misalignment, as North Macedonia does now in its own account. It cannot show why the gap persisted or whether a clause deferred to accession is enough.

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** The negative point is transferable, conditionally: simplifying permit procedures does not by itself address the difference in treatment that the Commission names, because EU citizens stay inside the foreigner regime. This holds only as far as the Commission's wording supports it, since no extracted item states it as a lesson. Cross-case point: commencement on accession is an untested design choice. Croatia's rating is unattributed [9], Montenegro's deferred clauses cannot yet have been applied [16], and Serbia's reported clause [17] coexisted with Commission findings of misalignment in 2015 and partial alignment in 2024 and 2025 [18][19][20].

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** Serbia's negotiating position and the reasons for its slow pace are not in the evidence on this issue, so they cannot be compared with North Macedonia's. The permit reforms read here concern foreigners generally, so they say little about how to draft rules for EU workers. Serbia's 2015 screening is a decade older than North Macedonia's 2025 screening. Only rough measures of size exist, namely EU shares of permits in different years: 3% in Serbia in 2024 [41] and, by North Macedonia's own account, 12.8% in 2020 and 10.7% in 2021 [42].

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: the transferable point is a warning, that simplifying permits for all foreigners does not by itself remove the difference in treatment the Commission names. Conditional, because it rests on the Commission's wording across Serbian reports rather than on a stated lesson, and Serbia's reasons for its slow pace are not in the evidence.

## Lessons across the cases (agent synthesis)

Three points recur across the cases. First, two countries wrote rules for EU nationals as a separate regime inside a general foreigners law, giving equal treatment with nationals and work without a permit: Croatia in its 2011 Aliens Act and Montenegro in its December 2025 amendments [14][16]. This answers the same legal mechanism the Commission names for North Macedonia, whose law treats EU citizens the same as other foreigners [32]. Second, both countries deferred some or all of these provisions to accession, and no case shows that deferral is enough: Croatia's rating is not attributed to the Act [9], Montenegro's deferred clauses cannot yet have been applied, and Serbia reported a clause of this kind in 2015 while the Commission still found misalignment in 2015 and partial alignment in 2024 and 2025 [17][18][20]. Third, Serbia suggests that simplifying permits for all foreigners can earn a finding of limited progress while the difference in treatment the Commission names remains. No case addresses posts in private companies reserved for nationals, which is part of North Macedonia's gap [43].

## Draft policy options (agent synthesis, internal draft)

These options are internal drafts for discussion. Each rests on a validated unresolved gap (open) and on comparator cases validated at G6b, and each transferability judgement is agent synthesis, rated conditional. None is proposed because another country adopted it, and none is shown by the evidence to work.

**Option 1: Write equal treatment for EU nationals into the law, as a distinct regime.**
- *Gap addressed:* the law on access to the labour market does not give EU workers equal treatment with nationals [32][15].
- *Mechanism targeted:* legal or regulatory gap (MEC-01).
- *Comparator evidence:* Croatia (relevance High) and Montenegro (relevance High) each placed EU nationals in a separate regime within their foreigners law, with equal treatment and permit-free work [14][16]. Transferability conditional: the design answers the same mechanism, but neither case shows that it earned the Commission's assessment or changed access for EU workers.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* North Macedonia has a separate law on employment of foreigners, so the regime would need to cover that law as well as any residence rules, not one act only; the provisions to be changed would first need to be listed.
- *Main risks:* drafting a clause that the Commission does not accept as full alignment, as in Serbia; leaving the reserved private sector posts untouched, since no case covers them.
- *Who would need to act:* the Government, through the ministry responsible for the law on employment of foreigners, now the Ministry of Economy and Labour [44], machine translated), with adoption by the Assembly.

**Option 2: Settle the commencement design early, rather than assume that deferral to accession is enough.**
- *Gap addressed:* the requirement that EU citizens can access the labour market without restrictions by accession [15].
- *Mechanism targeted:* legal or regulatory gap (MEC-01).
- *Comparator evidence:* all three cases. Croatia and Montenegro deferred provisions to accession, and Serbia reported a deferred clause while misalignment findings continued [17][19]. Transferability conditional: this is a warning drawn from the tool's reading of three cases, not a tested lesson, and what closed Montenegro's benchmark is not public.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* a clear view, through the negotiation process, of which provisions must apply before accession and which may start on accession.
- *Main risks:* a law that is aligned on paper but leaves EU workers inside the foreigner regime until accession, which may not satisfy a closing benchmark.
- *Who would need to act:* the Government and its negotiating structures, with the ministry responsible for labour.

**Option 3: Do not treat permit simplification or the general non-discrimination rule as the answer.**
- *Gap addressed:* the Commission's finding that EU citizens are treated as any other foreigner [32]. North Macedonia points to the reference to nationality discrimination in its Law on Labour Relations [45], but the validated gap judgement finds that this, being the country's own account, does not close the gap.
- *Mechanism targeted:* legal or regulatory gap (MEC-01).
- *Comparator evidence:* Serbia (relevance Medium) simplified permits for all foreigners and reported a general non-discrimination clause, while the Commission still found that EU citizens were covered by the rules for third-country nationals [20][41]. Transferability conditional: the point rests on the Commission's wording, not on a stated lesson, and Serbia's reasons for its slow pace are not in the evidence.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* any permit reform would be paired with an explicit provision on EU nationals, as in Option 1.
- *Main risks:* reporting progress on procedures while the gap the Commission names remains.
- *Who would need to act:* the ministry responsible for labour, and the Employment Service Agency where permit procedures change.

**Related step outside the comparator evidence.** Removing posts in private companies reserved for nationals is part of the gap but is not covered by any case, so no option is drafted on it here. The laws that reserve these posts are not identified in the evidence, and identifying them is an open question for the next run.
