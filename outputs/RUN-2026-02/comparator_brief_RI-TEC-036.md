# Comparator brief: National e-services portal limited in range and use

RI-TEC-036 · Moldova · **Status: Validated at G7 (GATE-0029), internal output.** It uses only judgements validated at G6 (GATE-0024) and, for comparator material, G6b (GATE-0028). Sections marked *Agent synthesis* are written by the tool from that material and add no new judgement. Numbers in square brackets refer to the evidence annex at the end. **Draft policy options at the end are internal drafts** and stay so after sign-off at G7 until a separate decision adapts them for an external audience.

## The issue in Moldova (validated at G6)

**Unresolved gap (partly_addressed).** The Reform Agenda target of 75% of business services digitalised has been met, and EVO, the public services portal and 118 Unified Public Service Centres are in use; but in 2026 only 61% of citizen services are digitalised, only 133 of 414 business services run fully end to end, the Reform Agenda notes that the portal lacks integration with EU systems, and the link to the Single Digital Gateway is still planned. Demand remains low in rural areas, which is why the Commission says the centres were set up. What remains is a fuller range of citizen services delivered end to end online, integration with EU systems, and use by people with weaker digital access or skills.

**Priority High, tractability High, direction of travel improving.**

## Reading this brief (agent synthesis)

This brief compares Moldova's national e-services portal with three countries: North Macedonia, whose portal usage rate doubled in the year its most-requested citizen services went online; Albania, which placed most services on one portal under a law obliging institutions to use it and reports high use; and Montenegro, which launched a new portal in December 2024 and is adding services with Reform Agenda support. North Macedonia was rated of High relevance, and Albania and Montenegro of Medium relevance. Moldova's position differs from all three: its EVO portal brings together 636 services, 447 of them online [1], and the Commission reports that uptake rose significantly in 2026, including in rural areas, where demand had been low [2][3]. What remains is a fuller range of citizen services delivered end to end, integration with EU systems, and use by people with weaker digital access or skills. No case shows what caused a rise in use: North Macedonia's Commission links its rise to new services only by saying that one accompanied the other [4], Albania's sources do not show that its law produced its range or use, and Montenegro's results are too early. None of the cases says anything about assisted access of the kind Moldova already provides through 118 service centres [5], so this brief offers no option on it. The lessons and transferability judgements are the tool's synthesis, validated by the analyst at G6b as conditional hypotheses.

### CMP-0015: North Macedonia

**Comparator relevance: High.** North Macedonia matches Moldova's issue and its central mechanism, low uptake of a working national portal: both portals are improving from persistent low demand, and in both a Reform Agenda condition ties e-services to EU payments. It is the only one of the three cases in which an independent source reports a quantified rise in use from a low level, a usage rate that doubled as the most-demanded services went online, which parallels the significant rise in uptake the Commission reports for Moldova in 2026, while Albania's use grows from an already high level. The context differs: North Macedonia's portal offers 338 services including partly available ones against 636 on EVO, a search of all 3,686 Moldovan evidence passages in the project found no usage figure defined as North Macedonia's are, so levels of use cannot be compared, and its constraint is a short-staffed ministry rather than cost.

**Problem addressed.** North Macedonia's portal was little used: the State Audit Office found 0.556 requests per registered user and that visually impaired persons could not use it [6][7], machine translated), the Commission recorded 292,179 users but only 2,070 applications a month in 2024 [8], and 78 institutions still offered 1,120 services only in person [9], machine translated). The Commission said in 2024 that the most commonly requested services were not yet online [10] and in April 2026 still called digital government quite underdeveloped, with most e-services rarely used and only a fraction fully transactional [11]. The audit attributes low use to citizens' weak interest, insufficient promotion and equal prices for electronic and paper documents [12], machine translated).

**Reform or mechanism used.** The portal (uslugi.gov.mk) rests on the 2019 Law on Electronic Management and Electronic Services (Law on Electronic Management and Electronic Services) and was upgraded through an EU-funded project to add services, first for businesses and then those most demanded by citizens (Upgrade of the National e-Services Portal (EU-funded project); [13]). The Reform Agenda sets a payment step to increase the offer of e-services by 50% by June 2027 (Reform 2.2.2.3 Widen the offer of public e-services; [14]). The long-term ICT strategy SMART/MK plans mandatory integration or connection of each e-service on the portal and a change in the price of e-services (Mandatory integration of e-services on the portal (A 3.2.1.3); Revision of the price of e-services (A 3.2.2.8); [15][16], machine translated), and an analysis for DigInfo centres to support users (DigInfo centres; [17], machine translated). Single Sign-On was integrated across service providers by 2026 (Single Sign-On System; [18]).

**How it was implemented.** Offered: the portal has been in use since December 2019 [19], machine translated). Counts differ by source and definition: 392 or 264 at the 2024 baseline, 124 accessible online by September 2024, 314 in the 2025 strategy, and 338 at the end of August 2026 including partly available ones, given as 336 in another chapter of the same report [20][21][22][23][24][25]. Upgrade: in 2024 the Commission reported only a few new services and the long-awaited 135 not yet configured [10][26]; in 2025 services for businesses, an easier user interface and new services mostly demanded by citizens, including birth, marriage and citizenship certificates [13][27]; and in 2026 new services for citizens and businesses [28]. The Reform Agenda step, mandatory integration, the price change and the DigInfo centres remain plans, and progress against the 50% target cannot be fixed because the baselines conflict. Capacity: the audit found 101 of 242 posts for the platform unfilled [29], machine translated), and the Commission described ownership as fragmented, with limited resources for the lead ministry [30].

**Evidence of results.** Used: the Commission states that the growth in services 'was accompanied by a growing usage rate, which doubled from the previous year' [4], and that 2025 and early 2026 saw significant improvements in digitalisation while service delivery still requires efforts to meet EU standards [31]. The starting level was low: about 13 of 100 people aged 18 to 70 were registered on the portal according to the 2025 strategy [32], machine translated). A search of all 4,629 passages in the North Macedonian evidence files for usage, user, application, request and end-to-end wording found no count of applications or users after 2024 other than that registration share, which states no reference date, and no share of portal services completed online end to end; the Commission says only that a fraction are fully transactional [11]. Who is served: the Commission says the portal caters for people with special needs [33][34], which conflicts with the audit's finding that persons with disabilities cannot use e-services [35], machine translated), and in 2026 it finds that digital divides leave rural and older women behind and asks for compliance with accessibility standards [36][37].

**Limitations of the evidence.** The usage rate has no stated definition, base, figure or origin, so the doubling cannot be placed on a scale; the April 2026 statement that most e-services are rarely used describes the level, and the November statement describes the change [11][4]. The Commission states the link between new services and use only as co-occurrence, and Single Sign-On integration and an easier interface came in the same period [18][13]. Service counts are not comparable across sources, and the 338 includes partly available services. The audit and strategy figures are machine translated and capped at Medium confidence. The North Macedonian evidence was gathered for the earlier country study and its 2026 update; North Macedonia's own assessments are context, not evidence about Moldova. The finding that the upgrade was implemented was made before the 2026 report and has not yet been revisited in its light. Claims of absence in this case rest on searches of all North Macedonian evidence passages in the project, not of the full source texts.

**Practical lesson for Moldova (agent synthesis).** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: in North Macedonia, use of the portal rose in the year the services citizens most often request went online, after years in which a growing count of rarely requested services left use low. Moldova's remaining gap sits in a similar place: 61% of citizen services are digitalised against 82% for businesses [38], and rural demand, which the Commission found low in 2025, rose significantly in 2026, including in rural areas where 118 service centres were set up to respond to persistently low demand [3][2]. The case suggests ranking further digitalisation by how often citizens request a service, and testing the audit's stated causes of low use, promotion and the price of electronic against paper documents; Moldova's own strategy already proposes lower fees for digital services [39].

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** As a conditional hypothesis only: putting the most-requested citizen services online end to end and checking use after each addition, promoting the portal, and giving electronic documents a price advantage over paper ones. Moldova already reports the share of services digitalised separately for citizens and businesses, so use could be followed as citizen services are added [38]. The account of causes rests on one audit, machine translated, and the rise in use on one unexplained Commission sentence.

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** North Macedonia's improvement starts from a narrower range than Moldova's: 338 services including partly available ones, against 636 on EVO of which 447 are online [1], so the gain from each added service may be smaller in Moldova. Levels of use cannot be compared: a search of all 3,686 Moldovan evidence passages in the project for usage wording found only figures defined differently from North Macedonia's, such as the share of public service users who accessed services electronically [40] and active users of the EVO application [41]. North Macedonia's main constraint is a short-staffed lead ministry, whereas Moldova's government names a cost of digitalisation above its own resources [42]. Moldova has already set up 118 centres for rural users [5], where North Macedonia offers only a planned analysis of DigInfo centres, so the case says nothing about assisted access. The Reform Agenda conditions differ: North Macedonia's counts e-services offered, while Moldova's open condition counts services integrated in the EVO Sign wallet [43].

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: North Macedonia's portal use doubled in the year its most-requested citizen services went online, and its audit ties earlier low use to weak promotion and equal prices for electronic and paper documents, so Moldova could test both against its low citizen demand, where 61% of citizen services are digitalised and its own strategy already proposes lower fees for digital services. Conditional, because the Commission links the rise in use to new services only by saying one accompanied the other, the rate has no stated definition and rose alongside Single Sign-On integration, and Moldova's demand has been lowest among rural and older users, whom added services may not reach, although the Commission reports uptake rising in 2026, including in rural areas.

### CMP-0008: Albania

**Comparator relevance: Medium.** Albania matches Moldova on consolidating services onto one portal, which Moldova's Government Decision 677/2025 pursues by strengthening access to public e-services through EVO (machine translated), and on the remaining inclusion gap: in 2026 the Commission asks Albania to address an emerging digital divide and strengthen citizens' ability to use e-services independently, while in Moldova demand was low in 2025, especially in rural areas, before uptake rose significantly in 2026, including in rural areas. It differs on the core symptom, because Albania's reported use is high and 95% of applications for 1,257 services are offered online, and the case cannot show what produced that use.

**Problem addressed.** Albania's gap is reported mainly as depth and inclusion: the Commission records 95% of applications for 1,237 services offered online in 2024 and 1,253 in 2025, yet accessibility for vulnerable groups remains unaddressed. The sources conflict on range, because the RCC also says Albania 'continues to lag behind the regional average in certain aspects of service scope and sophistication', with a citizen services score of 50 against 53 from desk research in 2025. It also asks Albania to improve access to the use of e-services and to address an emerging digital divide. The national monitor finds digitalisation largely complete but citizens not at the centre of design and delivery.

**Reform or mechanism used.** Law No. 43/2023 on Electronic Governance, adopted in June 2023, requires public institutions to use e-Albania as the primary point of access and requires e-payment through the portal; as stated in the sources, the duty falls on institutions, not users. The Digital Agenda 2022-2026 sets a 95% target. ADISA was absorbed by the National Agency for Information Society, which concentrates policy and operational control.

**How it was implemented.** Offered: the monitor records more than 90% of services placed on the portal since its previous cycle and most physical counters closed, while municipal offices still handle services that need in-person steps. Fully transactional: no extracted item gives a national count, and of seven sampled services the monitor calls two end-to-end, while its own footnote says the payment option usually does not work, and others, such as vehicle registration and VAT, need in-person payment. No service is yet delivered as a life event. Vehicle registration and VAT are described as offered outside the portal in one passage and as sampled services needing in-person payment in another.

**Evidence of results.** Used: the Regional Cooperation Council (RCC) reports e-government users at 78.5% of internet users in 2023 and 88.6% in 2024, with 3.43 million registered users and 15.2 million services provided in 2024 and 17 million in 2025. The monitor's perception survey has 66.9% agreeing that they can access services easily online and 30.5% disagreeing. No extracted item gives the share of those services that were completed online from start to finish.

**Limitations of the evidence.** The RCC gives no origin for the portal figures and does not define a registered user or a service provided; its text says 89% of the population where its table says 88.6% of internet users. The e-government user indicator counts any online contact with authorities, not portal use [44]. The extracted items hold no series from before the June 2023 law, so use before and after it cannot be compared. The monitor's informant scores are points awarded only if all informants agree [45]; 0 of 3 on accessible service delivery, 3 of 3 on ease of use), so they are not counts of people. The monitor states that 'these reforms explain why' informants describe transactional reliability and ease of access [46], but this rests on three informants, concerns their views and not use, and conflicts with its footnote that payment 'usually does not work' [47]. The monitor also reports the law's once-only duty both ways, as effectively implemented [48] and as partial [49][50][51][52]; these items sit in the unmatched queue, stay unused under G5b and are listed for the analyst. Claims of absence in this case refer to the extracted items, not the full source text.

**Practical lesson for Moldova (agent synthesis).** This case was built for North Macedonia; its lesson for Moldova is stated in the transferability judgement below.

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** The monitor reports that absorbing ADISA into the agency removed one named overlap, on monitoring, evaluation and reporting between ADISA and the Agency for Dialogue and Co-governance [53][54]. This is a narrow result with no measure, and North Macedonia already has a lead ministry that lacks resources and cooperation [30], so naming one body would not by itself address its problem. A legal duty on institutions to integrate services is an untested option: no extracted item shows that it caused Albania's range, and Albania's experience shows that working payment and signature cannot be assumed.

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** Closing counters cannot be assumed to transfer: the source gives no count, date or effect on users, and North Macedonia has 1,120 services available only in person and an audit finding that persons with disabilities cannot use e-services [9][35], both machine translated). Albania's own unaddressed accessibility issues show that its route has not solved this. Relative size was not assessed because no extracted item gives it. In these items the Commission's ask of Albania is a recommendation, while North Macedonia's increase in e-services is also a Reform Agenda condition [55].

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: Albania shows that bringing services onto one portal can coexist with near-complete range and high reported use, yet in 2026 the Commission still asks it to close a digital divide, resolve accessibility issues and plan and budget for maintaining the platforms. For Moldova, which has decided to strengthen access to public e-services through EVO, this suggests that consolidation alone should not be expected to raise use among rural and older users and that maintenance needs its own budget, given that the government says digitalisation costs exceed its resources. Conditional, because a search of all 137 Albanian evidence passages in the project found sources describing what the 2023 Law on Electronic Governance requires but none stating that it produced Albania's range of services or its use, and working payment cannot be assumed.

### CMP-0009: Montenegro

**Comparator relevance: Medium.** Montenegro matches Moldova on low use of e-government and on expanding the portal with Reform Agenda support, which parallels Moldova's condition to integrate at least 20 e-services in the EVO Sign wallet by December 2026. It differs in stage: its portal launched in December 2024 and offers around 100 e-services whose operability the Commission says still needs to be improved, against 636 services on EVO. A search of all 204 Montenegrin evidence passages in the project found no measure of use of the portal itself, only the Regional Cooperation Council's share of internet users using any e-government service, 37.5% in 2024, before almost all of the portal's services, so its results remain too early to show what raises use.

**Problem addressed.** Montenegro's government names low use of e-services and weak interoperability as key challenges, and the Commission repeatedly asks it to prioritise a platform for transactional services. The national monitor reports that the government's own Digital Services Plan 2025-2027 puts no service at the fifth level (full online service). E-government users were 37.5% of internet users in 2024, below the Western Balkans average of 54%.

**Reform or mechanism used.** Sources date the new eUprava portal differently in kind: the government adopted an information note on it in December 2024, when the monitor says it 'was not functional'; the Commission and the event report say it launched that month; and first services were published in April 2025. It runs under a plan for transactional services 2025-2027 covering 171 services and 21 institutions, revised on 18 December 2025; the RCC says the same plan sets out 162 additional services and the revised plan's table sums to 175, and these figures are not reconciled. The Law on Electronic Government gives the legal basis, and the government launched an initiative for all institutions to publish their services on the portal. A proposed Open Government action plan targets 150 transactional services by 2029.

**How it was implemented.** Offered: the government reports 106 services from 14 institutions on the portal, without saying how many are transactional, and eFirma and eTaxis run outside it. Fully transactional: the government's action plan states 45 transactional services [56], while its Digital Services Plan 2025-2027, as cited by the monitor, puts no service at level 5 [57]. These are two government statements not shown to be at the same date (the action plan is dated 12 October 2025; the monitor, dated September 2025, does not date the plan's state of play), not reconciled, and no extracted item holds an independent count of transactional services. Delivery against the plan was 25 of 57 by July 2025, then 57 of 57 on the government's own report, with no independent check in the extracted items. The ministry reports two officials in the responsible directorate, a refusal of budget reserve for Interior services and Employment Agency services waiting on a new law. The ministry's report on the Digital Transformation Strategy gives 43.14% of planned 2024 activities fulfilled, for the whole strategy and not only e-services (government reporting, machine translated; repeated by the monitor, [58]).

**Evidence of results.** Used: e-government users fell from 48.4% of internet users in 2023 to 37.5% in 2024, on an indicator of any online contact with authorities [44], and an oral UNDP figure puts actual use at 8%. No extracted item gives use of eUprava itself. The monitor's service delivery grades reflect findings to the end of 2024, when the portal was under construction [59][60]. Its statements that information improved and that none of the seven sampled services was on the portal are notes made at the time of writing, after the first services of April 2025. The government states that satisfaction is around 50%.

**Limitations of the evidence.** The portal is young. The monitor, a non-government body co-funded by the monitored ministry (SQ-00180), sets its findings to the end of 2024, when the portal was under construction, with later notes to July 2025 [59][60], so its grades largely predate the first services, and the government's report of full delivery and the 106 count are untested. The RCC text says use continues to grow, against the fall in its own table. The 8% figure is oral, second-hand and has no reference period, and the event report is rated Low. The 2024 user figures predate almost all of the portal's life, so they measure the starting position. The 106 services, 25 and 57 of 57, the 43.14%, the two officials, the budget refusal and 'key challenges' come from machine-translated Montenegrin government reports, capped at Medium; they are not legal texts, so they are outside LIM-0029 as worded, for the analyst to confirm at G6b, and item [61] is also flagged possible_misread. Claims of absence in this case refer to the extracted items, not the full source text.

**Practical lesson for Moldova (agent synthesis).** This case was built for North Macedonia; its lesson for Moldova is stated in the transferability judgement below.

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** As a conditional hypothesis only: a portal may add little where the services people need sit elsewhere, and North Macedonia's Commission findings say the most commonly requested services are not yet online [10]. It rests on one monitor and seven sampled services, whose grades largely predate the first services, that monitor also reports a gain in information, and no extracted item measures use of eUprava. The stated constraints of too few staff and refused budget have a parallel in North Macedonia's unfilled posts and limited resources for the ministry [29][30].

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** The Montenegrin portal launched in December 2024 on the Commission's account, while North Macedonia's has run since 2019 [62] and has a longer record of low use, so results at Montenegro's stage may not predict results at a later one. The cybersecurity crisis that the Commission dates to 2023 and says delayed services and eroded trust (the government's action plan refers instead to 'the 2022 attacks', [63], classed out_of_scope at pass 2) has no stated equivalent in North Macedonia's evidence here. Montenegro's different accession stage was not used as a reason, because no extracted item links it to the portal.

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: Montenegro shows that services delivered with Reform Agenda support can be counted, 57 in 2025 on the Commission's account, while their operability still needs to be improved, so meeting a count such as Moldova's at least 20 e-services in the EVO Sign wallet would not by itself show that the services work end to end or are used; Moldova's own business services show the same gap, with 310 of 414 counted as digitalised but only 133 fully digital end to end. Conditional and too early, because the Commission's statement that delivery improved gives no measure, a search of all 204 Montenegrin evidence passages in the project found no measure of use of the portal itself, and Moldova's portal already offers several times as many services.

## Lessons across the cases (agent synthesis)

Three points recur. First, which services go online may matter more than how many. In North Macedonia use stayed low while a growing count of rarely requested services went online, and rose in the year that birth, marriage and citizenship certificates were added [24][4]. Moldova's remaining gap sits among citizen services, of which 61% were digitalised by June 2026 against 82% of business services [38]. Second, a count of services delivered does not show that they work end to end or are used. Montenegro developed 57 e-services in 2025 with Reform Agenda support, yet the Commission says their operability still needs to be improved [64][65][66]. Moldova's own figures show the same gap: of 414 business services, 310 are counted as digitalised but 133 run fully end to end [67]. Third, consolidation on one portal does not by itself close a digital divide or pay for upkeep. Albania offers 95% of applications for 1,257 services online, yet in 2026 the Commission asks it to address an emerging digital divide, resolve accessibility problems and plan and budget for maintaining its platforms [68][69][70][71]. Moldova has decided, in a government decision known from machine translation, to strengthen access to e-services through EVO [72], and its Government says the cost of digitalisation exceeds its own resources [42].

## Draft policy options (agent synthesis, internal draft)

These options are internal drafts for discussion. Each rests on a validated unresolved gap (partly addressed) and on comparator cases validated at G6b; each transferability judgement is agent synthesis, rated conditional. None is proposed because another country adopted it, and no case shows that any of these steps caused a rise in use.

**Option 1: Rank the remaining citizen services by how often people request them, put them online end to end in that order, and follow use after each addition.**
- *Gap addressed:* the fuller range of citizen services delivered end to end, with 61% of citizen services digitalised [38], and low demand among some users [3].
- *Mechanism targeted:* low uptake of systems already in place (MEC-11).
- *Comparator evidence:* North Macedonia (relevance High), where the usage rate doubled as the most-demanded certificates went online [4][27]. Transferability conditional: the rate has no stated definition, it rose alongside Single Sign-On integration [18], and North Macedonia's range is narrower than Moldova's, so each added service may matter less in Moldova.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* data on how often each service is requested, on every channel, and a measure of use per service. Moldova's current measures, such as the share of public service users who accessed services electronically and active users of the EVO application, are defined differently and would need to be followed over time; both figures come from machine-translated government documents [40][41].
- *Main risks:* the most-requested services may depend on registers and data exchange that are not ready; added services may not reach older and rural users, since people aged 18 to 29 use e-services three times as often as those aged 60 to 74, according to a machine-translated government programme [73].
- *Who would need to act:* the e-Governance Agency, which runs the shared platforms, with the institutions that own the services.

**Option 2: Test whether lower fees for digital services and promotion of the portal raise use.**
- *Gap addressed:* use of the portal by people who still apply in person, and the persistent low demand the Commission records [2].
- *Mechanism targeted:* low uptake (MEC-11).
- *Comparator evidence:* North Macedonia's State Audit Office tied earlier low use to weak promotion and equal prices for electronic and paper documents, in a finding known from machine translation [12]. Moldova's own strategy already proposes lower fees or shorter delivery times for digital services [39]. Transferability conditional: the account of causes rests on one machine-translated audit, and no case shows the effect of a price change.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* a test designed so that its effect on use can be measured, for example on a few high-volume services.
- *Main risks:* lost fee income for a Government that says digitalisation already costs more than its resources [42]; lower fees that do not reach people without digital access or skills.
- *Who would need to act:* the Government, with the institutions that set fees for their services.

**Option 3: Report Reform Agenda progress on services that work end to end and are used, not only on services counted.**
- *Gap addressed:* the open condition to integrate at least 20 e-services in the EVO Sign wallet by December 2026 [43][74], and the gap between services counted as digitalised and those delivered end to end [67].
- *Mechanism targeted:* low uptake (MEC-11) and weak administrative capacity (MEC-03).
- *Comparator evidence:* Montenegro (relevance Medium), where services were delivered under the Reform Agenda while their operability still needs to be improved [64][66]. Transferability conditional and too early: no source measures use of Montenegro's portal itself, and Moldova's portal already offers several times as many services.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* an agreed definition of a service delivered end to end, which Moldova already applies to business services [67].
- *Main risks:* a condition met on paper by integrating services that are little used.
- *Who would need to act:* the e-Governance Agency, reporting with the Government on the Reform Agenda.

**A warning rather than an option: consolidating services on EVO will not by itself raise use among rural and older users, and the platforms need their own maintenance budget.**
Albania (relevance Medium) combines near-complete range on one portal with high reported use, yet the Commission still asks it to close a digital divide and to plan and budget for maintaining its platforms [69][71], and no source shows that its 2023 law produced its range or use. Transferability conditional: for Moldova, which has decided to strengthen access through EVO [72], the comparator evidence supports budgeting for maintenance alongside consolidation, not expecting consolidation to drive use on its own.
