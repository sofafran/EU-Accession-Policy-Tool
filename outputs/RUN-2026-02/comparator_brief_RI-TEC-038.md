# Comparator brief: Electronic public procurement system outdated and in need of replacement

RI-TEC-038 · Moldova · **Status: Validated at G7 (GATE-0029), internal output.** It uses only judgements validated at G6 (GATE-0024) and, for comparator material, G6b (GATE-0028). Sections marked *Agent synthesis* are written by the tool from that material and add no new judgement. Numbers in square brackets refer to the evidence annex at the end. **Draft policy options at the end are internal drafts** and stay so after sign-off at G7 until a separate decision adapts them for an external audience.

## The issue in Moldova (validated at G6)

**Unresolved gap (open).** The new system is still being procured: technical specifications were received in September 2025 and the call for bids was published in December 2025 with a deadline in February 2026, while the Reform Agenda sets a pilot of the new system by December 2027. MTender remains in use without planning, contract management and some procedures. What remains is designing, building and putting into operation a new e-procurement system that covers every procedure and stage of the procurement cycle.

**Priority High, tractability Medium, direction of travel persistent.**

## Reading this brief (agent synthesis)

This brief compares Moldova's outdated electronic procurement system, MTender, which it is replacing with a new system, with two countries: Montenegro, where an independent assessment found within about two years that a system introduced in 2021 rested on old technology that limited upgrading, and North Macedonia, whose integrated system the Commission has described as functioning efficiently in every report from 2021 to 2026. Montenegro was rated of Medium relevance and North Macedonia of Low relevance. Neither case shows an outdated system being replaced: Montenegro upgraded a recent system and no source shows whether the technology limit was removed [1][2], and North Macedonia upgraded a working one. The cases therefore offer nothing on how to replace a system, which is what Moldova is doing [3]. What they do offer is a caution about building and a method for checking before building, and that is why this brief gives a warning first and options about diagnosis rather than about the replacement itself. The lessons and transferability judgements are the tool's synthesis, validated by the analyst at G6b as conditional hypotheses.

### CMP-0013: Montenegro

**Comparator relevance: Medium.** Montenegro shows the core of Moldova's problem in another form: in 2023 an independent assessment found that a system under implementation since January 2021 needed substantial upgrading and that old technology limited further upgrading, as the Commission finds MTender outdated, and both countries rely on external funds. But Montenegro upgraded the system rather than replacing it, and a search of all 204 Montenegrin evidence passages in the project for procurement terms found none showing the technology limit removed, gaps in procedures or stages set out in law, or any effect on centralised procurement. The case therefore shows a recently introduced system judged technically limited and then improved, not an outdated system being replaced.

**Problem addressed.** The interim findings of the World Bank's MAPS assessment state that the current system is a good start but needs substantial upgrading [4] and that the assessment found system limitations for further upgrading, namely old technology [1]. This is close to the core of Moldova's issue, an outdated system, but it concerns a system under implementation only since January 2021 [5]. A search of all 204 Montenegrin evidence passages in the project for procurement terms found none that says the system fails to support procedures or stages set out in law, or that it hampers centralised procurement, and none that describes the system used before 2021 or says that the 2021 system replaced it.

**Reform or mechanism used.** Two linked responses. First, a diagnostic: the Public Procurement Policy Unit asked the World Bank to assess its public procurement system with specific emphasis on electronic procurement [6], using the MAPS methodology with an e-procurement supplementary module (MAPS assessment with e-Procurement supplementary module (World Bank)). Second, an upgrade of the existing system rather than a replacement: the EU agreed to provide further funds to implement the MAPS e-procurement recommendations and assigned funds for the upgrade of the system [2], and the Commission says the MAPS results will serve as a basis for enhancing the system [7].

**How it was implemented.** The assessment started in January 2023 [8], included a dedicated three-day assessment of the e-procurement system [9], was planned for delivery by January 2024 [10], and, according to the Commission, ended in December 2023 with a comprehensive evaluation focused on electronic procurement [11]. The Commission records some progress in 2023 in improving the functioning of the system [12], continuous improvements in 2023 to 2024 that facilitate data exchange between institutions and aim at detecting irregularities [13], and further improvements in 2024 and 2025 to reinforce monitoring and prevent corruption [14]. None of the sources in that search says whether these improvements are the EU-funded upgrade.

**Evidence of results.** The only stated effect is the Commission's sentence that improved functionalities of the system facilitate the monitoring of contract award, implementation and appeals [15]. It gives no measure, and it may describe what the system does rather than an outcome. No measured outcome found: a search of all 204 Montenegrin evidence passages in the project for procurement terms found no figure on use, coverage of procedures, processing or monitoring, and no later independent assessment of whether the old-technology limit was overcome.

**Limitations of the evidence.** The World Bank findings are interim slides by one staff member, stated without the underlying data, and the final MAPS e-procurement report (MNE-IO-2024-002) and the procurement administration's page on the system (MNE-GOV-2025-005) are not available in a usable form, so only the interim slides can be used. In the same search, no passage from the Commission's 2025 report mentions the MAPS assessment or a new system. The Commission's 2025 and 2026 recommendations to introduce a red-flag system, and its 2026 statement that automatic detection of irregularities and the link to the justice database are still awaited [16][17][18], concern a function of a working system, a separate issue, and are context only. The Commission's 2026 report also records a rise in the share of centralised procurement, which it does not link to the electronic system, so it is not used here. No source names the system.

**Practical lesson for Moldova (agent synthesis).** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: Moldova is now procuring a new system, with technical specifications received in September 2025 and a call for bids published in December 2025 [19][3]. Montenegro shows that a system introduced in 2021 could be judged within about two years to rest on old technology that limits upgrading, so a new system can itself become the problem Moldova is trying to leave behind. Montenegro's response was an independent diagnostic requested by the procurement policy body, which was then used to secure EU funds for an upgrade; whether the upgrade worked is not known.

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** The diagnostic step: an independent assessment of the electronic system under a recognised method, requested by the national policy body, with a dedicated technical review of the system, and its findings used to direct donor or EU funds. Moldova already has funding from international partners and World Bank review of its procurement documents [20][21], and the same mechanism of insufficient resources applies, since both countries rely on external funds.

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** The response itself: Montenegro upgraded a recently introduced system, while the Commission finds MTender outdated and lacking whole stages of the procurement cycle [22][23] and Moldova has chosen to build a new system. The case gives no evidence on replacing a system, on covering every procedure and stage in law, or on centralised procurement, and none on whether the old-technology limit was resolved. Montenegro's procurement policy unit and the body receiving the EU funds are named only by abbreviation, so the institutional set-up cannot be compared with Moldova's.

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: an independent assessment of the electronic system, requested by the procurement policy body and used to direct EU or donor funds, could work in Moldova, which already has funding from international partners and World Bank review of its procurement documents. The condition is that such a review reaches the technology choice of the new system before it is built, since Montenegro's system was judged to rest on old technology within about two years of its start. The upgrade route itself does not transfer, because Moldova is replacing MTender and the case gives no evidence that Montenegro's upgrade worked.

### CMP-0014: North Macedonia

**Comparator relevance: Low.** North Macedonia's integrated system is described by the Commission as functioning efficiently in every report from 2021 to 2026, and the version launched on 1 April 2025 is an update of a working system; a search of all 4,629 passages in the North Macedonian evidence files found none saying the earlier system was outdated or inadequate. The case matches the state Moldova is aiming for, an obligatory system adapted to the procurement law, rather than Moldova's problem, and the Commission's limited progress finding leaves the content and effect of the update unknown. It does not show an outdated system being replaced.

**Problem addressed.** No source in the project describes the North Macedonian system as outdated or inadequate. The Commission's 2020 recommendation was to upgrade the portal with new modules [24], and North Macedonia's problem with the system is underuse of modules in a working system, not its age. A search of all 4,629 passages in the North Macedonian evidence files for electronic procurement wording combined with outdated, old, replace, new, re-engineer, updated, upgrade, technology or inadequate found only upgrade and update statements, none stating that the system was outdated or functionally inadequate.

**Reform or mechanism used.** Incremental upgrading of one integrated system run by the Public Procurement Bureau (Electronic System for Public Procurement (ESPP); Upgrade of the electronic public procurement system). By North Macedonia's own account in the 2023 screening report, the system covers e-planning, e-publication, e-submission, e-award and e-appeals and its use is obligatory for all contracting authorities and entities at central and local level [25]. The portal was upgraded and adjusted to the new legal framework, with awarded contracts linked to contract notices made available electronically [26], and the Bureau launched an updated version on 1 April 2025 [27].

**How it was implemented.** Modules were added in stages: e-appeals became fully operational on 1 April 2019 by the country's account [28], the e-market for low-value procurement was launched in 2021 by the same account [29], and the Commission records a red-flag function and a COVID-19 marking function [30][31]. The system is in use, and the upgrade to the new legal framework has been carried out [32][26]. The Commission records limited progress with the updated version [33], and a search of all 4,629 North Macedonian evidence passages for electronic procurement wording found none that states what the 2025 version changed or why it was introduced.

**Evidence of results.** The Commission states that the system functions efficiently in 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025 [34][35][36][37][38], and in 2026 that it operates efficiently, covering all transactions [32]. It credits the electronic procurement and e-appeals system with a high level of transparency [39]; on that statement the system is judged to have worked for transparency, with medium confidence that it contributed. These results concern the system as a whole; no source in the project gives a result for the 2025 updated version.

**Limitations of the evidence.** The North Macedonian evidence was gathered for the earlier country study and has not been extended, so nothing after the 2026 Commission report is held. The coverage of the cycle and obligatory use rest on the country's own account in the screening report. The listed coverage does not mention contract management. The 2025 update is described in one sentence, and the Commission's limited progress finding gives no detail. Underuse of the e-market and of e-auctions is a separate issue and is not relied on here.

**Practical lesson for Moldova (agent synthesis).** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: North Macedonia shows the end state Moldova is aiming for, an integrated system obligatory for all contracting authorities, adapted to the procurement law and judged efficient by the Commission year after year. It does not show how to get there from an outdated system. Once Moldova's new system is working, North Macedonia's staged addition of modules and its single bureau managing the system could guide what follows; Moldova's Reform Agenda pilot already starts with essential modules [40].

**What is transferable (agent synthesis).** Adapting the system to the procurement law when the law changes, which answers Moldova's problem of a system that does not allow all tools in the law [22][41]; adding modules in stages after the core works; obligatory use by all contracting authorities; and one body responsible for the system.

**What is not transferable (agent synthesis).** Replacement of an outdated system, which the case does not show. Contract management, which MTender lacks [23], is not among the stages listed for North Macedonia. The weak administrative capacity behind Moldova's limited progress and its reliance on external funding have no counterpart in the North Macedonian evidence: the same search found no passage linking the procurement system to weak capacity or to donor funding.

**Transferability judgement: conditional.** Agent synthesis, not stated by a source: adapting the system to the procurement law, making its use obligatory for all contracting authorities and adding modules in stages under one responsible body could serve Moldova, whose Reform Agenda pilot already starts with essential modules. The condition is that Moldova first has a working new core system, since the North Macedonian case shows the upgrading of a sound system and nothing on replacing an outdated one. Contract management, which MTender lacks, is not among the stages listed for North Macedonia.

## Lessons across the cases (agent synthesis)

Two points recur. First, a new system can itself become the problem. Montenegro's system was under implementation from January 2021 [5], and an independent World Bank assessment, requested by the national procurement policy body, found in 2023 that it was a good start but needed substantial upgrading and that old technology limited further upgrading [6][4][1]. The assessment's findings were then used to secure EU funds for an upgrade [2], but the only stated effect since is the Commission's unmeasured sentence on improved monitoring functions [15]. Moldova received the technical specifications for its new system in September 2025 and published the call for bids in December 2025 [19][3], so its technology choice is being made now. Second, a working system follows the law and grows in stages. North Macedonia's system was adapted to its new procurement law and modules were added over several years, under one responsible body, and its use is obligatory for all contracting authorities [26][25][32]; this is the state Moldova is aiming for, not the route to it. Moldova's own problem is that MTender does not allow all the tools in its law and lacks annual planning and contract management [22][23][41], and contract management is not among the stages listed for North Macedonia either, so neither case gives evidence on it.

## Draft policy options (agent synthesis, internal draft)

These options are internal drafts for discussion. Each rests on a validated unresolved gap (open) and on comparator cases validated at G6b; each transferability judgement is agent synthesis, rated conditional. None is proposed because another country adopted it, and no case shows that any of these steps produced a working system.

**A warning first: a new system can rest on technology that limits it within a few years.**
Montenegro (relevance Medium) shows a system introduced in 2021 and judged within about two years to rest on old technology that limits further upgrading [5][1]. Moldova is replacing MTender because the Commission finds it outdated [22], and the Commission asks it to finalise the new system and make it operational [42]. The comparator evidence advises against treating the award of a development contract, or a pilot of essential modules by December 2027 [40], as evidence that the new system will avoid MTender's problems. It gives no support for any particular technology, design or supplier.

**Option 1: Commission an independent review of the new system's design and technology choice, early enough to change it.**
- *Gap addressed:* designing, building and putting into operation a new system that covers every procedure and stage of the procurement cycle [40][23][42].
- *Mechanism targeted:* weak administrative capacity (MEC-03), in the technical judgement needed to specify and check a large system.
- *Comparator evidence:* Montenegro's procurement policy body asked the World Bank for an assessment under a recognised method with a dedicated e-procurement module and a three-day technical review of the system [6][9], and the findings directed EU funds [2]. Moldova already has World Bank review of its tender documents for the new system, according to a machine-translated government report [21], which is a review of the procurement documents, not of the system's technology. Transferability conditional: the review would need to reach the technology choice before the system is built, whereas Montenegro's came after; and the case does not show that its upgrade worked.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* a review whose findings can still change the contract, and a method recognised by the funders.
- *Main risks:* a review that arrives after the design is fixed; a review used only to unlock funds, with no check that its findings were acted on.
- *Who would need to act:* the body procuring the new system, named in the Government's report as OGPAE [21], with the Centre for Information Technologies in Finance, which owns and operates MTender [43], and the international partners funding the system [20].

**Option 2: Specify and accept the new system against the procurement law and the whole cycle, and plan how it will follow later changes to the law.**
- *Gap addressed:* the procedures, award instruments, annual planning and contract management that MTender lacks, and the monitoring the Public Procurement Agency cannot carry out because delivery, invoices, payments, amendments and performance are not captured digitally [23][41][44].
- *Mechanism targeted:* weak administrative capacity (MEC-03) and insufficient resources (MEC-09), since the system depends on funding from international partners [20].
- *Comparator evidence:* North Macedonia (relevance Low) shows a working system adapted to a new procurement law, extended module by module under one responsible body and obligatory for all contracting authorities, which the Commission describes as functioning efficiently year after year [26][25][34][32]. Transferability conditional: it applies only once Moldova has a working new core system, it shows nothing about replacing an outdated one, and contract management is not among its listed stages.
- *Conditions and preconditions:* a map of every procedure and stage in Moldova's law against the modules planned, including those beyond the essential modules in the Reform Agenda pilot [40]; a budget for adapting the system after donor funding ends, which neither case shows.
- *Main risks:* a pilot of essential modules that leaves planning and contract management for later, keeping the alternative solutions that the Commission says may cause delays [23].
- *Who would need to act:* the Centre for Information Technologies in Finance as owner and operator, with the Public Procurement Agency, whose monitoring depends on the data captured.

No option is offered on how to migrate from MTender to the new system, because neither case gives evidence on replacing a system.
